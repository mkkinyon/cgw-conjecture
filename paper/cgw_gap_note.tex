\title{A gap in the proof of Lemma 3.12 of Cavenagh--Greenhill--Wanless, \\
\emph{The cycle structure of two rows in a random Latin square}}
\author{[author]}
\date{3 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Case 3 of the splitting procedure used to prove Lemma 3.12 of
\cite{CGW08} (cross-switch with respect to $\{\omega(j),\omega(j')\}$,
then backflip with respect to $\{j,j'\}$) does not behave as claimed:
the cross-switch reverses an $\omega$-arc containing $j'$ (or $j$),
after which $\{j,j'\}$ is at $\omega$-distance $2$.  When
$\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$ the backflip therefore produces a square of
the wrong type.  When $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2<\max(\alpha,\beta)$ the type
is right, but the resulting edge is in general not an edge of the
joining multigraph, so the identity $G_S=G_J$ on which the double count
rests fails; already for $n=5$, the sharp case, the splitting multigraph
has vertices of degree $24$ on the $S(\mu,F)$ side where the proof
asserts at most $18$.  The lower bound of Lemma 3.12 survives (and is
in fact elementary); the upper bound $3/2$, hence the upper bound of
Theorem 3.13 and the results of Section 4 that use it (listed below),
is not established by the published argument for any split other than
$(2,2)$.  For $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$ the count can be corrected and
gives the bound with $2$ in place of $3/2$; for $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$
a canonical repair of case 3 exists when $\alpha$ is even and
$\alpha<2\beta$ (or symmetrically), and we do not see one when
$\alpha,\beta$ are both odd.  Exact data for $n\le11$ and sampled
squares up to $n=100$ indicate that the statement is true with much
room to spare.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setting and the procedure}

We use the notation of \cite{CGW08}, Section 3.  $L$ is a Latin square
of order $n$ containing a suitable partial square $F$; $\omega$ is the
permutation of the first $m$ columns with $1\circ\omega(j)=2\circ j$,
whose cycle type $\lambda\in P(m)$ defines $S(\lambda,F)$.  For
columns $j,j'$ let $\delta_{j,j'}$ be the row permutation with
$\delta(i)\circ j=i\circ j'$; $\{j,j'\}$ is an \emph{$A$-pair} if rows
$1,2$ lie in different cycles of $\delta_{j,j'}$ and a \emph{$B$-pair}
otherwise.  The \emph{backflip} at an $A$-pair $\{j,j'\}$ lying on one
$\omega$-cycle of length $\alpha+\beta$ with $\omega^\alpha(j)=j'$
trades the $\delta_{j,j'}$-cycle through row $2$; it splits that
$\omega$-cycle into the two arcs between $j$ and $j'$, of lengths
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ (Lemma 3.5).  The same trade at an $A$-pair
whose columns lie on different $\omega$-cycles, of lengths $\alpha$ and
$\beta$, is the \emph{flip} (Definition 3.2); it joins the two cycles
into one, with $\omega'^{\beta}(j)=j'$ (Lemma 3.3).  The \emph{switch}
at such a pair is the trade of rows $1,2$ along one of the two
$\omega$-cycles (Definition 3.6 selects the cycle containing the
smallest symbol; below we sometimes fix the cycle by another rule,
which is harmless for the counts), and it turns a $B$-pair into an
$A$-pair (Lemma 3.7).  The \emph{cross-switch} at a
$B$-pair $\{j,j'\}$ with $\omega^\alpha(j)=j'$ (Definition 3.9) is a
Latin bitrade $(Q,Q')$ that preserves $S(\lambda,F)$ (Lemma 3.10).

Let $\mu$ be $\lambda$ with one part $\alpha+\beta$ split into
$\alpha,\beta\ge2$.  The splitting procedure (p.~294) considers, for
$L\in S(\lambda,F)$, every pair $\{j,j'\}$ with $\omega^\alpha(j)=j'$
on an $(\alpha+\beta)$-cycle and produces $L'\in S(\mu,F)$ as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[1.] if $\{j,j'\}$ is an $A$-pair, backflip at $\{j,j'\}$;
\item[2.] else if $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ is an $A$-pair, backflip at
$\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$;
\item[3.] otherwise cross-switch with respect to $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$;
``now $\{j,j'\}$ has become an $A$-pair, and we backflip with respect to
$\{j,j'\}$''.
\end{enumerate}
Item 4 asserts that in each case $L'\in S(\mu,F)$.  Together with a
fifth edge (a switch) this gives every $L\in S(\lambda,F)$ degree
exactly $2(\mu_{\alpha+\beta}+1)(\alpha+\beta)$ in a bipartite
multigraph between $S(\lambda,F)$ and $S(\mu,F)$, while the joining
procedure gives every $L\in S(\mu,F)$ degree between
$\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\alpha\beta$ and three times that; the two
multigraphs are asserted to coincide, and the double count is
Lemma~3.12:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L312}
\frac12\;\le\;\frac{|S(\lambda,F)|\,(\mu_{\alpha+\beta}+1)(\alpha+\beta)}{|S(\mu,F)|\,\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\,\alpha\beta}\;\le\;\frac32
\qquad(\alpha\ne\beta).
\end{equation}
(For $\alpha=\beta$ the statement has $2(\mu_{2\alpha}+1)/(\alpha\mu_\alpha(\mu_\alpha-1))$
in place of the fraction; everything below applies verbatim, with the
splits $(\alpha,\alpha)$, $\alpha\ge3$, in the class
$\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$ and $(2,2)$ the only unaffected split.)

\section{The cross-switch reverses an arc}

Write $e_1=1\circ j$, $e_2=1\circ j'$, $e_3=2\circ j$, $e_4=2\circ j'$
and consider the first form of Definition 3.9
($\min(e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4)\in\{e_1,e_4\}$); the second form
($\min\in\{e_2,e_3\}$) is the first form applied to the ordered pair
$(j',j)$, i.e.\ with the roles of $j$ and $j'$ (and of $\alpha$ and
$\beta$) exchanged, so that the cross-switch at an unordered $B$-pair
is well defined.  On rows $1,2$ the bitrade is
\[
Q'\supseteq\{(2,j,e_2),(1,j',e_3)\}\cup
\{(1,\omega^kj,\,2\circ\omega^kj),\ (2,\omega^kj,\,1\circ\omega^kj)\;:\;1\le k<\alpha\},
\]
i.e.\ in every interior column of the $\omega$-arc from $j$ to $j'$ the
entries of rows $1,2$ are swapped, row $2$ receives $e_2=1\circ j'$ at
column $j$, and row $1$ receives $e_3=2\circ j$ at column $j'$.  (This
is the definition; the proof of Lemma 3.10 confirms it: row $1$ keeps
the symbol set $\{1\circ\omega^kj:1\le k\le\alpha\}$ on the cells
$\{\omega^kj:1\le k<\alpha\}\cup\{j'\}$, and ``precisely one column
from the pair $\{\omega^kj,\omega^{k'}j'\}$ has symbols swapped in rows
$1$ and $2$''.)  The remaining cells of $Q$ lie in columns $j,j'$ and
in rows other than $1,2$; they do not affect $\omega$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rev}
Let $L'$ be the cross-switch of $L$ at the $B$-pair $\{j,j'\}$,
$\omega^\alpha(j)=j'$, and let $\omega'=\omega_{L'}$.  Then
$\omega'(j)=\omega^{\alpha-1}(j)$, $\omega'(\omega^kj)=\omega^{k-1}(j)$
for $2\le k<\alpha$, $\omega'(\omega j)=j'$, and $\omega'=\omega$ on
every other column.  In words: the interior of the $\omega$-arc from $j$
to $j'$ is traversed in reverse, and the cycle structure of $\omega$ is
otherwise unchanged.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $1',2'$ for the new rows.  Then $1'\circ j=e_1$,
$1'\circ\omega^kj=1\circ\omega^{k+1}j$ for $1\le k<\alpha$ (since
$2\circ\omega^kj=1\circ\omega^{k+1}j$), $1'\circ j'=1\circ\omega j$;
$2'\circ j=1\circ j'=1\circ\omega^\alpha j$,
$2'\circ\omega^kj=1\circ\omega^kj$ for $1\le k<\alpha$, $2'\circ j'=e_4$.
Now $\omega'(c)$ is the column whose row-$1'$ entry equals $2'\circ c$:
for $c=j$ this is $\omega^{\alpha-1}j$; for $c=\omega^kj$ with
$2\le k<\alpha$ it is $\omega^{k-1}j$; for $c=\omega j$ it is $j'$
(the symbol $1\circ\omega j$ now sits at $(1',j')$); for $c=j'$ it is
$\omega j'$, unchanged; for $c=\omega^{-1}j$, $2'\circ c=2\circ c=e_1=1'\circ j$,
so $\omega'(c)=j$; all other columns are untouched in both rows.
\end{proof}

The example on p.~293 of \cite{CGW08} shows exactly this: there
$\omega=(1\,2\,3\,4\,5\,6\,7)$, $j=2$, $j'=5$, $\alpha=3$, and the
displayed cross-switch has rows $1\,2\,4\,5\,3\,6\,7$ and
$2\,5\,3\,4\,6\,7\,1$, i.e.\ $\omega'=(1\,2\,4\,3\,5\,6\,7)$.

\section{Case 3 lands in the wrong type}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:gap}
Let $\{j,j'\}$ and $\{J,J'\}=\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ both be $B$-pairs
with $\omega^\alpha j=j'$ on an $(\alpha+\beta)$-cycle, and let $L'$ be
the cross-switch of $L$ at $\{J,J'\}$ (either form).  Then $\{j,j'\}$ is
an $A$-pair of $L'$, but in the first form it is at $\omega'$-distance
$2$ (from $j$ to $j'$), and in the second form at $\omega'$-distance $2$
from $j'$ to $j$.  Consequently the backflip at $\{j,j'\}$ splits the
$(\alpha+\beta)$-cycle into parts $2$ and $\alpha+\beta-2$, which is
the type $\mu$ only if $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First form.  The arc from $J=\omega j$ to $J'=\omega^\alpha J$ has
interior $\omega J,\dots,\omega^{\alpha-1}J$, and $\omega^{\alpha-1}J=\omega^\alpha j=j'$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:rev}, $\omega'(j)=\omega'(\omega^{-1}J)=J$ and
$\omega'(J)=\omega^{\alpha-1}J=j'$.  The status of $\{j,j'\}$: column
$j'$ is an interior column of the arc, so rows $1,2$ are swapped there,
while column $j$ is untouched ($j\notin\{J,J'\}$ and $j$ is not in the
arc); hence $\delta_{j,j'}$ changes by a transposition of rows $1,2$
and $\{j,j'\}$ toggles from $B$ to $A$, as claimed in \cite{CGW08}.
The second form reverses the arc from $J'$ to $J$, whose interior
contains $j=\omega^{-1}J$ as its last column, and the same computation
gives $\omega'(J')=j$, i.e.\ $\omega'^2(j')=j$.
\end{proof}

So the asserted membership $L'\in S(\mu,F)$ in item 4 fails in case 3
whenever $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$.

\paragraph{The case $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$.}  Say $\alpha=2\le\beta$.
In the form of the cross-switch that reverses the arc of length $2$
(a single interior column), nothing is reversed and case 3 is as
described.  In the other form the arc of length $\beta$ is reversed;
$j$ or $j'$ is its last interior column, the backflipped square has
parts $2$ and $\beta$, so it does lie in $S(\mu,F)$, and item 4 holds.
But the proof of Lemma 3.12 rests on the identity $G_S=G_J$, and this
fails: the case-3 edge $L$--$L'$ is supposed to be the joining rule-4
edge from $L'$ (flip at $\{j,j'\}$ to $L''$, then cross-switch at
$\{\omega_{L''}j,\omega_{L''}j'\}$), which returns $L$ by Corollary
3.11 only if $\{\omega_{L''}j,\omega_{L''}j'\}=\{J,J'\}$; and when the
long arc has been reversed this is not so (e.g.\ in the second form
$\omega_{L''}(j)=\omega^{\beta-2}J'\ne J$).  Which form occurs is
decided by the position of the smallest corner symbol, not by the
author, so about half of the case-3 edges and their switch edges
(item 5) are missing from $G_J$.  We computed both multigraphs exactly
for $n=5$, $\lambda=(5)$, $\mu=(2,3)$, $F=\emptyset$, over all
$161280$ Latin squares of order $5$ (the procedures select trades by
the smallest symbol, so one cannot normalise the first row): $G_S$ and
$G_J$ each have $1036800$ edges, but $172800$ edges of $G_S$ are not
edges of $G_J$ (exactly half of the case-3 edges and the switch edges
of the same instances; all edges of cases 1 and 2 are in $G_J$), and
the degrees of the $\mu$-squares in $G_S$ are $12$, $18$ and $24$ (for
$11520$, $34560$ and $11520$ squares) against $18$ for every
$\mu$-square in $G_J$.  So the per-vertex assertion ``each vertex of $S(\mu,F)$
has degree $d\le3\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\alpha\beta$'' is false for the
splitting multigraph, though it holds on average over $S(\mu,F)$; the
inequality $Z\le3\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\alpha\beta|S(\mu,F)|$ itself
therefore still holds at $n=5$, with equality (the ratio is exactly
$3/2$ there), but not for the reason given.  For
$(4)\to(2,2)$ at $n=4$ the two multigraphs coincide, as they must: only
arcs of length $2$ occur.  In summary, the published argument for the
upper bound is incomplete for every split with $\max(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$.

\paragraph{Numerical confirmation.}  We implemented Definition 3.9
literally (rows $1,2$ as above; in columns $j,j'$ the rows
$\delta^k(1)$, $1\le k<a$, with $\delta^a(1)=2$, swap their two entries,
exactly as in the definition --- this is what balances the corner
cells) and checked on uniformly sampled
Latin squares (Jacobson--Matthews) that the cross-switch is a Latin
square of the same type in every instance.  Applying case 3 as written:
in $1006$ ordered instances $(j,j')$ with $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$ at
$n=9$ ($300$ sampled squares) and $3098$ at $n=12$ ($300$ squares),
the result had the wrong type in every single instance (e.g.\ $(2,7)$
with $(\alpha,\beta)=(3,4)$ gives $(2,2,5)$ instead of $(2,3,4)$; $(9)$
with $(3,6)$ gives $(2,7)$).

\section{What survives, and what depends on the gap}

Only case 3 is affected.  The joining rules 1--2 are valid and each
rule-2 edge is a case-1 edge or the switch edge of a case-1 instance,
so each $L\in S(\mu,F)$ has degree at least
$\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\alpha\beta$ in the multigraph of valid splitting
edges, in which each $L\in S(\lambda,F)$ has degree at most
$2(\mu_{\alpha+\beta}+1)(\alpha+\beta)$.  Hence the \emph{lower} bound
$\frac12$ in \eqref{eq:L312} stands.  (In fact it is elementary: with
$X$ the set of pairs $(L,\{j,j'\})$, $L\in S(\lambda,F)$,
$\omega^\alpha j=j'$, and $J$ the analogous set of joining pairs, the
flip and the switch give $|J|=2|X_A|$ exactly, so \eqref{eq:L312}
reads $\frac13\le|X_A|/|X|\le1$, and the lower bound $\frac12$ is
$|X|\ge|X_A|$.)  The \emph{upper} bound $\frac32$, equivalently
\[
\Pr\bigl[\{j,j'\}\text{ is an $A$-pair}\bigr]\;\ge\;\tfrac13
\quad\text{for $(L,\{j,j'\})$ uniform on $X$},
\]
is not established by the published argument for $\max(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$;
Proposition~\ref{prop:two} below establishes it with $\tfrac14$ in place of $\tfrac13$
for $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$, so it remains open for $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$.

Within \cite{CGW08} the upper bound of Lemma 3.12 is used for: the
upper bound of Theorem 3.13; the lower bound $(2/3)^{\kappa-1}$ of
Lemma 4.1 and, in Lemma 4.2, the upper bound whenever the path from
$\lambda$ to $\mu$ contains a splitting step and the lower bound
whenever it contains a joining step; the bounds on $\PP_F(E)$ of
Theorem 4.3 (whose proof invokes the equality of the two multigraphs
``as in the proof of Lemma 3.12''); the upper bound
$\PP_F((m))\le2m^{-2/3}$ of Lemma 4.4; both halves of Corollary 4.5
(its lower half uses the $(2/3)^{\kappa-1}$ bound, its upper half the
factor $m^{1/3}$ from Lemma 4.4); and the results that rest on these:
Corollary 4.6, Lemma 4.7, Theorem 4.9, Corollary 4.10 and Theorem
4.11.  (Corollary 4.10, on the number of intercalates, is in any case
superseded by Kwan and Sudakov \cite{KS18}.)  The upper bound
$2^{\kappa-1}$ of Lemma 4.1, the lower bound $\PP_F((m))\ge2m^{-2}$ of
Lemma 4.4, Corollary 4.8, and the upper bounds on short-cycle counts
derived from the joining direction are unaffected.  For instance, from
the joining direction alone one still gets $\E[\ell N_\ell]\le2m/(m-\ell)$
for the number $N_\ell$ of $\ell$-cycles, $\ell<m$ (sum the
inequality $\E[\ell N_\ell\,\beta N_\beta]\le2\E[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}]$,
with $N_\ell N_\beta$ read as $N_\ell(N_\ell-1)$ when $\beta=\ell$, over
$\beta$), and by joining $k$ cycles successively one obtains a
factorial-moment bound of the form
$\E[(\kappa)_k]\le2^{k-1}(4+k^2/\log m)(\log m)^k$ for the number of
cycles $\kappa$, valid for every $k$ (the derivation is in the
Appendix).  With $k=\log m$ this gives $\Pr[\kappa\ge16\log m]=O(m^{-2}\log m)$,
and with $k=\lceil\sqrt m\rceil$ it gives, by Markov's inequality on $(\kappa)_k\ge(8\sqrt m)^k$,
\[
\Pr[\kappa\ge9\sqrt m]\le m\Bigl(\frac{\log m}{4\sqrt m}\Bigr)^{\sqrt m}
=\exp\bigl(-(\tfrac12-o(1))\sqrt m\,\log m\bigr),
\]
which is the second assertion of Theorem 4.9 with a better rate.  The
first assertion, $\Pr[\kappa\ge k]=o((\tfrac34)^k\exp(\pi\sqrt{2m/3}))$,
also follows: it is vacuous unless $k\ge8.9\sqrt m$, and for such $k$
Markov's inequality on $(\kappa)_{k'}$ with $k'=\lceil k/2\rceil$ gives
$\Pr[\kappa\ge k]\le\tfrac12(4+k'^2/\log m)(4\log m/k)^{k'}$, which is
$o((\tfrac34)^k)$.  So the statements of Theorem 4.9 and of the
$\log m$-scale tail survive (by a different proof), while the lower bound on the number of cycles of
Theorem 4.11 does not.  Likewise the upper half of Corollary 4.5
survives in a weaker form.  Write $\CC(\lambda)=|S(\lambda,F)|/\gamma(\lambda)$,
where $\gamma(\lambda)$ is the number of permutations of type $\lambda$,
so that $\CC$ is the completion count per rectangle of type $\lambda$ and
\eqref{eq:L312} reads $\tfrac12\le\CC(\lambda)/\CC(\mu)\le\tfrac32$.
Joining the $\kappa$ parts of a type one pair at a time and using at
each step the surviving inequality
$\CC(\text{finer})\le2\,\CC(\text{coarser})$ (valid also when the two
joined parts are equal) gives $\CC(\lambda)\le2^{\kappa-1}\CC((m))$ for a
type $\lambda$ with $\kappa$ parts (this is the upper half of Lemma~4.1,
listed above as unaffected), hence
\[
\PP_F(\lambda)\le\frac{\gamma(\lambda)\,2^{\kappa-1}\CC((m))}{\gamma((m))\,\CC((m))}
\le\frac{m}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{\kappa}\frac{2}{c_i}
\]
for a type with parts $c_1,\dots,c_\kappa$, using $\gamma(\lambda)\le m!/\prod c_i$,
$\gamma((m))=(m-1)!$ and $\PP_F(\lambda)\propto\gamma(\lambda)\CC(\lambda)$ (in
place of the factor $m^{1/3}$, which came from Lemma 4.4).

\paragraph{A downstream use.}  Gill, Mammoliti and Wanless
\cite{GMW25} use Corollary 4.5 (upper half) and Theorem 4.9 to prove
their Theorem~9 (with probability $1-o(\exp(-\tfrac14n^{1/2}))$ the first
two rows of a random Latin square contain a row cycle of length at least
$n^{1/2}\log^{1-\varepsilon}n$), on which the average-case bound of their
Theorem~1 rests through Theorem~12.  Both inputs are affected by the
gap, but both are replaced by the surviving bounds just stated: with
$\PP_F\le\tfrac m2\prod(2/c_i)$ in place of $m^{1/3}\prod(2/c_i)$ the
estimate in their proof becomes $\exp(-\delta\sqrt n(1-o(1))\log^\varepsilon n)$
times a polynomial factor, and the number of cycles is at most
$9\sqrt n$ with probability $1-\exp(-\Omega(\sqrt n\log n))$; so their
Theorem~9 holds as stated.

Cavenagh and Wanless \cite{CW16} use three results of \cite{CGW08} in
the proof of their Theorem~1.1 (asymptotically the same number of Latin
squares of each parity): the upper half of Corollary~4.5 in their
Lemma~2.1 (no row cycle of length $\ge n-\log n$ in the last two rows,
with probability $1-o(1)$), Theorem~4.9 in their Lemma~2.2, and the
upper bound of Theorem~3.13 in their Lemma~2.3, for the splits of an
even part $z$ into two odd parts $a,z-a$ (so $\min(a,z-a)\ge3$).  The
second use is repaired as above (our Proposition is stated for
$F=\emptyset$; its proof uses only flips and switches at marks in the
free columns and trades along cycles outside $F$, so it applies in
$\Omega(F)$ with $m$ in place of $n$, but we have not written this out;
their Lemma~2.2 states the probability as $1-o(c^m)$, which neither
Theorem~4.9 nor the repair gives, but only $1-o(1)$ is used).
The first is not repaired by the weaker surviving constant: with $n/2$ in
place of $n^{1/3}$ the estimate in Lemma~2.1 becomes
$O(2^{(\log n)/2}\log n)$, and already its $\lambda=(n)$ term is $1$; what
Lemma~2.1 needs is that the cycle through column~$1$ has length at
most $n-\omega(1)$ with probability $1-o(1)$, which includes
$\PP_F((n))=o(1)$, discussed below.  The third is the unproved direction
at exactly the splits where case~3 fails, outside the range of
Proposition~\ref{prop:two}; what their Lemma~2.3 actually needs is
weaker than $\tfrac32$ --- any constant, or even the averaged statement
$\sum_{\mu\in M}\gamma(\mu)|S(\mu,F)|/(\gamma(\lambda)|S(\lambda,F)|)\to\infty$
over the odd splits $M$ of a large even part --- but we do not have
that either.  So, as far as we can see, Theorem~1.1 of \cite{CW16}
currently depends on the gap at two points.  The statement is expected
to be true independently of the gap: Kwan, Petrova and Sawhney
\cite{KPS25} have since proved Cameron's conjecture on parities (their
Theorem~1.2, and the trivariate Theorem~1.3), of which Theorem~1.1 of
\cite{CW16} is a special case (Theorem~1.3(4), as they note); their
written proof of part~(3), hence of~(4), uses \cite{CW16} as a black
box, so formally the dependence on the gap runs through it, but
their Remark~6.6 sketches an independent re-proof of the
Cavenagh--Wanless theorem by their own method (switching a $2\times3$
and a $3\times2$ subrectangle), which we have not checked.  Independently
of this, their large deviation principle, Theorem~1.3(5), is deduced
from their Theorem~6.4 and Lemma~6.5(ii) without any use of \cite{CW16}
or of \cite{CGW08}, and it implies the law of large numbers for the
number of odd rows (their Theorem~1.3(1)), hence, as they remark, by row
exchangeability
$\Pr[\text{rows }1,2\text{ have the same parity}]=\tfrac12+o(1)$; this
supersedes Theorem~4.3 of \cite{CGW08} (affected by the gap, as listed
above).  (Their written deduction of the law of large numbers goes
through part~(4), hence through \cite{CW16}; the route through
part~(5) avoids it.)

More generally, the surviving direction bounds fine types from above
only: it gives no upper bound on the probability of a coarse type.  In
particular we know of no proof, from the surviving material or from our
own identities, that the first two rows of a random Latin square form a
single $n$-cycle with probability $o(1)$ (the statement
$\PP_F((m))\le2m^{-2/3}$ of Lemma~4.4).  What is available is a constant:
by row-pair symmetry and the intercalate count of \cite{KS18},
$\E N_2\ge\tfrac12-o(1)$; the $\ell=\beta=2$ case of the surviving
inequality gives $\E[N_2(N_2-1)]\le2\E N_4\le m/(m-4)$; so by the second
moment method $\Pr[N_2\ge1]\ge(\E N_2)^2/\E[N_2^2]\ge\tfrac16-o(1)$ and
$\PP_F((m))\le\tfrac56+o(1)$.  Proposition~\ref{prop:two} does not improve
this: it gives $\PP_F((m))\le4\PP_F((2,m-2))$, the surviving inequality
gives $\PP_F((2,m-2))\le\tfrac m{m-2}\PP_F((m))$, and iterating over
$k$ two-cycles loses a factor of order $4^kk!$.  (We went through the
titles of the papers citing \cite{CGW08} that are indexed by MathSciNet
or listed by Google Scholar and read those whose titles suggested a
use of the affected results: besides \cite{GMW25,CW16,KPS25}, these were
Kwan--Sah--Sawhney--Simkin, Allsop--Wanless, Divoux--Kelly--Kennedy--Sidhu
and Allsop--Morris \cite{AM26}, which cite \cite{CGW08} for the
intercalate estimates, superseded by \cite{KS18}, or, in the last case,
for the reversibility and effect lemmas for cycle and cross switches in
\S3 of \cite{CGW08}, which are unaffected.  We have not read the
remaining citing papers, whose titles concern autotopisms, transversals,
bitrades and loops.)

\section{A partial repair}

A cross-switch at a $B$-pair $\{J,J'\}$ at any $\omega$-distance
$a\ge2$ is a type-preserving trade (the proof of Lemma 3.10 does not
use $a=\alpha$), and by Lemma~\ref{lem:rev} it reverses the arc from
$J$ to $J'$.  If $\{J,J'\}$ is disjoint from $\{j,j'\}$, it toggles the
status of $\{j,j'\}$ iff exactly one of $j,j'$ is an interior column of
that arc, say at position $i$ ($1\le i<a$), and then the
$\omega$-distance from $j$ to $j'$ changes by $\pm(a-2i)$ (sign
according to which of $j,j'$ is interior).  Thus, among cross-switches
at pairs disjoint from $\{j,j'\}$, the status-toggling moves that
preserve the distance are precisely those at $B$-pairs
\emph{symmetric about $j$ or about $j'$} ($a=2i$).

Suppose $\alpha$ is even and $\alpha<2\beta$, and let
$R=\{\omega^{\alpha/2}j,\ \omega^{3\alpha/2}j\}$: the pair at distance
$\alpha$ symmetric about $j'$, whose arc avoids $j$.  If $R$ is a
$B$-pair, cross-switch at $R$: $\{j,j'\}$ becomes an $A$-pair whose
two $\omega'$-arcs still have lengths $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (in the
form that reverses the arc of $R$ through $j'$ the distance from $j$
to $j'$ stays $\alpha$; in the other form it becomes $\beta$), and the
backflip at $\{j,j'\}$ lies in $S(\mu,F)$.
If $R$ is an $A$-pair, backflip at $R$ itself, which is a pair at
distance $\alpha$.  Either way case 3 acquires a valid edge
(verified on $3928$ sampled instances at $n=10,13$: type $\mu$ in all of
them).  The same works with the roles of $\alpha,\beta$ exchanged when
$\beta$ is even and $\beta<2\alpha$.  The multiplicities on the
$S(\mu,F)$ side change (an $A$-pair's backflip may now serve two
splitting pairs), so the constant $3/2$ would have to be recomputed.

When $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are both odd, every distance-preserving
toggle has even arc length, so no symmetric pair is at distance $\alpha$
or $\beta$; if all symmetric pairs about $j$ and $j'$ happen to be
$A$-pairs, no single cross-switch repairs $\{j,j'\}$, and two-step
repairs pass through a third type, which breaks the two-sided count.
We do not see a canonical repair in this case (nor when
$\alpha\ge2\beta$ with $\beta$ odd).  Repairs that use other rows do
exist --- for a $B$-pair $\{j,j'\}$ with column cycle $Z\ni1,2$, trade
rows $z,w$ lying on different arcs of $Z$ between $1$ and $2$ along
the row cycle of $\{z,w\}$ through $j$ (if $j,j'$ lie in different row
cycles of $\{z,w\}$; this separates $1$ from $2$ and keeps the type),
or cross-switch rows $z,w$ at $\{j,j'\}$ (if they lie in one; this
changes the type to $\mu$) --- but they are indexed by a free pair of
rows and so have unbounded multiplicity on the $S(\mu,F)$ side, which
defeats the double count.

\section{The case $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$: the bound holds with constant $2$}

For $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$ the published count can be corrected at the
price of a weaker constant.  Say $\alpha=2\le\beta$, $m=\beta+2$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:two}
If $\alpha=2$ (or $\beta=2$), then
\[
\frac{|S(\lambda,F)|\,(\mu_{m}+1)\,m}{|S(\mu,F)|\,\mu_2\mu_\beta\,2\beta}\;\le\;2
\qquad(\beta\ne2),
\]
and for $\alpha=\beta=2$ the quantity of Lemma 3.12 is $\le2$ as well.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every splitting instance $(L,\{j,j'\})$ yields two squares in
$S(\mu,F)$: in cases 1 and 2 this is item 4, and in case 3 the
cross-switch either reverses nothing (the arc of length $2$) or
reverses the arc of length $\beta$, and in both forms the backflip at
$\{j,j'\}$ produces parts $2$ and $\beta$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:gap}).
So $Z=2(\mu_m+1)m\,|S(\lambda,F)|$ counts valid edges.  It remains to
show that every $M\in S(\mu,F)$ receives at most $4$ edges per joining
pair, i.e.\ per pair $\{q,q'\}$ with $q$ in a $2$-cycle and $q'$ in a
$\beta$-cycle of $\omega_M$.  Attribute each edge $(L,M)$ to a joining
pair of $M$ as follows: the direct edge of case $c\in\{1,2,3\}$ to the
pair at which the backflip was made ($\{j,j'\}$ in cases 1, 3;
$\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ in case 2), which is an $A$-pair of $M$ (the
backflipped pair is an $A$-pair afterwards); the switch edge of cases 1
and 3 to the switched pair $\{j,j'\}$, which is a $B$-pair of $M$
(Lemma 3.7: it was an $A$-pair before the switch); and the switch edge
of case 2 to $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$, which is a $B$-pair of $M$ (it
was the backflipped $A$-pair of $L'$, and the switch at $\{j,j'\}$
trades the row cycle through $j$ or through $j'$, which contains
exactly one of $\omega j,\omega j'$, so the status toggles by the
argument in the proof of Lemma 3.7).  Now fix $M$ and a joining pair
$Q$ of $M$ and count the edges attributed to $Q$.

If $Q$ is an $A$-pair of $M$, only direct edges arrive, and
$L_1:=\mathrm{flip}_Q(M)$ is determined.  Case 1 contributes
$(L_1,Q)$; case 2 contributes $(L_1,\{\omega_{L_1}^{-1}q,\omega_{L_1}^{-1}q'\})$;
in case 3, $L$ is a cross-switch of $L_1$ at a $B$-pair $\{J,J'\}$ with
$Q=\{j,j'\}$ at $\omega_L$-distance $2$, and $\{J,J'\}$ is determined by
$L_1$ and $Q$: let $(a,b)$ be the ordering of $Q$ with
$\omega_{L_1}^2(a)=b$ (unique since $\beta\ge3$; for $\beta=2$ both
orderings give the same candidate below and there is no long arc, so
case 3 then contributes at most $1$); if nothing was
reversed, $\{J,J'\}=\{\omega_{L_1}a,\omega_{L_1}b\}$; if the long arc
was reversed, the computation of Proposition~\ref{prop:gap} (with $j'$
the column of $Q$ at distance $2$ after $j$ in $L$) gives
$\omega_{L_1}(j')=J'$ and $\omega_{L_1}^{-1}(j')=J$, i.e.\ $(a,b)=(j',j)$
and $\{J,J'\}=\{\omega_{L_1}^{-1}a,\omega_{L_1}a\}$, the pair symmetric
about $a$.  So case 3 contributes at most $2$ edges.  Total $\le4$.

If $Q$ is a $B$-pair of $M$, only switch edges arrive.  For cases 1 and
3 the switch was made at $Q$ itself, $M'=\mathrm{sw}_Q(M)$ is
determined (the switch is an involution: it preserves the symbol sets
of the two row cycles, so the smallest-symbol rule re-selects the same
cycle), and the direct edge into $M'$ at the $A$-pair $Q$ is the case-1 edge
or one of the two case-3 edges listed above: $\le3$ edges.  For case 2, $Q=\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$
and the switch was made at $\{j,j'\}$; the switch inverted the row
cycle of $M'$ through $j$ or through $j'$ (whichever carries the
smaller symbol, which is read off from $M$ since the symbol sets are
preserved), so $j=\omega_M(\omega j)$ if the cycle through $j$ was
inverted and $j=\omega_M^{-1}(\omega j)$ otherwise, and similarly
$j'$; then $M'=\mathrm{sw}_{\{j,j'\}}(M)$ and
$L=\mathrm{flip}_Q(M')$ are determined: $1$ edge.  Total $\le4$.

Hence $Z\le4\,\mu_2\mu_\beta\,2\beta\,|S(\mu,F)|$ (resp.\
$4\binom{\mu_2}{2}4|S(\mu,F)|$ for $\alpha=\beta=2$), and the result
follows as in the proof of Lemma 3.12.
\end{proof}

We verified the recipe by computer: on sampled squares at $n=7,10,12$
every direct and every switch edge of every splitting instance with
$\alpha=2$ is recovered from its target and attributed pair by the
inverse recipes above, with at most $4$ candidates per pair
(\texttt{s13\_cgw\_min2.py}); and in the exact $n=5$ computation of
Section~3 the multiplicities per (target, attributed pair) are exactly
$2$, $3$ or $4$, direct edges always arrive at $A$-pairs and switch
edges at $B$-pairs.  The constant $2$ is what this count gives at
$n=5$ ($24=4\cdot6$ is attained), where the truth is $3/2$.

In the language of Section~4, Proposition~\ref{prop:two} says that for
a marked pair at distance $2$ (or $m-2$) on an $m$-cycle,
$\Pr_X[A]\ge\tfrac14$: a lower bound on a different-cycles event, which
the surviving direction alone could not give.  The reason it works for
$\alpha=2$ and not for $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$ is not multiplicity but
type: for $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$ the wrong-form case-3 edges land in
$S(\mu',F)$ with $\mu'$ the $(2,m-2)$ split, so the same count gives
only $2(|X|-|X_{3}|)\le3|J|$, where $X_3$ is the set of instances in
case 3 ($\{j,j'\}$ and $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ both $B$-pairs), i.e.\
$\Pr_X[A]\ge\tfrac13(1-\Pr_X[\text{case }3])$, which is empty without a
lower bound on $\Pr_X[A\text{ at }\{j,j'\}\text{ or at }\{\omega j,\omega j'\}]$.

The statement of Lemma 3.12 is almost certainly true, and far from
sharp for $n\ge6$.  By the identity $|J|=2|X_A|$ the quantity in

\section{The factorial-moment bound for the number of cycles}

We derive, from the joining direction alone, the bound used in \S4.
Take $F=\emptyset$ (a uniform Latin square $L$ of order $n$, so $m=n$;
the argument uses only flips, switches and the identity $|J|=2|X_A|$
of \S4, so it applies in $\Omega(F)$ with $m$ in place of $n$, but we
state it for $F=\emptyset$).  Let $N_\ell$ be the number of
$\ell$-cycles of $\omega$ and $\kappa=\sum_\ell N_\ell$.  The two facts
used are those behind $|J|=2|X_A|$: for columns $j_1,j_2$ in different
$\omega$-cycles $C_1,C_2$, the flip at $\{j_1,j_2\}$ (the trade of the
$\delta_{j_1,j_2}$-cycle through row~$2$) is defined when $\{j_1,j_2\}$
is an $A$-pair and joins $C_1,C_2$ into one cycle of length
$|C_1|+|C_2|$ with $\omega'^{|C_2|}(j_1)=j_2$ (Lemma 3.3), the other
cycles being unchanged; and when $\{j_1,j_2\}$ is a $B$-pair, the switch
along $C_2$ (we always switch the cycle of the second column, rather
than the one selected by Definition 3.6; any fixed rule serves)
inverts $C_2$ in place, leaves every other cycle unchanged, and makes
$\{j_1,j_2\}$ an $A$-pair (Lemma 3.7).  The backflip at $\{j_1,j_2\}$ in the joined
square returns the square before the flip, so joining is at most
two-to-one onto its image (the original square or its switch).

\emph{(i) $\E[\ell N_\ell]\le2n/(n-\ell)$ for $\ell<n$.}  For
$\beta\ne\ell$, count instances $(L,\{j,j'\})$ with $j$ on an
$\ell$-cycle and $j'$ on a $\beta$-cycle: $\ell N_\ell\,\beta N_\beta$ per
square; joining maps them at most two-to-one into the instances
$(L',\{j,j'\})$ with $\omega'^{\beta}(j)=j'$ on an $(\ell+\beta)$-cycle,
of which there are $(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}$ per square (this is
$|J|\le2|X|$ for the split $(\ell,\beta)$).  So
$\E[\ell N_\ell\,\beta N_\beta]\le2\E[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}]$, and
for $\beta=\ell$ the same count with the $\ell^2\binom{N_\ell}2$
instances on unordered pairs of distinct $\ell$-cycles and the
$\ell N_{2\ell}$ unordered targets gives
$\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]\le4\ell\E[N_{2\ell}]$.  Summing over
$\beta\ne\ell$, using $\sum_\beta\beta N_\beta=n$, and adding the
$\beta=\ell$ term,
$\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]+\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]\le2\E\bigl[\sum_{m'\ge\ell+2}m'N_{m'}\bigr]\le2n$,
and the left side is $n\E[\ell N_\ell]-\ell\E[\ell N_\ell]$.

\emph{(ii) $\E[(\kappa)_k]\le2^{k-1}\bigl(4+k^2/\log n\bigr)(\log n)^k$ for every $k\ge1$.}
Count instances $(L,(C_1,j_1),\dots,(C_k,j_k))$ with $C_1,\dots,C_k$
pairwise distinct cycles of lengths $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k$ and
$j_i\in C_i$: there are $\ell_1\cdots\ell_k$ per ordered $k$-tuple of
distinct cycles with these lengths.  Join $C_1$ and $C_2$ at
$\{j_1,j_2\}$ (switching $C_2$ first if the pair is a $B$-pair), then
join the result with $C_3$ at $\{j_1,j_3\}$, and so on, always switching
the cycle of the new column when needed, so that the cycle containing
$j_1$ is never inverted; after $k-1$ steps all $j_i$ lie on one cycle of
length $m'=\sum\ell_i$ at positions relative to $j_1$ determined by
$(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)$, every cycle other than $C_1,\dots,C_k$ is
unchanged, and the composite map is at most $2^{k-1}$-to-one.  Hence the
number of instances with lengths $(\ell_i)$ is at most
$2^{k-1}\sum_{L'}m'N_{m'}(L')$, i.e.
$\E[\ell_1\cdots\ell_k\,T(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)]\le2^{k-1}\E[m'N_{m'}]$
with $T$ the number of ordered $k$-tuples of distinct cycles with these
lengths.  Summing over $(\ell_i)$, since $\sum_{(\ell_i)}T=(\kappa)_k$,
\[
\E[(\kappa)_k]\le2^{k-1}\sum_{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k\ge2}
\frac{\E[m'N_{m'}]}{\ell_1\cdots\ell_k}\Big|_{m'=\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_k}.
\]
Split at $m'\le n/2$, where $\E[m'N_{m'}]\le4$ by (i) and
$\sum_{m'\le n/2}\sum_{\sum\ell_i=m'}\prod\ell_i^{-1}\le(\sum_{\ell\le n/2}\ell^{-1})^k\le(\log n)^k$,
and $m'>n/2$, where some $\ell_i\ge m'/k$, so
$\sum_{\sum\ell_i=m'}\prod\ell_i^{-1}\le k\cdot(k/m')(\log n)^{k-1}$ and
$\sum_{m'>n/2}\E[m'N_{m'}]\,k^2(\log n)^{k-1}/m'\le k^2(\log n)^{k-1}$,
because at most one cycle is longer than $n/2$.

\emph{(iii) Tails.}  Markov's inequality on $(\kappa)_k$: with
$k=\lceil\log n\rceil$ and $K=A\log n$, on $\{\kappa\ge K\}$ one has
$(\kappa)_k\ge((A-1)\log n)^k$, so
$\Pr[\kappa\ge A\log n]\le(7+\log n)(2/(A-1))^{\log n}$, which is
$O(n^{-2}\log n)$ for $A\ge16$; with $k=\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$ and
$K=9\sqrt n$, $(\kappa)_k\ge(8\sqrt n)^k$ on $\{\kappa\ge K\}$ and
$4+k^2/\log n\le2n/\log n$ for large $n$, so
$\Pr[\kappa\ge9\sqrt n]\le2^{k-1}(2n/\log n)(\log n)^k/(8\sqrt n)^k
\le n\,(\log n/(4\sqrt n))^{\sqrt n}$ for large $n$, as used in \S4.
