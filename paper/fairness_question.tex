\section*{Setting}

Let $L$ be a Latin square of order $n$ on the symbol set $[n]$, with
rows and columns indexed by $[n]$.  Two rows, $1$ and $2$, and two
columns, $j\ne j'$, are distinguished throughout.

\paragraph{The frame.}  Let $\pi=\pi_L$ be the permutation of the
rows defined by
\[
L\bigl(\pi(r),\,j'\bigr)=L(r,j)\qquad(r\in[n]),
\]
i.e.\ $\pi(r)$ is the row in which column $j'$ holds the symbol that
column $j$ holds in row $r$; we call $\pi$ the \emph{frame} of $L$.
It is a derangement, since $\pi(r)=r$ would force
$L(r,j)=L(r,j')$.  The \emph{bit} of $L$ is
\[
\beta(L)=\mathbf 1\{\text{rows $1$ and $2$ lie in a common cycle of }\pi_L\},
\]
and we write $C_1,C_2$ for the cycles of $\pi_L$ through rows $1$ and
$2$ (so $\beta=1$ iff $C_1=C_2$).

\paragraph{Row-pair permutations.}  For rows $x\ne y$ let
$\rho_{x,y}$ be the permutation of the columns defined by
$L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$.  On the columns of any cycle $Q$ of
$\rho_{x,y}$ the two rows carry the same set of symbols.

\paragraph{Two families of moves.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
\item[(\textsc{trade})] For rows $x\ne y$ with $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$,
let $Q$ be the cycle of $\rho_{x,y}$ containing $j'$, and exchange the
entries of rows $x$ and $y$ in the columns of $Q$.
\item[(\textsc{turn})] For a cycle $c$ of $\pi_L$ containing neither
row $1$ nor row $2$, exchange the entries of columns $j$ and $j'$ in
the rows of $c$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lemma}
Both moves produce Latin squares, are involutions, and leave rows $1$
and $2$ unchanged.  A turn replaces $\pi$ by $\pi^{-1}$ on the rows of
$c$ and leaves it unchanged elsewhere; the cycle partition is
unchanged.  A trade at $(x,y)$ replaces $\pi$ by
$(x\,y)\circ\pi$ if $j\notin Q$ (so the cycles of $x$ and $y$ are
merged if distinct and split if equal), and by
$(x\,y)\circ\pi\circ(x\,y)$ if $j\in Q$ (the cycle partition is
unchanged up to exchanging the labels $x,y$).  In particular a trade
can change $\beta$ only when $j\notin Q$; call such a pair $(x,y)$
\emph{flippable}.  If $\pi(x)=y$ or $\pi(y)=x$ then $(x,y)$ is not
flippable.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are direct verifications.  Both rows carry the same
symbols on $Q$, and both columns carry the same symbols on $c$, which
gives the Latin property; the traded cycle is again the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle of $j'$ in the new square, and the turned cycle is
again a cycle of the new frame, which gives the involution property.
The frame formulas follow from the definition of $\pi$, since only the
entries of rows $x,y$ in columns $j,j'$ matter.  For the last claim:
with $b'=L(y,j')$ and $q^*$ the column in which row $x$ holds $b'$,
one has $\rho_{x,y}(q^*)=j'$, so $Q$ is the $\rho_{x,y}$-cycle of
$q^*$.  If $\pi(x)=y$ then $b'=L(x,j)$, so $q^*=j\in Q$; if
$\pi(y)=x$ then $L(x,j')=L(y,j)$, so $\rho_{x,y}(j')=j\in Q$.

\section*{Components and the question}

Fix a $2\times n$ Latin rectangle $R$ (the first two rows) and
columns $j\ne j'$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonadj}
R(1,j)\ne R(2,j')\qquad\text{and}\qquad R(1,j')\ne R(2,j),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $\pi(1)\ne2$ and $\pi(2)\ne1$ for every extension of $R$.
(Without \eqref{eq:nonadj} the question below is void: if
$R(1,j)=R(2,j')$ then $\pi(1)=2$, hence $\beta=1$, for every extension
of $R$.)  Let $S(R)$ be the set of Latin squares whose first two rows
are $R$.  Each move is an involution of $S(R)$; let $\mathcal K(R)$
be the set of orbits (``components'') of the group they generate, and
for a component $K$ let
\[
p(K)=\frac{|\{L\in K:\beta(L)=1\}|}{|K|}.
\]
We call the bit \emph{fair} on $K$ if $p(K)$ is close to $\tfrac12$.

\begin{question}[component fairness]
Is it true that, uniformly over $R$ and over column pairs $(j,j')$
satisfying \eqref{eq:nonadj},
\[
\frac1{|S(R)|}\sum_{K\in\mathcal K(R)}|K|\,\bigl(2p(K)-1\bigr)^2
\;\longrightarrow\;0\qquad(n\to\infty)?
\]
Equivalently: for $L$ uniform in $S(R)$, the conditional probability
that $\beta(L)=1$ given the component of $L$ is $\tfrac12+o(1)$ in
$L^2$.  A quantitative version asks for the left side to be
$o(1/\log^2n)$.  A weaker, averaged version that is still useful is
stated in Remark~\ref{rem:weaker}(i).
\end{question}

\begin{remark}[why the question is natural]\label{rem:natural}
Consider the Markov chain on $S(R)$ which, with probability
$\tfrac12$, picks a uniform pair $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ and trades, and
otherwise picks a uniform row $x\notin\{1,2\}$ and turns its
$\pi$-cycle if that cycle avoids rows $1,2$ (holding if not).  Each
move is an involution chosen with state-independent probability, so
the kernel is symmetric and the uniform measure on each component is
stationary.  Every state has a hold: by \eqref{eq:nonadj} the cycle
$C_1$ is not $(1\,2)$, so it contains a row $x\notin\{1,2\}$ whose
turn is rejected.  Hence the chain is aperiodic on each component, and
for the stationary chain started from the uniform measure on $S(R)$
the left side of the Question equals
$\lim_{t\to\infty}\E[\beta'(L_0)\beta'(L_t)]$ with $\beta'=2\beta-1$,
the long-time autocorrelation of the bit.

Along the chain the frame $\pi$ performs the walk of left
multiplication by transpositions $(x\,y)$, $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$,
\emph{thinned} to the flippable pairs: the turns reverse a cycle and
the non-flippable trades conjugate by $(x\,y)$, and neither changes
$\beta$.  The unthinned walk preserves $a=\pi^{-1}(1)$ and
$b=\pi^{-1}(2)$.  Contracting the arcs $a\to1\to\pi(1)$ and
$b\to2\to\pi(2)$ identifies the fibre
$\{\pi:\pi^{-1}(1)=a,\ \pi^{-1}(2)=b\}$ with the permutations of
$[n]\setminus\{1,2\}$ having no fixed point outside $\{a,b\}$ (a fixed
point at $a$ or $b$ is the $2$-cycle $(1\,a)$ or $(2\,b)$ of $\pi$),
and the walk with the walk by uniformly random transpositions of
$[n]\setminus\{1,2\}$ in which the moves that would create a fixed
point --- exactly the $\pi$-adjacent pairs --- are rejected.  Rows
$1,2$ share a cycle of $\pi$ iff $a,b$ share a cycle of the contracted
permutation, which is the case for exactly half of each fibre: left
multiplication by $(a\,b)$ is an involution of the fibre that
toggles it.  So if every non-adjacent pair were flippable the answer
would be yes, with the left side $o(1)$ after $O(n\log n)$ steps if,
as one expects by comparison with the unrestricted walk
(Diaconis--Shahshahani), the rejections do not slow the walk down.
The content of the Question is that the thinning by flippability does
not destroy this.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what is known numerically]\label{rem:numerics}
The chain of Remark~\ref{rem:natural}, modified to reject
non-flippable trades (its components are finer than those above, so
the evidence transfers), was run on uniformly random Latin squares of
order $n=30,50,100$, with $(j,j')$ at distance at least $2$ in both
directions inside a cycle of the column permutation $\sigma$ of $R$
defined by $R(2,\sigma(q))=R(1,q)$.  A uniformly random pair
$x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ that is not $\pi$-adjacent (i.e.\ $\pi(x)\ne y$,
$\pi(y)\ne x$) is flippable with probability $0.500\pm0.001$, the
same whether $x,y$ lie in one cycle of $\pi$ or in two; the
$\pi$-adjacent pairs, which are never flippable, are a fraction
$\approx2/n$ of all pairs.  The autocorrelation of the bit, as a
function of the number of trades performed, decays at least as fast
as that of the unthinned walk and reaches the noise floor
($\approx0.01$) within $64$ trades at each $n$.  Column pairs with
$j,j'$ in different cycles of $\sigma$ were not tested at these
sizes.

The components have been enumerated exactly for $n\le7$ (seven
instances at $n=7$, $|S(R)|\approx6.6\cdot10^6$ each, all cycle types
of $\sigma$).  At $n=7$ there is always one component containing
$99.1$--$99.7\%$ of $S(R)$, on which $p(K)$ agrees with
$\Pr[\beta=1\mid R]$ to four decimals ($0.493$--$0.502$ for
within-cycle marks, exactly $\tfrac12$ for cross-cycle marks); the
remaining squares lie in hundreds of components of size $120$--$960$
and are the squares in which all, or all but one or two, row pairs
$\rho_{x,y}$ are single $7$-cycles (isotopes of $\mathbb Z_7$ and other
row-Hamiltonian squares), so that every trade swaps two whole rows and
nothing is flippable.  These contribute $0.003$--$0.008$, essentially
the whole left side.  ($n=6$ is degenerate: the moves preserve the row
parity of $L$ there, because all free cycles are $2$-cycles.)  So the
component structure is expected to be trivial for large $n$ --- one
component up to a super-polynomially small set of locked squares ---
and the Question with $R$ fixed is then the quenched statement
$\Pr[\beta=1\mid R]=\tfrac12+o(1)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[weaker forms that are still useful]\label{rem:weaker}
(i) The application in Remark~\ref{rem:provenance} needs only an
averaged version: for every cycle type $\lambda$ of $\sigma$, with $L$
uniform among the Latin squares of order $n$ whose column permutation
$\sigma$ has cycle type $\lambda$, $R$ its first two rows, and
$(j,j')=(j,\sigma^\alpha(j))$ with $j$ in an $m$-cycle of $\sigma$
and $\alpha,m-\alpha\ge2$ prescribed, the expectation of the left
side of the Question is $o(1/\log^2n)$.  (ii) A natural route is to
show that, for all but an $o(1/\log^2n)$-fraction of $L\in S(R)$, a
positive proportion of the bit-changing pairs are flippable --- when
$\beta=1$ these are the pairs $x,y\in C_1$ such that rows $1$ and $2$
lie on different arcs of the split of $C_1$ into
$(x,\pi(x),\dots,\pi^{-1}(y))$ and $(y,\pi(y),\dots,\pi^{-1}(x))$;
when $\beta=0$ they are the pairs $x\in C_1$, $y\in C_2$ --- and to
combine this with a mixing estimate for the thinned walk.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[provenance]\label{rem:provenance}
The Question arose in a programme on the Cavenagh--Greenhill--Wanless
conjecture on the cycle type of the second row of a random Latin
square.  Let $\CC_n(\nu)$ be the number of Latin squares of order $n$
whose column permutation $\sigma$ (defined in
Remark~\ref{rem:numerics}) has cycle type $\nu$, let $\lambda$ contain
a part $m=\alpha+\alpha'$ with $\alpha,\alpha'\ge2$, and let $\mu$ be
$\lambda$ with that part split into $\alpha,\alpha'$.  For $L$
uniform among the squares of type $\lambda$ and $(j,j')$ a uniform
pair $\{j,\sigma^\alpha(j)\}$ with $j$ in an $m$-cycle of $\sigma$,
the marked-pair identity
\[
2\,\frac{\CC_n(\lambda)}{\CC_n(\mu)}-1=\frac{\Pr[\beta=1]}{\Pr[\beta=0]}
\]
reduces the conjecture to $\Pr[\beta=1]=\tfrac12+o(1/\log n)$ over
such instances, and a positive answer to the quantitative Question,
averaged as in Remark~\ref{rem:weaker}(i), gives exactly that.  The
question itself makes no reference to this.
\end{remark}
