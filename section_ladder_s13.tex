\section{The ladder identity: the bias is carried by the squares with
no flippable ladder pair}\label{sec:ladder}

Session 13, after the component enumeration of \S\ref{sec:s13comp7}.
This section proves an exact identity that replaces the whole
chain \S\ref{sec:spectralexact}--\S\ref{sec:s13comp7} (pool, master
formula, annealed bias, transfer, trade chain) by a single
combinatorial statement.  Notation as in \S\ref{sec:s13trapped}:
$(L,P)\in X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$, $P=\{j,j'\}$, frame
$\pi=\pi_P$ ($L(\pi(r),j')=L(r,j)$), $C_r$ the $\pi$-cycle of row $r$,
$\rho_{x,y}$ the column permutation of rows $x,y$
($L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$), $(x,y)$ \emph{flippable} iff $j,j'$ lie
in different cycles of $\rho_{x,y}$, and the \emph{$j'$-trade} at
$(x,y)$ exchanges rows $x,y$ on the $\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through $j'$,
which replaces $\pi$ by $(x\,y)\circ\pi$ if $(x,y)$ is flippable and by
$(x\,y)\circ\pi\circ(x\,y)$ otherwise.  $B=\{1\sim2\}$ (rows $1,2$ in a
common frame cycle), $A$ its complement, $\mathbf 1_B$ the bit; for $B$-instances $d_{12}$ and
$d_{21}$ are the numbers of $\pi$-steps from $1$ to $2$ and from $2$ to
$1$.

\begin{definition}[ladder]\label{def:ladder}
Put $K_0=\min(|C_1|,|C_2|)$ on $A$ and $K_0=\min(d_{12},d_{21})$ on
$B$, and for $1\le k<K_0$ let
\[
x_k=\pi^{-k}(1),\qquad y_k=\pi^{-k}(2),\qquad
T_k=\text{the $j'$-trade at }(x_k,y_k).
\]
The pairs $\{x_k,y_k\}$ are the \emph{ladder} of $(L,P)$, and
$\mathrm{Flip}(L,P)=\{k<K_0:(x_k,y_k)\text{ flippable}\}$.
\end{definition}

The rows $x_1,\dots,x_{K_0-1}$ are distinct rows of $C_1\setminus\{1\}$
(on $A$) or of the arc from $2$ to $1$ (on $B$), the $y_k$ likewise in
$C_2\setminus\{2\}$ or the arc from $1$ to $2$; so the $2(K_0-1)$
ladder rows are distinct and avoid rows $1,2$, and $K_0\ge2$: on $A$
because $\pi$ is a derangement, on $B$ by the admissibility of the mark
($\pi(1)\ne2$, $\pi(2)\ne1$).

\begin{theorem}[ladder identity]\label{thm:ladder}
For every $n$, $\lambda$ and split $\alpha+\beta=m$ with
$\alpha,\beta\ge2$,
\[
\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A]\;=\;\Pr_X\bigl[B,\ \mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\bigr]
-\Pr_X\bigl[A,\ \mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\bigr],
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\bigl|\Pr_X[B]-\tfrac12\bigr|\;\le\;\tfrac12\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset].
\]
The same identity holds with $X$ replaced by $S(R)\times\{P\}$ for any
fixed admissible pair of first rows $R$ and mark $P$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $(L,P)\in X$ and write $K=\{1,\dots,K_0-1\}$.  The ladder depends
on $(L,P)$, so the $T_k$ are not maps of $X$; the statement to prove is
that $(L,P)\sim T_S(L,P)$, $S\subseteq K$ (the trades at the
unordered pairs of $S$, in any order), is an equivalence relation on
$X$ whose classes have exactly $2^{|K|}$ elements and on which the bit
is $\mathbf 1_B(T_S(L,P))=\mathbf 1_B(L,P)+|S\cap\mathrm{Flip}|\bmod2$.
This follows once we know that each $T_k$ is an involution of $X$
which preserves $K_0$, the set of unordered ladder pairs and the
flippability of each of them, that $T_k$ toggles the bit exactly when
$k\in\mathrm{Flip}$, and that trades at disjoint row pairs commute:
then the same trades, at the same unordered pairs with the same
flippabilities, are available from every point of a class (symmetry
and transitivity), the $2^{|K|}$ points $T_S(L,P)$ are distinct
(rows $x_k,y_k$ are changed by $T_k$ and by no other $T_i$), and the
bit formula holds.  If $\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset$ exactly half of the
subsets $S$ have $|S\cap\mathrm{Flip}|$ odd, so $B$ and $A$ each
contain half of the class; summing over classes gives the identity,
and the bound follows from $\Pr[B]+\Pr[A]=1$.

\emph{$T_k$ is a well-defined involution of $X$.}  The $j'$-trade at
$(x_k,y_k)$ changes only rows $x_k,y_k\notin\{1,2\}$, so $\sigma$ and
the mark are untouched and $(L,P)$ stays in $X$; it is an involution
because the traded columns are again the $\rho'_{x_k,y_k}$-cycle
through $j'$ (\S\ref{sec:s13trapped}).

\emph{Effect on the frame.}  If $k\in\mathrm{Flip}$ the new frame is
$\pi'=(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi$.  On $A$ this merges $C_1$ and $C_2$: the
new cycle runs $1\to\pi(1)\to\dots\to x_{k+1}\to y_k\to\pi(y_k)\to\dots
\to2\to\dots\to y_{k+1}\to x_k\to\dots\to1$, so $d'_{12}=|C_1|$ and
$d'_{21}=|C_2|$ (reading $x_{|C_1|}=1$, $y_{|C_2|}=2$).  On $B$,
$x_k$ lies on the arc from $2$ to $1$ and $y_k$ on the arc from $1$ to
$2$ (as $k<\min(d_{12},d_{21})$), and the split into
$(x_k,\pi(x_k),\dots,\pi^{-1}(y_k))$ and $(y_k,\dots,\pi^{-1}(x_k))$
puts $1$ ($k$ steps after $x_k$, fewer than the $d_{12}$ steps from
$x_k$ to $y_k$) in the first cycle and $2$ in the second: the new
state is in $A$ with $|C'_1|=d_{12}$, $|C'_2|=d_{21}$.  In both cases
$K_0$ is preserved and the bit toggles.  If $k\notin\mathrm{Flip}$ the
new frame is $(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi\circ(x_k\,y_k)$: the cycle partition
is unchanged up to exchanging the labels $x_k,y_k$, so $K_0$ and the
bit are preserved.

\emph{The ladder is preserved.}  Let $\pi'=(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi$ (the
conjugation case is similar and easier).  Since
$\pi'^{-1}(r)=\pi^{-1}((x_k\,y_k)(r))$, the preimage chains of $1$ and
$2$ under $\pi'$ agree with those under $\pi$ up to depth $k$ and are
exchanged beyond it: $\pi'^{-i}(1)=x_i$ and $\pi'^{-i}(2)=y_i$ for
$i\le k$, while $\pi'^{-(k+1)}(1)=\pi^{-1}(y_k)=y_{k+1}$ and then
$\pi'^{-i}(1)=y_i$, $\pi'^{-i}(2)=x_i$ for $k<i<K_0$.  So the unordered
pairs $\{x_i,y_i\}$, $i\in K$, are the ladder of $T_k(L,P)$.  In the
conjugation case the same computation with $(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi\circ(x_k\,y_k)$
gives $\pi'^{-i}(1)=(x_k\,y_k)(x_i)$, which again permutes the pairs
$\{x_i,y_i\}$ among themselves (only $i=k$ is affected, by exchanging
$x_k,y_k$).

\emph{Flippability is preserved and the $T_k$ commute.}  $T_k$ changes
rows $x_k,y_k$ only, and the flippability of $(x_i,y_i)$, $i\ne k$,
depends only on rows $x_i,y_i$, which are different rows.  Likewise
$T_i$ and $T_k$ act on disjoint pairs of rows, each along a column set
determined by its own two rows, so $T_iT_k=T_kT_i$.  (After $T_k$ the
pair $\{x_i,y_i\}$ may have changed its index, but the trade at an
unordered pair does not depend on the index or on the order of the
two rows.)

The statement for fixed $R$ holds because every $T_k$ fixes rows $1$
and $2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[the conjecture reduces to the ladder]\label{cor:ladder}
Write $f(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)=\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]$.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked}, $\bar q(\mu\to\lambda)-1=(\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A])/\Pr_X[A]$
and $\Pr_X[A]\ge(1-f)/2$, so $|\bar q-1|\le2f/(1-f)$: $\mathrm{EQ}(\delta)$
holds with $\delta(n)=\max_{\lambda,\alpha\ne\beta}2f/(1-f)$.  If this
$\delta(n)\to0$, Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} gives
\[
\dtv(\PP_n,\QQ_n)=O\Bigl(\log n\cdot\max_{\lambda,\alpha\ne\beta}
f(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)+n^{-2/3}\Bigr).
\]
In particular the weak CGW conjecture follows from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L}
\textup{(L)}\qquad \Pr_X\bigl[\text{no ladder pair is flippable}\bigr]=o(1/\log n)
\quad\text{uniformly in }\lambda,\alpha,\beta .
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[what (L) asks, and what the data say]\label{rem:ladderdata}
(i) The ladder consists of $K_0-1$ pairs of \emph{disjoint} rows,
$K_0=\min(|C_1|,|C_2|)$ resp.\ $\min(d_{12},d_{21})$, and a pair is
flippable iff columns $j,j'$ lie in different cycles of its row
permutation --- the complement of the conjugate
(rows$\leftrightarrow$columns) of the bit itself.  (L) does not ask these coins to be fair, only that they
do not all fail.

(ii) Exact enumeration at $n=7$ (\texttt{s13\_comp7.c}, four
instances) confirms the identity to all digits
($\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]=-0.005848$ and $\Pr[B,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]
-\Pr[A,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=-0.005848$ for $\lambda=(7)$,
$\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{2,5\}$; $+0.003509$ for $\{3,4\}$; $-0.004651$
for $(5,2)$; $0$ for a cross-cycle pair), where however
$\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=0.40$--$0.42$: the bound only bites for
large $n$.  On uniform squares with true marks
(\texttt{s13\_ladder.py}, \texttt{runs/s13/f1/ladder\{30,50,100\}.log};
$9000$, $7200$, $3000$ instances):
\[
\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid K_0]=2^{-(K_0-1)}
\]
within statistical error for $K_0\le6$ and consistent with it beyond
($n=50$: $0.480,0.265,0.142,0.070,0.036,0.030,0.002$ for $K_0=2..8$
against $0.5,0.25,0.125,0.0625,0.031,0.016,0.008$), the ladder coins
are flippable with frequency $0.4990$, $0.5002$, $0.4993$, and
$\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=0.125,\ 0.079,\ 0.041$ against
$\E[2^{-(K_0-1)}]=0.124,\ 0.077,\ 0.038$.  Since $\Pr[K_0=k]\approx4/n$
for small $k$ and $\sum_{k\ge2}2^{-(k-1)}=1$, this is $\approx4/n$
($0.133,0.080,0.040$): if the coins are fair and independent, (L)
holds with $O(1/n)$ and the conjecture would follow with
$\dtv=O(\log n/n)$.  The heuristic rests on uniformity: for
isotopes of $\mathbb Z_n$ every $\rho_{x,y}$ is an $n$-cycle and
$\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset$ always.

(iii) \emph{Cost of a short ladder.}
$\Pr[A,K_0\le\ell]\le\Pr[|C_1|\le\ell]+\Pr[|C_2|\le\ell]\le72\ell/n$
for $\ell\le n/2-1$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped}.  On $B$ the
ladder identity itself gives
$\Pr[B,K_0\le\ell,\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset]
=\Pr[A,K_0\le\ell,\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset]\le72\ell/n$, and the
switching of Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} gives
$\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\le n/2]\le4/n$ (and the same for $d_{21}$):
from such a square pick $y$ strictly inside the arc from $1$ to $2$
($\ell-1$ choices) and $x$ in a free cycle $C_x$ ($n-|C_1|\ge n/2$
choices), turn $C_x$ if $(x,y)$ is not flippable, and $j'$-trade; the
arc becomes $1\to\dots\to\pi^{-1}(y)\to x\to(C_x)\to y\to\dots\to2$, so
$d_{12}$ grows by $|C_x|$ and nothing else changes; given the target,
the position of $x$ on the new arc ($\ell-1$ choices) and the binary
``turn back'' pin the move, so there are at most $2(\ell-1)$
preimages.  What is missing is
$\Pr[B,\ K_0\le\ell_0,\ |C_1|>n/2,\ \mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=O(\ell_0/n)$:
a short arc inside a cycle covering most rows, with no flippable
ladder pair; there the free rows may be absent and the flippability
of a pair inside $C_1$ cannot be toggled by a turn.  \emph{To do.}

(iv) \emph{Local witnesses.}  A ladder pair is flippable as soon as
the $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$-cycle through $j$ is short and avoids $j'$ (or
the one through $j'$ is short and avoids $j$).  On real squares the
length of the $j$-cycle of a ladder pair is spread like a uniform
variable on $\{2,\dots,n\}$ ($\Pr[\le\ell]=0.021,0.081,0.18,0.38$ for
$\ell=2,5,10,20$ at $n=50$ against $(\ell-1)/(n-1)=0.020,0.082,0.18,0.39$;
$0.010,0.041,0.092,0.19$ at $n=100$), and among instances with
$K_0\ge n/4$ a flippable ladder pair with $j$-cycle of length $\le10$
exists in $93\%$ ($n=50$) and $95\%$ ($n=100$), $\le20$ in $99.6\%$
and $99.8\%$.  A non-flippable pair has $j'$ on its $j$-cycle, hence
no $j$-cycle avoiding $j'$ of any length, so
$\{\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset,K_0\ge\ell_0\}$ is contained in the event
below; granting the missing part of (iii) with $\ell_0=n/\log^3n$
(total cost $O(1/\log^3n)$), (L) follows from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lloc}
\Pr\Bigl[K_0\ge\tfrac n{\log^3n}\ \text{and no ladder pair has a
$j$-cycle of length}\le\log^5n\text{ avoiding }j'\Bigr]=o(1/\log n),
\end{equation}
a statement about at least $n/\log^3n$ disjoint row pairs each of
which carries such a cycle with probability $\approx\log^5n/n$
(expected number $\gtrsim\log^2n$ witnesses).  Short cycles through a given
column in the row permutation of a given row pair are substructures
of polylogarithmic size, so \eqref{eq:Lloc} is the kind of statement
that the switching method of Kwan--Sudakov \cite{KS18} and its
successors is built for: a second-moment bound needs
$\operatorname{Cov}(W_k,W_{k'})=o(\Pr[W_k]\Pr[W_{k'}]/\log n)$ for
disjoint pairs $k\ne k'$ (with $\E\sum_kW_k\gtrsim\log^2n$ the
diagonal term is already $O(1/\log^2 n)$), in the space of completions
of two rows and two columns.  No fairness of any global statistic is needed anywhere.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[relation to the earlier programme]
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} is the orbit identity of the trade chain
(\S\ref{sec:s13tradechain}(b)) specialised to the abelian subgroup
generated by the ladder trades, for which the orbit structure is
explicit: on each orbit the bit is \emph{exactly} fair unless no ladder
pair is flippable.  Compared with the pool of intercalates
(Theorem~\ref{thm:master}) the ladder has deterministic bit-changing
pairs and an exact bias formula, at the price of needing the pairs
to be flippable; compared with the trade chain it needs no mixing.
The quenched version (fixed $R$) reduces the fairness Question of
\texttt{paper/fairness\_question.tex} to the quenched form of (L):
$|2\Pr[B\mid R]-1|\le\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid R]$, and since the
ladder classes refine the components of the trade chain, also
$|2p(K)-1|\le\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid K]$ for every component
$K$.  Items (I), (II), (III), (T),
(F1), (F2) and the component question are superseded by (L).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Offset ladders, and what (L) needs}\label{sec:ladderoffset}

The ladder is one of a family.  For an integer $c$ (an \emph{offset}) put
\[
K_c=\begin{cases}\min(|C_1|-c,\ |C_2|)&\text{on }A,\\
\min(d_{21}-c,\ d_{12})&\text{on }B,\end{cases}
\qquad
D_c=\bigl\{(x_{c+i},\,y_i)=(\pi^{-(c+i)}(1),\pi^{-i}(2)):\ 1\le i<K_c\bigr\},
\]
so $D_0$ is the ladder and for $c>0$ the $x$-row is $c$ steps deeper
than the $y$-row ($x$-depths $c+1,\dots,c+K_c-1$ stay inside
$C_1\setminus\{1\}$, resp.\ the arc from $2$ to $1$; $y$-depths
$1,\dots,K_c-1$ inside $C_2\setminus\{2\}$, resp.\ the arc from $1$ to
$2$).  We take $c\ge0$; the families with the $y$-row deeper are
obtained by exchanging the roles of rows $1$ and $2$ (and of
$C_1,C_2$, $d_{12},d_{21}$) throughout.

\begin{theorem}[offset ladders]\label{thm:ladderoffset}
For every offset $c\ge0$, on the domain $\mathcal D_c=\{K_c\ge2\}$
(where $D_c\ne\emptyset$) the $j'$-trades at the pairs of $D_c$ are commuting
involutions that preserve $K_c$, the unordered pairs of $D_c$ and their
flippabilities, and toggle the bit iff the traded pair is flippable.
Hence, with $\mathrm{Flip}_c$ the set of flippable pairs of $D_c$,
\[
\Pr[B,\mathcal D_c]-\Pr[A,\mathcal D_c]
=\Pr[B,\mathcal D_c,\mathrm{Flip}_c=\emptyset]-\Pr[A,\mathcal D_c,\mathrm{Flip}_c=\emptyset].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identical to Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder}; only the bookkeeping changes.
Let $\pi'=(x_{c+i}\,y_i)\circ\pi$.  The preimage chain of $1$ under
$\pi'$ agrees with that of $\pi$ up to depth $c+i$ and then continues
along the chain of $2$: $\pi'^{-(c+i+t)}(1)=y_{i+t}$; symmetrically
$\pi'^{-(i+t)}(2)=x_{c+i+t}$ ($t\ge1$).  So the pair of $D_c$ at
$y$-depth $i'>i$ is $\{y_{i'},x_{c+i'}\}$, unchanged as a set, and the
pairs at depth $i'<i$ are untouched: $D_c$ is preserved.  (The same
computation shows that trading a pair at depths $(k,k')$ sends a
deeper pair at depths $(K,K')$ to depths $(K'+k-k',\,K-k+k')$, whose
offset is $2(k-k')-(K-K')$; it is preserved iff $K-K'=k-k'$, so only
families of constant offset, i.e.\ the $D_c$, are preserved.)  On $A$
the trade merges $C_1,C_2$ into a cycle with
$d'_{12}=|C_1|-(c+i)+i=|C_1|-c$ and $d'_{21}=|C_2|+c$, so
$K'_c=\min(d'_{21}-c,d'_{12})=\min(|C_2|,|C_1|-c)=K_c$; on $B$ the pair
separates rows $1,2$ ($c+i<d_{21}$ puts $x_{c+i}$ on the arc from $2$
to $1$, $i<d_{12}$ puts $y_i$ on the arc from $1$ to $2$) and gives
$|C'_1|=d_{12}+c$, $|C'_2|=d_{21}-c$, again
$K'_c=\min(d_{12},d_{21}-c)=K_c$.  No pair of $D_c$ is frame-adjacent:
$y_i=\pi(x_{c+i})$ would need $d_{21}=c-1$ (or $\pi(1)=2$ when $c=0$)
and $x_{c+i}=\pi(y_i)$ would need $d_{21}=c+1$, both excluded on
$\mathcal D_c$.  Commutation,
preservation of flippability and the orbit count are as before.
\end{proof}

Exact enumeration at $n=7$ ($\lambda=(7)$, $\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{2,5\}$)
confirms the identities for $c=0,1,2,3$ to all digits
($\Pr[B,\mathcal D_c]-\Pr[A,\mathcal D_c]=-0.005848,\ +0.001462,\
+0.003509,\ -0.001316$, equal to the right-hand sides).

\paragraph{Using the offsets to price short arcs.}  Fix $\ell\ge2$ and
consider $B$-instances with $d_{12}=\ell$.  For $0\le c\le n/2-\ell$
and $d_{21}\ge c+\ell$ one has $K_c=\ell$, so $D_c$ has $\ell-1$ pairs,
and the offset-$c$ trade at a flippable pair of $D_c$ lands in $A$
with $|C_1|=\ell+c\le n/2$ and $|C_2|=d_{21}-c\ge\ell$.  Within a
class of $\mathcal D_c$ all $B$-members have the same $(d_{12},d_{21})$
and all $A$-members the same $(|C_1|,|C_2|)=(d_{12}+c,d_{21}-c)$, and
$\mathrm{Flip}_c$ is a class invariant, so Theorem~\ref{thm:ladderoffset}
class by class and Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offsetprice}
\Pr\bigl[B,\ d_{12}=\ell,\ d_{21}\ge c+\ell,\ \mathrm{Flip}_c\ne\emptyset\bigr]
=\Pr\bigl[A,\ |C_1|=\ell+c,\ |C_2|\ge\ell,\ \mathrm{Flip}_c\ne\emptyset\bigr]
\le\frac{18}{n-\ell-c-1}\le\frac{36}{n-2}.
\end{equation}
Averaging over $c$: if $\theta(L,P)$ denotes the fraction of offsets
$c\in[0,n/2-\ell]$ whose diagonal $D_c$ contains a flippable pair,
then for every $\theta_0>0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offsetavg}
\Pr\bigl[B,\ d_{12}=\ell,\ |C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,\ \theta\ge\theta_0\bigr]\le\frac{36}{(n-2)\,\theta_0},
\end{equation}
since on $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$ one has $d_{21}=|C_1|-\ell\ge n/2\ge c+\ell$
for every such $c$, so $\E[\theta\,\mathbf 1_E]$ is the average over
$c$ of the left side of \eqref{eq:offsetprice}, and Markov's
inequality applies.  Together with the splice-in bound
$\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|<n/2+\ell]\le4/(n-2\ell+1)\le8/n$ for
$\ell\le n/4$ (the argument of Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iii) with
$n-|C_1|>n/2-\ell$ free rows), this prices every short arc except on
the event $\{\theta<\theta_0\}$.

\paragraph{The single remaining input.}  Say that a pair of rows
$(x,y)$, $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$, carries a \emph{witness} if the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through column $j$ has length at most
$\ell'=\log^5n$ and does not contain $j'$ (then $(x,y)$ is flippable).
A frame-adjacent pair ($y=\pi(x)$, so $\rho_{x,y}(j)=j'$) never
carries one; the families below contain no such pair.
For a pair of rows of a random Latin square the length of the
$j$-cycle is spread like a uniform variable on $\{2,\dots,n\}$
(Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iv)), so a witness has probability
$\approx\ell'/n$.

\begin{hypothesis}[witness lemma]\label{hyp:witness}
Let $(L,P)$ be uniform on $X$.  Let $\mathcal G$ be one of the
following families of \emph{blocks} of pairs of rows, determined by
the frame: (i) the ladder, each pair a block of its own; (ii) for a
$B$-instance with $d_{12}=\ell\le n/4$ and $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$, the
diagonals $D_c$, $0\le c\le n/2-\ell$, each a block of $\ell-1$ pairs.
Let $G_{\mathcal G}$ be the number of blocks containing a pair that
carries a witness, and
$\mu_{\mathcal G}=\sum_{\mathcal B\in\mathcal G}\min\bigl(\tfrac12,\ell'|\mathcal B|/n\bigr)$.
Then there is a constant $\kappa>0$ such that
\[
\Pr\bigl[G_{\mathcal G}<\kappa\,\mu_{\mathcal G}\ \text{ and }\ \mu_{\mathcal G}\ge\log^2n\bigr]=o(1/\log n).
\]
(This is annealed: an exceptional set of frames of probability
$o(1/\log n)$ is allowed.  For a single pair of non-adjacent rows the
witness probability is $\approx\ell'/n$, Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iv),
so $\kappa\mu_{\mathcal G}$ is a constant fraction of the expected
count if blocks are nearly independent given their shared rows.)
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{proposition}[(L) follows from the witness lemma]\label{prop:Lwitness}
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness} implies \textup{(L)}, hence the weak CGW
conjecture.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $\ell_0=n/\log^2n$.  By Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder},
$|\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]|\le\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]\le
\Pr[K_0\ge\ell_0,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]+\Pr[A,K_0<\ell_0]+\Pr[B,K_0<\ell_0]$.

\emph{Long ladders.}  On $\{K_0\ge\ell_0\}$ take family (i):
$\mu\ge(\ell_0-1)\ell'/n\ge\log^2n$, so $G\ge\kappa\mu\ge1$ except on an
event of probability $o(1/\log n)$, and $G\ge1$ means
$\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset$.

\emph{Short cycles.}  $\Pr[A,K_0<\ell_0]\le72\ell_0/n=72/\log^2n$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped}.

\emph{Short arcs.}  $\Pr[B,K_0<\ell_0]\le\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}\bigl(\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell]+\Pr[B,d_{21}=\ell]\bigr)$;
we treat $d_{12}=\ell$, and $d_{21}=\ell$ is the same argument with the
roles of rows $1,2$ exchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} covers
$C_2$, and the offset families become those with the $y$-row deeper).
Fix $\ell$.  The part with $|C_1|<n/2+\ell$ costs $\le8/n$.  On
$|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$ take family (ii), with $N=\lfloor n/2-\ell\rfloor+1\ge n/4$
blocks: $\mu=N\min(\tfrac12,(\ell-1)\ell'/n)\ge\tfrac14\min(n/2,(\ell-1)\ell')\ge\log^2n$,
so except on an event $F_\ell$ of probability $o(1/\log n)$ the
fraction of offsets whose diagonal contains a flippable pair is
$\theta=G/N\ge\theta_0(\ell):=\kappa\min(\tfrac12,(\ell-1)\ell'/n)$.  By
\eqref{eq:offsetavg},
\[
\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,\theta\ge\theta_0(\ell)]
\le\sum_{\ell-1\le n/(2\ell')}\frac{36\,n}{(n-2)\,\kappa\,(\ell-1)\ell'}
+\sum_{n/(2\ell')<\ell-1<\ell_0}\frac{72}{(n-2)\kappa}
=O\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{\ell'}+\frac{\ell_0}{n}\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\frac1{\log^2n}\Bigr),
\]
and $\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}8/n\le8/\log^2n$.  The exceptional events: the
family (ii) is a function of the frame, and the events $\{d_{12}=\ell\}$
are disjoint, so $\sum_\ell\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,F_\ell]
=\Pr\bigl[\bigcup_\ell\{B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,F_\ell\}\bigr]$ is
the probability of a single failure event of the hypothesis, which is
$o(1/\log n)$.
Altogether $\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=o(1/\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the witness lemma is, and is not]
(i) It is a statement about substructures of polylogarithmic size
(a cycle of length $\le\log^4n$ of a row-pair permutation through a
given column), in the space of completions of two rows and two
columns, for families of pairs selected by the frame.  It asks for a
lower bound on their expected number and a lower-tail
concentration (a second moment with relative variance $o(1/\log n)$
suffices) --- the territory of
the switching method \cite{KS18}: a switching that shortens or lengthens
the $j$-cycle of $\rho_{x,y}$ by a bounded amount, applied in the
conditional space, compares the counts of squares with
$j$-cycle lengths $m$ and $m+1$ and gives the uniform spread, and the
same switchings applied to two pairs at once control the covariance.
(ii) It asks nothing about long cycles, same-cycle events, or the
fairness of any global statistic.  (iii) Rigid squares ($\mathbb Z_p$
isotopes and their relatives, where no row pair has a short cycle)
enter only through the failure probability, which must be
$o(1/\log n)$; for them the hypothesis is simply the statement that
they are rare.  (iv) The constants: $\ell'=\log^5n$ and
$\ell_0=n/\log^2n$ are one admissible choice; the proof needs
$\ell_0\ell'/n\ge\log^2n$, $\ell_0/n=o(1/\log n)$ and
$\log n/\ell'=o(1/\log n)$.  (v) The block form is what the
short-arc argument uses: the number of offsets with a witness, not the
number of witnesses, since the diagonals of different offsets are
dependent through the shared rows $y_i$.  (vi) The hypothesis is
stated only for the two families used; a version for arbitrary
frame-determined families would be false, since frame-adjacent pairs
never carry a witness.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Status of the witness lemma: literature and data}\label{sec:witnessstatus}

\paragraph{Data.}  \texttt{s13\_witness.py} (\texttt{runs/s13/f1/witness50.log}:
$9000$ instances at $n=50$).  The single-pair witness frequency for
ladder pairs is $0.079,0.168,0.314$ for $\ell'=5,10,20$ against
$(\ell'-1)/(n-1)=0.082,0.184,0.388$ (the deficit is the probability
that $j'$ lies on the short cycle, $\approx\ell'/n$).  For two
disjoint ladder pairs $\E[WW']/p^2=1.03,1.00,0.99$ ($1.7\cdot10^5$
samples).  The number $G$ of ladder pairs with a witness and the number
$S$ of rows $x$ preceding row $1$ whose pair with $y_1=\pi^{-1}(2)$
carries a witness (the ``star'' family, all pairs sharing one row)
both have variance equal to the binomial value within the resolution
of the data (ratios $0.95$--$1.1$ in bins of $K_0$, resp.\ of the star
size; the inflation in the smallest bins is the spread of $K_0$ within
a bin).  So at $n=50$ the witness indicators behave as independent
events of probability $\approx\ell'/n$, both across disjoint pairs and
across pairs sharing a row, to within a few percent --- the hypothesis
holds with margin in the data.

\paragraph{Literature (session-13 scout; see the references).}  Nothing
in print gives the witness lemma, but the tools are there.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item Cavenagh--Greenhill--Wanless \cite{CGW08} work with switchings
inside two rows (flip, backflip, cross-switch), uniformly over an
arbitrary prescription of a set of full columns, and prove that the
cycle-type law of a row pair is within a factor $3/2$ of the
derangement law per merge/split (their Theorem~3.13), within $n^{3/2}$
overall for types with $\le\frac65\log n$ cycles (Corollary~4.6),
$\Pr[\text{single $n$-cycle}]\in[2n^{-2},2n^{-2/3}]$ (Lemma~4.4), and
whp more than $(\log n)^{1-c}$ cycles (Theorem~4.11).  Their
Theorem~3.13 is exactly $\Pr_X[A]\ge1/3$ (\S\ref{sec:cgwcycles}), and
summed over types it gives the unconditional lower-bound clause of the
witness lemma with the right dependence on $\ell'$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles}); what it does not give is the
conditional version (two prescribed columns on which the two rows
carry distinct symbols) or the concentration.
\item Allsop--Morris \cite{AM26} give universal bounds
$(\delta/n)^{|P|}\le\Pr[L\supseteq P]\le(\Delta/n)^{|P|}$ for partial
squares $P$ on $\le\alpha n$ rows and $\le\beta n$ columns, $2\alpha+\beta<1$.
Summed over the patterns of a $j$-cycle of length $\ell$ this gives
$\Pr[\text{length }\ell]\in[\delta^{2\ell},\Delta^{2\ell}]/n$: the
right order $1/n$ for each $\ell$, with an exponential-in-$\ell$ slack;
whether $\delta=\Delta=1+o(1)$ for small $P$ is stated as open there,
and that is exactly the witness lemma's lower-bound clause.  Their
hypotheses do not allow full prescribed columns together with
prescribed rows, so the conditioning on rows $1,2$ and columns $j,j'$
would have to be handled separately (the switchings never touch those
cells, so this should be bookkeeping).
\item Lower tails: Kwan--Sudakov and Kwan--Sah--Sawhney bound lower
tails of the \emph{total} intercalate count with $\exp(-n^{c})$ errors,
by comparison with Latin rectangles and the McKay--Wanless completion
ratio $e^{O(n\log^2n)}$; that technique is useless for a count of
polylogarithmic mean.  For the witness lemma one needs either a second
moment with pairwise covariances of relative size $o(1/\log n)$
(switchings on two pairs at once, exact to $1+O(\mathrm{polylog}/n)$)
or a Poisson-type lower tail from matched creation/destruction
degrees, as in the Kwan--Sudakov ratio method.
\item Nothing is known about any ``two given columns in different
cycles'' event with constant probability, in any conjugate form; the
witness lemma deliberately avoids such events.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Verdict.}  (L), hence the weak CGW conjecture, is reduced
to a local statement (Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness}) whose two clauses
--- $\Theta(\ell'/n)$ lower bounds for short $j$-cycles of prescribed
row pairs in the space of completions of two rows and two columns, and
lower-tail concentration of their count across a frame-determined
family --- are true in the data with margin and are of the kind the
two-row switching method of \cite{CGW08,KS18} is designed for.  The
unconditional form of the first clause follows from \cite{CGW08}
(Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} below); the conditional form and
the concentration are what remains.

\subsection{The unconditional witness bound follows from CGW's switching theorem}
\label{sec:cgwcycles}

The lower-bound clause of the witness lemma, for a fixed pair of rows
of a uniform square without any conditioning, is a consequence of a
theorem that has been in print since 2008.  Cavenagh, Greenhill and
Wanless \cite{CGW08} (Lemma 3.12, Theorem 3.13) prove, for adjacent
types $\lambda$ (one part $m=\alpha+\beta$) and $\mu$ (that part split
into $\alpha\ne\beta$), that
\[
\tfrac12\;\le\;\frac{|S(\lambda)|\,\lambda_m\,m}{|S(\mu)|\,\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\,\alpha\beta}
\;\le\;\tfrac32 ,
\]
uniformly over $\lambda,\alpha,\beta$ (and over any prescription of a
set of full columns on which rows $1,2$ carry the same symbols).  In
the notation of \S\ref{sec:marked} the middle term is $|X|/|J|$ for
$X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$ and $J=J(n,\mu;\alpha,\beta)$, so by
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} ($|J|=2|X_A|$) the CGW theorem is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgwA}
\tfrac13\;\le\;\Pr_X[A]\;\le\;1
\qquad\text{for every }n,\lambda,\alpha,\beta\quad\text{(lower bound: CGW($3/2$), see Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap})}
\end{equation}
(for $\alpha=\beta$ CGW's second form of the lemma and the $\alpha=\beta$
conventions of \S\ref{sec:marked} give the same middle term $|X|/|J|$):
rows $1,2$ lie in different frame cycles of a marked pair with
probability at least $1/3$.  The bound is attained: at $n=5$,
$(5)\to(2,3)$, $|X_A|=1440$, $|X_B|=2880$, $\Pr_X[A]=1/3$ exactly, and
CGW's $3/2$ is sharp there.  (The upper bound $\Pr_X[A]\le1$ is the
trivial direction $|J|\le2|X|$ of our identity; CGW's constant $1/2$
is its switching-count shadow.)  The conjecture asks for $\Pr_X[A]=\frac12+o(1/\log n)$.

\begin{remark}[a gap in the published proof of CGW's Lemma~3.12]\label{rem:cgwgap}
The lower bound in \eqref{eq:cgwA} is CGW's upper bound $3/2$, and
its proof requires every splitting pair of a $\lambda$-square to
carry exactly two edges.  Case~3 of their splitting procedure
(``$\{j,j'\}$ and $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ both $B$-pairs:
cross-switch with respect to $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$, then backflip
with respect to $\{j,j'\}$'') does not produce an element of
$S(\mu,F)$ when $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$.  Indeed the rows-$1,2$ part of
the cross-switch at a $B$-pair $(J,J')$ with $\omega^\alpha J=J'$ is
forced by the proof of their Lemma~3.10 (row $1$ keeps the symbol set
$\{1\circ\omega^kJ:1\le k\le\alpha\}$ on the cells
$\{\omega^kJ:1\le k<\alpha\}\cup\{J'\}$, and in every interior column
the two rows are swapped), which gives $1'\circ\omega^kJ=1\circ\omega^{k+1}J$
for $1\le k<\alpha$, $1'\circ J'=1\circ\omega J$, $2'\circ\omega^kJ=1\circ\omega^kJ$,
$2'\circ J=1\circ J'$; hence $\omega'(J)=\omega^{\alpha-1}J$,
$\omega'(\omega^kJ)=\omega^{k-1}J$ for $2\le k<\alpha$, $\omega'(\omega J)=J'$:
the cross-switch \emph{reverses the $\omega$-arc from $J$ to $J'$}.
With $(J,J')=(\omega j,\omega j')$ the reversed arc has $j'=\omega^{\alpha-1}J$
in its interior, so afterwards $\omega'(j)=\omega j$ and
$\omega'(\omega j)=j'$: the pair $\{j,j'\}$ is at distance $2$, and the
backflip at it splits the $(\alpha+\beta)$-cycle into parts $2$ and
$\alpha+\beta-2$.  For $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2<\max(\alpha,\beta)$ the
type is still $\mu$, but the proof is still incomplete: the form of
the cross-switch (chosen by the smallest corner symbol) that reverses
the \emph{long} arc moves $j$ or $j'$, so that joining rule~4 from the
backflipped square cross-switches at a pair other than
$\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ and does not return $L$; the case-3 edge and
its switch edge are then missing from $G_J$, and the identity
$G_S=G_J$ asserted in the proof of Lemma~3.12 fails.  Exact
computation (\texttt{s13\_cgw\_multigraph.py}, all $161280$ squares of
order $5$, $\lambda=(5)\to\mu=(2,3)$ --- the sharp case): $G_S$ and
$G_J$ have $1036800$ edges each, $172800$ edges of $G_S$ are not in
$G_J$ (half of the case-3 edges and their switch edges; all case-1,2
edges are in $G_J$), and the $\mu$-squares have $G_S$-degrees
$12,18,24$ (for $11520,34560,11520$ squares) against $18$ in $G_J$; so
the degree bound $\le3\alpha\beta=18$ asserted for every $\mu$-vertex
is false for $G_S$ (it holds on average, so the edge-count inequality
still holds at $n=5$, with equality, but not for the reason given).  For $(4)\to(2,2)$ the multigraphs coincide.  So the
published argument is incomplete for every split with
$\max(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$; the only unaffected split is $(2,2)$.
Independent check: \texttt{s13\_cgwcase3.py} implements the
cross-switch (rows $1,2$ as above, columns $j,j'$ swapped on the
$\delta_{j,j'}$-arc from row $2$ to row $1$, which is what balances
the corner cells), verifies that it is a Latin square of the same type
in every instance, and finds that case~3 lands in the wrong type in
$2676$ of $2676$ instances at $n=9,12$ with $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$
(a referee subagent found the gap first, with its own implementation).
We found no published correction (the text we use is the journal
version, DOI 10.1002/rsa.20216, and the example on its p.~293 exhibits
the reversal: $\omega=(1234567)$, $j=2$, $j'=5$, $\alpha=3$, and after
the cross-switch $\omega'=(1243567)$).

\emph{A partial repair.}  A cross-switch at a $B$-pair $(J,J')$ at
any distance $a\ge2$ is a type-preserving trade (the proof of
Lemma~3.10 does not use $a=\alpha$), it reverses the $\omega$-arc from
$J$ to $J'$, and it toggles $\{j,j'\}$ iff exactly one of $j,j'$ lies in
the interior of that arc, say at position $i$; the distance from $j$
to $j'$ then changes by $a-2i$.  So the distance-preserving toggles
of $\{j,j'\}$ are exactly the cross-switches at pairs \emph{symmetric
about $j$ or about $j'$} ($a=2i$), and they exist as $B$-pairs or not.
When $\alpha$ is even and $\alpha<2\beta$, the pair
$R=(\omega^{\alpha/2}j,\omega^{3\alpha/2}j)$ --- symmetric about $j'$,
at distance $\alpha$, with $j$ outside its arc --- always gives an
edge: if $R$ is $B$, cross-switch at $R$ and backflip at $\{j,j'\}$
(now an $A$-pair at distance $\alpha$); if $R$ is $A$, backflip at
$R$ itself.  Symmetrically for $\beta$ even and $\beta<2\alpha$.
(\texttt{s13\_cgwfix.py}: type $\mu$ in all $3928$ covered instances
at $n=10,13$.)  The $\mu$-side multiplicities change (an $A$-pair's
backflip may serve two splitting pairs), so the constant $3/2$ would
have to be recomputed.  When $\alpha,\beta$ are both odd, every
distance-preserving toggle has even arc length $a\ne\alpha,\beta$, so
if all the symmetric pairs are $A$-pairs no one-cross-switch repair
exists and we see no canonical repair; the same holds for
$\alpha\ge2\beta$ with $\beta$ odd.  The gap is therefore open in
those classes, which the reduction chains do use (and the
$G_S=G_J$ failure for $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$ is not addressed by this
repair at all).

\emph{Our own machinery bears on the missing direction.}  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder}, $\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A]=\Pr_X[B,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]-\Pr_X[A,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]\le\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]$,
so $\Pr_X[B]\le\tfrac12+\tfrac12\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]$: CGW's
$3/2$, i.e.\ $\Pr_X[B]\le2/3$, follows from
$\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]\le1/3$ --- a constant-probability
version of (L), e.g.\ (CP) with $c\ge2/3$ for a single ladder pair
(sharp at $n=5$, where $\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=1/3$ and
$\Pr_X[B]=2/3$).
Conversely nothing in the present notes uses CGW($3/2$) on the main
line.

\emph{What survives:} the direction
$|S(\lambda)|N_\lambda\ge\tfrac12|S(\mu)|N_\mu$, which by
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} is the trivial $|X|\ge|X_A|$; CGW's
Lemma~4.1 upper bound ($|S(\lambda,F)|(m-1)!/(|S((m),F)|\gamma(\lambda))\le2^{\kappa-1}$),
Lemma~4.4's lower bound $\Pr[(m)]\ge2/m^2$, and the upper bounds of
Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} (the factor $2$, hence
$\E[N_\ell]\le(4+o(1))/\ell$).  \emph{What is now conditional} on the
truth of CGW's $3/2$ --- call it \textup{CGW($3/2$)}, equivalently
$\Pr_X[B]\le2/3$ for all $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)$, a weak form of our
own target $\Pr_X[B]=\tfrac12+o(1/\log n)$ --- is: the lower bound
$\tfrac13$ in \eqref{eq:cgwA}; the lower bounds (factor $\tfrac23$) of
Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} and the witness bound
$(\tfrac23-o(1))(\ell'-1)/n$; the unconditional (CP) constant $2/5$
of \S\ref{sec:crossspace}; the ``$\ge\tfrac13$'' of \eqref{eq:CPcols};
CGW's $\Pr[(n)]\le2n^{-2/3}$ and Corollary~4.5, hence the tail bound
$\PP_n(\kappa>A\log n)=O(n^{-2/3})$ used in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} --- now re-derived from the surviving
direction in Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive}.  The
data ($\Pr_X[B]\approx\tfrac12$ throughout) leave no doubt about the
statement, and the exact tables at $n\le11$ verify it there.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[the number of cycles, from the surviving direction only]\label{prop:tailsurvive}
Let $L$ be a uniform Latin square of order $n$, $\sigma$ the column
permutation of rows $1,2$, $N_\ell$ its number of $\ell$-cycles and
$\kappa=\sum_\ell N_\ell$.  Then, using only the identity
$|J|=2|X_A|$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} and $|X_A|\le|X|$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(i)] $\E[\ell N_\ell]\le2n/(n-\ell)$ for every $\ell$; in
particular $\E[N_\ell]\le4/\ell$ for $\ell\le n/2$.
\item[(ii)] For every $k\ge1$, with $(\kappa)_k=\kappa(\kappa-1)\cdots(\kappa-k+1)$,
\[
\E\bigl[(\kappa)_k\bigr]\;\le\;2^{k-1}\sum_{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k\ge2}\frac{\E[mN_m]}{\ell_1\cdots\ell_k}\Big|_{m=\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_k}
\;\le\;2^{k-1}\Bigl(4+\frac{k^2}{\log n}\Bigr)(\log n)^k .
\]
\item[(iii)] For $A\ge3$, $\Pr[\kappa\ge A\log n]\le(7+\log n)\,n^{-\log((A-1)/2)}$,
which is $O(n^{-2}\log n)$ for $A\ge16$;
and $\Pr[\text{all cycles of }\sigma\text{ have the same length }\ell<n]\le4d(n)/n$,
$d(n)$ the number of divisors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For $\beta\ne\ell$ the joining instances $(L,\{j,j'\})$ with $j$ in
an $\ell$-cycle and $j'$ in a $\beta$-cycle number $\ell N_\ell\beta N_\beta$
per square, the splitting instances (pairs at distance $\ell$ on an
$(\ell+\beta)$-cycle) number $(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}$, and
$|J|=2|X_A|\le2|X|$ gives
$\E[\ell N_\ell\,\beta N_\beta]\le2\E[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}]$; for
$\beta=\ell$ the same count with $\ell^2\binom{N_\ell}{2}$ and
$\ell N_{2\ell}$ gives $\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]\le4\ell\E[N_{2\ell}]$.
Summing the first over $\beta\ne\ell$ ($\sum_\beta\beta N_\beta=n$) and
adding the second (its right side is the missing summand $m=2\ell$):
$\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]+\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]\le2\E[\sum_{m\ge\ell+2}mN_m]\le2n$.  Hence
$n\E[\ell N_\ell]=\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]+\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]+\ell\E[\ell N_\ell]
\le2n+\ell\E[\ell N_\ell]$.

(ii) Count the instances $(L,(C_1,j_1),\dots,(C_k,j_k))$: $C_i$
pairwise distinct $\sigma$-cycles of $L$ of lengths $\ell_i$ and
$j_i\in C_i$; there are $\ell_1\cdots\ell_k$ of them per ordered
$k$-tuple of distinct cycles with these lengths.  Join $C_1$ and $C_2$
at the pair $\{j_1,j_2\}$ by a joining step: flip if the pair is an
$A$-pair; if it is a $B$-pair, first trade the row cycle $C_2$ of $j_2$
(a switch in the sense of Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle}, whose proof
applies to the row cycle through either column of the pair; it inverts
$C_2$ in place and does not touch the cycle of $j_1$), then flip.  The
flip replaces $\sigma$ by $\sigma\circ(j_1\,j_2)$ on rows $1,2$, so
the result has $j_1,j_2$ on one cycle of length $\ell_1+\ell_2$ with
$j_2=\sigma'^{\ell_2}(j_1)$ whether or not the trade was applied
(CGW Lemma~3.3), every other cycle of $\sigma$ (in particular
$C_3,\dots,C_k$) untouched, and the map is at most two-to-one (the
backflip at $\{j_1,j_2\}$ returns $L$ or the traded square).  Repeat
with $\{j_1,j_3\}$, \dots, $\{j_1,j_k\}$, always trading the cycle of
the new column when needed, so that the cycle containing $j_1$ is never
inverted: after step $i$ every earlier $j_r$ is shifted by exactly
$\ell_{i+1}$.  After $k-1$ steps all $j_i$ lie on one cycle of length
$m=\sum\ell_i$ at the positions $j_i=\sigma'^{\ell_i+\cdots+\ell_k}(j_1)$,
determined by $j_1$ and $(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)$, and the composite is at
most $2^{k-1}$-to-one.  (With CGW's min-symbol rule the trade may
invert the merged cycle and the positions are not determined; the
count would then be $2^{2k-3}$-to-one.)  Hence the number of instances with lengths
$(\ell_i)$ is at most $2^{k-1}\sum_{L'}mN_m(L')$, i.e.\
$\E[\ell_1\cdots\ell_k\,T(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)]\le2^{k-1}\E[mN_m]$ with
$T$ the number of ordered $k$-tuples of distinct cycles with these
lengths; summing over $(\ell_i)$ gives the first bound, since
$\sum_{(\ell_i)}T=(\kappa)_k$.  For the second, split at $m\le n/2$,
where $\E[mN_m]\le4$ by (i) and
$\sum_{m\le n/2}\sum_{\sum\ell_i=m}\prod\ell_i^{-1}\le(\sum_{\ell\le n/2}\ell^{-1})^k\le(\log n)^k$,
and $m>n/2$, where some $\ell_i\ge m/k$, so
$\sum_{\sum\ell_i=m}\prod\ell_i^{-1}\le k\cdot(k/m)(\log n)^{k-1}$, and
$\sum_{m>n/2}mN_m\cdot k^2(\log n)^{k-1}/m\le k^2(\log n)^{k-1}$ because at
most one cycle is longer than $n/2$.

(iii) Markov for $(\kappa)_k$ with $k=\lceil\log n\rceil\le\log n+1$:
on $\{\kappa\ge K\}$, $(\kappa)_k\ge(K-k+1)^k\ge((A-1)\log n)^k$ for
$K=A\log n$, and $k^2/\log n\le\log n+3$, so
$\Pr[\kappa\ge K]\le2^{k-1}(4+k^2/\log n)(\log n)^k/((A-1)\log n)^k
\le(7+\log n)(2/(A-1))^{k}\le(7+\log n)(2/(A-1))^{\log n}$ for $A\ge3$,
and $(A-1)/2\ge e^2$ for $A\ge16$.  For the equal-length types,
$\ell\mid n$ and $\ell<n$ force $\ell\le n/2$, so
$\Pr[N_\ell=n/\ell]\le\E[N_\ell]\ell/n\le4/n$; $\ell=n$ is the $n$-cycle.
\end{proof}

With this, the tail estimates in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}
no longer need \cite[Cor.~4.5]{CGW08}: $\PP_n(\kappa>K)=O(n^{-2}\log n)$
for $K=\lceil16\log n\rceil$ and $\PP_n(\text{non-plain})=O(n^{-1+o(1)})$,
which is all the proof uses (the exponent of $n$ in the tail only has
to be negative).


\begin{proposition}[short cycles in two rows; lower bounds under CGW($3/2$), Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}]\label{prop:shortcycles}
Let $L$ be a uniform Latin square of order $n$, $\sigma$ the column
permutation of two fixed rows, $N_\ell$ the number of $\ell$-cycles of
$\sigma$, and $Q_q$ the $\sigma$-cycle through a fixed column $q$.
For all $\ell\ne\beta$ with $\ell,\beta\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgwprod}
\tfrac23\,\E\bigl[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}\bigr]\;\le\;\E[\ell N_\ell\cdot\beta N_\beta]\;\le\;2\,\E\bigl[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}\bigr].
\end{equation}
Consequently, with $\tau_n=\Pr[\sigma\text{ has at least }10\sqrt n\text{ cycles}]$,
which is $e^{-\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ for $n\ge n_0$ (\cite{CGW08}, Theorem~4.9; the
theorem is asymptotic, so no explicit $n_0$), for every $2\le\ell\le\sqrt n/40$,
\[
\frac{2}{3\ell}\Bigl(1-\frac{(\ell+1)(4+n\tau_n)}{n}\Bigr)\;\le\;\E[N_\ell]\;\le\;\frac{4+n\tau_n}{\ell},
\qquad
\Pr\bigl[|Q_q|=\ell\bigr]=\frac{\ell\,\E N_\ell}{n}\in\Bigl[\frac{2/3-o(1)}{n},\ \frac{4+o(1)}{n}\Bigr].
\]
In particular $\Pr[|Q_q|\le\ell',\ q'\notin Q_q]\ge(\tfrac23-o(1))(\ell'-1)/n$
for any second column $q'$ and $\ell'\le\sqrt n/40$: a fixed pair of
rows carries a witness of length $\le\ell'$ with probability at least
$(2/3-o(1))(\ell'-1)/n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $\ell\ne\beta$ let $X(\ell,\beta)$ be the set of $(L,\{q,q'\})$
with $q,q'$ in a common $\sigma$-cycle of length $\ell+\beta$ and
$q'=\sigma^\ell(q)$, and $J(\ell,\beta)$ the set of $(L,\{q,q'\})$ with
$q$ in an $\ell$-cycle and $q'$ in a $\beta$-cycle.  Each $(L,\{q,q'\})$
in $X(\ell,\beta)$ has a type $\lambda$ with an $(\ell+\beta)$-part and
lies in $X(n,\lambda;\ell,\beta)$; splitting that part gives the type
$\mu$ of $J(n,\mu;\ell,\beta)\subseteq J(\ell,\beta)$, and this is a
bijection between the types occurring on the two sides.  Summing the
CGW inequality over types,
$\tfrac23|X(\ell,\beta)|\le|J(\ell,\beta)|\le2|X(\ell,\beta)|$.  Now
$|X(\ell,\beta)|=\sum_L(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}(L)$: every $q$ in an
$(\ell+\beta)$-cycle gives the pair $\{q,\sigma^\ell(q)\}$, and
distinct $q$ give distinct pairs because $\{q,\sigma^\ell q\}=\{q_2,\sigma^\ell q_2\}$
with $q_2\ne q$ forces $\sigma^{2\ell}q=q$, i.e.\ $\ell=\beta$.  And
$|J(\ell,\beta)|=\sum_L\ell N_\ell(L)\,\beta N_\beta(L)$ ($q$ in an
$\ell$-cycle, $q'$ in a $\beta$-cycle; the unordered pair determines
which is which since $\ell\ne\beta$).  Dividing by the number of
squares gives \eqref{eq:cgwprod}.

Sum \eqref{eq:cgwprod} over $\beta\ge2$, $\beta\ne\ell$.  Since
$\sigma$ is a derangement, $\sum_{\beta\ge2,\beta\ne\ell}\beta N_\beta=n-\ell N_\ell$,
and $\sum_{\beta}(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}=n-W_\ell$ with
$W_\ell=\sum_{m\le\ell+1}mN_m+2\ell N_{2\ell}$ the number of columns in
cycles of length $\le\ell+1$ or exactly $2\ell$.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgwsum}
\tfrac23\,\E[n-W_\ell]\;\le\;\E\bigl[\ell N_\ell\,(n-\ell N_\ell)\bigr]\;\le\;2\,\E[n-W_\ell]\;\le\;2n .
\end{equation}
Upper bound: on $\{\ell N_\ell\le n/2\}$ the middle term is at least
$\ell N_\ell\cdot n/2$, so $\E[\ell N_\ell;\ \ell N_\ell\le n/2]\le4$ and
$\E[mN_m]\le4+n\Pr[N_m>n/(2m)]\le4+n\tau_n$ for every $m\le\sqrt n/20$,
because $N_m>n/(2m)\ge10\sqrt n$ means at least $10\sqrt n$ cycles, and
CGW's Theorem~4.9, $\Pr[\ge k\text{ cycles}]=o\bigl((3/4)^ke^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}\bigr)$,
is $e^{-\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ for $k=10\sqrt n$ ($10\log\tfrac43=2.88>\pi\sqrt{2/3}=2.57$).
By column symmetry
$\Pr[|Q_q|=m]=\E[mN_m]/n\le(4+n\tau_n)/n$ for each $m$.  Lower bound:
$\E[\ell N_\ell]\cdot n\ge\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]\ge\tfrac23(n-\E W_\ell)$
and $\E W_\ell/n=\sum_{m\le\ell+1}\Pr[|Q_q|=m]+\Pr[|Q_q|=2\ell]\le(\ell+1)(4+n\tau_n)/n$
($\ell+1$ terms, all with $m\le2\ell\le\sqrt n/20$, where the upper bound applies).
Finally $\Pr[q'\in Q_q\mid|Q_q|=m]=(m-1)/(n-1)$ by column symmetry,
which gives the last claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
(i) For a uniform derangement the two sides of \eqref{eq:cgwprod} are
\emph{exactly} equal (both count $n!\,D_{n-\ell-\beta}/((n-\ell-\beta)!\,D_n)$:
ordered pairs of an $\ell$- and a $\beta$-cycle against marked
$(\ell+\beta)$-cycles), so \eqref{eq:cgwprod} is a constant-factor
form of the cycle-count part of the CGW conjecture; type by type the
exact ratio is $2\Pr_X[A]$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:marked}.  At $n=5$ the
ratio $|J(2,3)|/|X(2,3)|$ is $2/3$ exactly.
(ii) Until now the literature had $\E N_\ell\ge c_\ell/\ell$ only with
$c_\ell$ decaying exponentially in $\ell$ (from the universal bounds of
Allsop--Morris \cite{AM26}), and $\E N_\ell\to1/\ell$ only for $\ell=2$
(Kwan--Sudakov, Kwan--Sah--Sawhney).  We have not found the constant-factor bound for all
$\ell\le\sqrt n/40$ in the literature, although it is an
immediate corollary of \cite{CGW08}; the point is that the switching
theorem compares a cycle of length $\ell+\beta$ with the \emph{pair}
of cycles $(\ell,\beta)$ in one step, so no loss accumulates along
$\ell$.
(iii) The data (Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iv)): $\Pr[|Q_q|=\ell]\approx1/n$
for all $\ell$ at $n=50,100$, inside $[0.66,4]/n$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What is still missing for the witness lemma.}  The
proposition is unconditional.  The witness lemma needs the same lower
bound (a) for pairs of rows selected by the frame, i.e.\ conditionally
on columns $j,j'$, and (b) inside $X$, i.e.\ conditionally on the type
of $\sigma$ and the mark, together with (c) the lower-tail
concentration.  For (a), CGW's framework already allows an arbitrary
prescription of full columns, so conditioning on columns $j,j'$ is of
the kind their argument tolerates; the one obstacle is their standing
assumption that the two distinguished rows carry the same symbols on
the prescribed columns (so that their row-pair permutation restricted
to the free columns is a permutation).  For a ladder pair $(x,y)$ this
fails --- the four symbols $L(x,j),L(x,j'),L(y,j),L(y,j')$ are distinct,
since an equality would force $\pi(x)=y$ or $\pi(y)=x$ --- and the free
part of rows $x,y$ consists of cycles together with two paths; the
witness is precisely ``the path starting at the column where row $y$
holds $L(x,j)$ is short and ends at the column where row $x$ holds
$L(y,j)$''.  Whether CGW's three switchings and their counting extend
to this path structure is examined in \S\ref{sec:crossspace}: they do
not, and the reason is instructive.  (c) is the second-moment version
of the same question.

\paragraph{A duality (heuristic).}  Transposing the square exchanges
the roles.  With $T(c,r)=L(r,c)$ and $\rho$ in the convention
$L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$, the frame $\pi$ of $(j,j')$ in $L$ is the
row-pair permutation $\sigma_T:=\rho^T_{j,j'}$ of rows $j,j'$ of $T$, and
$\rho_{x,y}$ is the frame of the column pair $(x,y)$ of $T$.  So the
witness event for the ladder pair $(x,y)$ --- a short
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through column $j$ avoiding $j'$ --- becomes the
event that the column pair $(x,y)$ of $T$ has rows $j,j'$ in different
frame cycles with the cycle of row $j$ short; the ladder pairs become
the column pairs $(\sigma_T^{-k}(c_1),\sigma_T^{-k}(c_2))$ of $T$, where
$c_1,c_2$ are the columns of $T$ that were rows $1,2$ of $L$; the bit
of $(L,P)$ becomes ``$c_1,c_2$ in a common $\sigma_T$-cycle''; and the
conditioning becomes: on $\sigma_T$ (over which the hypothesis is
annealed) and on the frame type of the column pair $(c_1,c_2)$ of $T$
(the type $\lambda$ of $L$).  So the conditional witness lemma is
equivalent to a \emph{lower} bound on short frame cycles of the
$\sigma_T$-shifts of a column pair, given $\sigma_T$ --- the opposite
direction to Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped}, which bounds them from
above by the splice-in switching.  The splice-in move
lengthens $C_1$ using a free cycle and is always available; the cut
that shortens it needs a flippable pair inside $C_1$.  This is the
same flippability obstruction in yet another guise; heuristically it
suggests what a proof of the witness lemma must contain --- a lower
bound on flippable pairs inside a frame cycle --- which is what CGW's
cross-switch supplies for the pair $(1,2)$ at the level of constants
(this is a reading of the structure, not a demonstrated reduction).

\subsection{The path version of CGW's lemma: where it breaks, and what survives}
\label{sec:crossspace}

Work in $\Omega(F)$, $F=$ the two full columns $p=j$, $p'=j'$, for a
pair of rows $x,y$ carrying four distinct symbols on $\{p,p'\}$ (every
ladder pair); this extends CGW's notation, whose $F$ is required to be
suitable for the distinguished rows.  Write $Q_p,Q_{p'}$ for the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycles through $p,p'$ (here $\rho_{x,y}$ is the column
permutation with $L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$, so that
$\sigma=\rho_{1,2}$ up to inversion).  \emph{Crossed}: $p'\in Q_p$,
with arcs $P_1$ (from $p$ to $p'$, $d_1-1$ interior columns) and $P_2$
(from $p'$ to $p$, $d_2-1$); \emph{parallel}: $Q_p\ne Q_{p'}$, interior
sizes $\ell_p-1$, $\ell_{p'}-1$ (these two sizes are unrelated to the
split $(\alpha,\beta)$ of the mark).  Parallel is flippability.  All
other $\rho_{x,y}$-cycles are \emph{free}, and a \emph{free column} is
a column other than $p,p'$.  ``Flip'' below means the turn of a column
cycle of a free column pair $(q,c)$ through $x$ or through $y$ (CGW's
flip and backflip alike); ``flippable'' remains the property of the
row pair.

\paragraph{Which of CGW's moves are legal.}  A flip at a free column
pair $(q,c)$ (turn of the $(q,c)$-column cycle through $y$) never
touches columns $p,p'$ and is legal in $\Omega(F)$; it replaces
$\rho_{x,y}$ by $(q\,c)\circ\rho_{x,y}$ when $(q,c)$ is an $A$-pair
(rows $x,y$ in different $(q,c)$-column cycles).  A switch (trade of
rows $x,y$ along a $\rho$-cycle containing exactly one of $q,c$) is
legal iff that cycle is free.  A cross-switch at a $B$-pair $(q,c)$ in
one cycle is legal iff the $\rho$-arc from $q$ to $c$ avoids $p,p'$.
Hence: pairs $(q,c)$ inside one free cycle, or $q$ in a free cycle and
$c$ anywhere, or both on the same path with the arc inside the path,
are fully repairable as in CGW (cases 1--3 of their splitting
procedure), and the within-path/free-cycle statistics of
Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} go through in $\Omega(F)$.  But a
\emph{cross-path} pair --- $q\in P_1$, $c\in P_2$ in the crossed state,
or $q\in Q_p\setminus p$, $c\in Q_{p'}\setminus p'$ in the parallel
state --- cannot be repaired when it is a $B$-pair: in the parallel state the
switch would trade along $Q_p$ or $Q_{p'}$ (through $p$ or $p'$), and
in the crossed state both arcs between $q$ and $c$ contain $p$ or
$p'$, so the cross-switch is illegal too.  And the cross-path pairs
are exactly the ones whose flip (at an $A$-pair) exchanges crossed and
parallel.  CGW's condition that the distinguished rows carry the same
symbols on $F$ is therefore not cosmetic: it is what makes every pair
repairable, by excluding paths.  (This paragraph settles legality with
respect to the prescribed columns only.  Preservation of rows $1,2$,
needed in $X$, is automatic for the switch, which trades rows
$x,y\notin\{1,2\}$, and requires for a flip or a cross-switch that the
rows it changes --- those of the traded column cycle or column arc ---
avoid $\{1,2\}$ or contain both of them; the latter conjugates
$\sigma$ by $(q\,c)$ and keeps type and mark.  Only the
within-free-cycle statistics of Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} go
through in $\Omega(F)$ without further work; the within-path
statistics meet boundary cases, when a neighbour pair
$\{\omega(j),\omega(j')\}$ hits $p$ or $p'$, which we have not
checked.)

\paragraph{What survives: two exact identities in the cross space.}
Identity (I) holds conditionally on the frame of $(p,p')$ and on rows
$1,2$ (it preserves both); identity (F) holds in $X$ conditionally on
the frame, and conditionally also on rows $1,2$ if legality is
restricted to column cycles avoiding rows $1,2$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(F)] The flip at a cross-path $A$-pair is an involution between
crossed and parallel squares preserving the pair and its $A$-status
(Lemma~\ref{lem:cycletrade}), legal when the $(q,c)$-column cycle
through $y$ avoids rows $1,2$ (or contains both --- then $\sigma$ is
conjugated and the mark is kept, but rows $1,2$ change).  Summing,
$\E[\#\text{legal $A$ cross-path pairs};\ \mathrm{crossed}]
=\E[\#\text{legal $A$ cross-path pairs};\ \mathrm{parallel}]$.
\item[(I)] An intercalate on rows $x,z$ ($z\notin\{1,2,x\}$;
$z\ne y$ is automatic, since $\{q,c\}$ is never a $2$-cycle of
$\rho_{x,y}$ for the pairs counted) and free columns $q,c$ is a Latin
trade whose switch replaces $\rho_{x,y}$ by $\rho_{x,y}\circ(q\,c)$
and touches nothing else that matters; it is always legal.  It
separates $p,p'$ iff crossed with $q\in P_1$, $c\in P_2$, and merges
iff parallel with $q\in Q_p\setminus p$, $c\in Q_{p'}\setminus p'$.
With $I_{\rm sep}$, $I_{\rm merge}$ the numbers of such intercalates,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:icid}
\E[I_{\rm sep};\ \mathrm{crossed}]=\E[I_{\rm merge};\ \mathrm{parallel}]
\qquad\text{exactly, in every fibre (rows $1,2$ and columns $p,p'$ fixed).}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Identity (I) has the merit that its moves are local and automatically
legal, and its weights are intercalate counts of a single row with
columns constrained to two sets.  On real squares (\texttt{s13\_icsw.py},
\texttt{runs/s13/f1/icsw50.log}: $9000$ ladder pairs at $n=50$)
$\E[I_{\rm sep}\mid\mathrm{crossed}]=0.98\cdot\E[(d_1-1)(d_2-1)/n]$ and
$\E[I_{\rm merge}\mid\mathrm{parallel}]=0.98\cdot\E[(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)/n]$,
with ratio $0.97$--$0.99$ in every bin of the product.  But (I) is a
\emph{fair} identity and yields no inequality by itself: the only
deterministic bound is $I_{\rm merge}\le(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)$ (at
most one row $z$ per column pair), and this is attained up to
constants in structured squares --- in the Cayley table of
$\mathbb Z_8\times\mathbb Z_2$ a parallel pair with
$\ell_p=\ell_{p'}=8$ has $I_{\rm merge}=12>\min(\ell_p,\ell_{p'})-1$.
A constant lower bound on flippability would follow from (i)
$\E[I_{\rm sep}\mid\mathrm{crossed},d_1,d_2]\ge\kappa(d_1-1)(d_2-1)/n$,
(ii) an arc-trapping bound (crossed squares have macroscopic arcs
except with probability $\le\Pr[\mathrm{crossed}]-c$), and (iii) the
matching upper bound
$\E[I_{\rm merge}\mid\mathrm{parallel},\ell_p,\ell_{p'}]\le
C(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)/n$: two-sided control of the two-cell pattern
``$L(z,q_0)=L(x,c_0)$ in the row $z$ holding $L(x,q_0)$ at $c_0$''
conditionally on rows $1,2$, columns $p,p'$ and the structure of rows
$x,y$.  (An earlier version of this paragraph claimed
$I_{\rm merge}\le\min(\ell_p,\ell_{p'})-1$ and deduced
$\Pr[\mathrm{parallel}]\ge\frac2n\E[I_{\rm sep};\mathrm{crossed}]$;
both are wrong, as the example shows.)

\paragraph{Why neither input is available.}  The universal bounds of
Allsop--Morris require the conditioning to be a partial square on
$\le\alpha n$ rows and $\le\beta n$ columns; two full rows or two full
columns violate this.  CGW's framework tolerates full columns but not
full rows, and its repair moves fail across the paths.  For (ii), the
splice-in switching that proves Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} needs
turns of column cycles through $y$ avoiding rows $1,2$ --- legality is
again a cycle-avoidance event --- while the intercalate version of the
splice has availability $1/n$ per position and yields only
$\Pr[\mathrm{crossed},d_1=d]\lesssim1/d$.  In every formulation the
obstruction is one and the same: in the space with two rows and two
columns prescribed there is no exact move that is both automatically
legal and able to change a cross-path status, and the probability
that such moves are available is a cycle-avoidance event of the kind
we are trying to bound.

\paragraph{The core problem, stated once.}  Let $(L,P)$ be uniform on
$X$, condition on rows $1,2$ and columns $p,p'$, and let $(x,y)$ be a
pair of rows carrying four distinct symbols on $\{p,p'\}$.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CP}
\textup{(CP)}\qquad
\Pr\bigl[p,p'\text{ lie in different cycles of }\rho_{x,y}\bigm|\text{rows }1,2,\ \text{columns }p,p'\bigr]\;\ge\;c>0 ,
\end{equation}
uniformly over the conditioning (or: for the ladder, not all pairs
crossed).  Which half of the conditioning is the obstacle is settled
by transposition (\S\ref{sec:orbit} below): with only the two
\emph{columns} prescribed, (CP) is CGW's Theorem~3.13 for the
transposed square and holds with $c=\frac13$ under CGW($3/2$)
(Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}) ($c=\frac12$ exactly, unconditionally, when
rows $1,2$ are apart in the frame); with only rows $1,2$ prescribed
(their type and the mark --- the conditioning that defines $X$) it is
the path version, which breaks for the reason above; with both it is
what (L) needs, and the frame conditioning is then free.  Everything
in \S\ref{sec:ladder}--\S\ref{sec:crossspace} is exact except this one
constant.

\paragraph{The unconditional core problem is settled with an explicit constant (under CGW($3/2$), Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}).}
For a uniform Latin square and fixed rows $1,2$ and columns $p,p'$ let
$W=\{p,p'\text{ in a common }\sigma\text{-cycle}\}$ ($\sigma$ the column
permutation of rows $1,2$) and $B=\{1,2\text{ in a common frame cycle
of }(p,p')\}$.  Three exact facts determine the joint law of $(W,B)$
up to one number.  (i) $\Pr[B]=\Pr[W]$: transposition preserves the
uniform measure and carries $W$ to the event $B$ for rows $p,p'$ and
columns $1,2$, which has the same probability as $B$ by row and
column relabelling.  (ii) $\Pr[B,\neg W]=\Pr[A,\neg W]$: on $\neg W$
the trade of rows $1,2$ along the $\sigma$-cycle through $p$ inverts
that cycle in place, so it is an involution preserving $\neg W$, and
it changes column $p$ at rows $1,2$ only, so it toggles $B$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle}, whose proof uses only
$p\in Q\not\ni p'$).  (iii) $\Pr[A\mid W,\lambda,\alpha]\ge\frac13$
for every type and every split with $\alpha,\beta\ge2$: conditionally
on the type of $\sigma$ and on the mark data, $L$ is uniform on a
fibre of $X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$ over the ordered mark, all such
fibres have the same $A$-probability by column relabelling, so this
is \eqref{eq:cgwA}; the pairs at $\sigma$-distance $1$ have $B$ surely
and total probability $\delta\le2/(n-1)$ by column symmetry.
Writing $w=\Pr[W]$: $\Pr[A,\neg W]=\Pr[B,\neg W]=\frac{1-w}2$,
$\Pr[B,W]=w-\frac{1-w}2=\frac{3w-1}2$, $\Pr[A,W]=\frac{1-w}2$, hence
$\frac{1-w}2\ge\frac13(w-\delta)$ and $\Pr[B,W]\ge0$ give
\[
\frac13\;\le\;\Pr[W]\;\le\;\frac35+\frac25\delta\;\le\;\frac35+\frac{4}{5(n-1)},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\Pr\bigl[p,p'\text{ in different cycles of }\rho_{1,2}\bigr]\;\ge\;\frac25-\frac{4}{5(n-1)},
\]
and $\E|Q_p|=1+(n-1)\Pr[W]\le(\frac35+o(1))n$ by column symmetry: the
cycle of two given rows through a given column covers at most three
fifths of the columns on average.  (The fairness conjecture is
$w=\frac12$; the data give $0.50$.  Exhaustively at $n=5$:
$w=\Pr[B]=11/14$, $\Pr[B,\neg W]=\Pr[A,\neg W]=3/28$,
$\Pr[B,W]=19/28$, and $w=\frac35+\frac25\delta$ exactly with
$\delta=13/28$, matching $\Pr_X[A]=\frac13$ at $n=5$.)  So (CP) holds
without any conditioning, with $c=2/5$, by combining the two exact
involutions with CGW's constant.  What is missing is the same
statement after conditioning on two rows and two columns.  For a
ladder pair $(x,y)$ in place of $(1,2)$: (i) fails (transposition does
not preserve the conditioning); (ii) survives the conditioning on rows
$1,2$ but not on columns $p,p'$, which the trade of rows $x,y$ alters
--- and with both prescribed the apart/together status of $(x,y)$ is
fixed by the frame, so the $2\times2$ table degenerates; (iii) is
CGW's lemma in the path version, which breaks as described.

\subsection{The orbit method: commuting column turns in $X$}
\label{sec:orbit}

This subsection records what the search for a switching proof of (CP)
produced: (a) a transposition remark that locates the obstacle
precisely; (b) an exact fairness statement for generic row pairs in
$X$; (c) a product bound for the ladder bits, which replaces the
witness lemma by a \emph{genericity} hypothesis; (d) data.  Throughout
$X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$, and ``legal'' means: preserves the
type of rows $1,2$ and the mark, i.e.\ maps $X$ to itself.

\paragraph{(a) Transposition: which half of the conditioning is the
obstacle.}  Let $L$ be uniform on \emph{all} Latin squares of order
$n$ (no condition on rows $1,2$ in this paragraph), let $x,y$ carry
four distinct symbols on $\{p,p'\}$, and condition on the two full
columns $p,p'$ (the frame $\pi$ of $(p,p')$).  In the transposed square $L^{\mathsf T}$ the
columns $p,p'$ are the distinguished rows, the frame $\pi$ is their
column permutation, and the event ``$p,p'$ lie in different cycles of
$\rho_{x,y}$'' is the event ``$\{x,y\}$ is an $A$-pair for the
distinguished rows''.  Conditioning on the two full rows $p,p'$ of
$L^{\mathsf T}$ is conditioning on their type and on the position of
the pair $\{x,y\}$ in their cycle structure, which is free by symbol
and column relabelling.  Hence, by Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle}
($|J_A|=|J_B|$, which needs no condition on the cycle lengths) and
\eqref{eq:cgwA}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CPcols}
\Pr\bigl[p,p'\text{ in different cycles of }\rho_{x,y}\bigm|\text{columns }p,p'\bigr]
\;=\;\tfrac12\ \text{ if $x,y$ are apart in the frame},\qquad
\ge\tfrac13\ \text{ if together at distances $\ge2$ (the latter under CGW($3/2$))}.
\end{equation}
So (CP) with the \emph{columns} prescribed is CGW's theorem; it is the
conditioning on rows $1,2$ --- on their type and the mark, which is
what defines $X$ and cannot be removed --- that is the path version.
In the same transposed picture the ladder trades $T_k$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} at flippable pairs are CGW's flips and
backflips at the column pairs $\{x_k,y_k\}$ of $L^{\mathsf T}$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} is exactly the legal half of CGW's
Lemma~3.12 for the distinguished rows $p,p'$: the other half --- the
switch and the cross-switch at $\{x_k,y_k\}$ --- trades the columns
$p,p'$ of $L$ along a frame cycle or frame arc through exactly one of
rows $1,2$ (apart: $C_1\ni1$, $C_1\not\ni2$; together: the arc from
$x_k$ to $y_k$ contains $1$ but not $2$, the other arc $2$ but not
$1$), so it changes the type of rows $1,2$ and leaves $X$.  The switch
is, in the original orientation, the unflip of rows $1,2$ at $(p,p')$
(which maps $X_A\to J_A$); it toggles the crossed/parallel status of
\emph{every} pair $(x,y)$ with $x$ on the side of row $1$ and $y$ on
the side of row $2$, and reverses the orientation of one side, so it
carries the ladder (a diagonal of the side-by-side array of pairs) to
a different family (an anti-diagonal).  The cross-switch likewise maps
$X_B$ to $J_B$ (it splits the $\sigma$-cycle through $p,p'$ while
keeping rows $1,2$ in one frame cycle, CGW Lemma~3.10) and toggles
the interior pairs.  Both are exact but not usable for the ladder bits
in $X$.

\paragraph{(b) Row trades are free in $X$; column turns are
conditionally free.}  For rows $z,w\notin\{1,2\}$ every trade of rows
$z,w$ along a $\rho_{z,w}$-cycle or $\rho_{z,w}$-arc changes rows
$z,w$ only, hence is legal in $X$.  In particular:

\begin{lemma}[exact fairness of column-pair status for generic rows]
\label{lem:rowfair}
Let $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ and let $(q,c)$ be a column pair lying in two
different cycles of $\rho_{x,y}$.  The CGW switch at $(q,c)$ for the
rows $x,y$ (trade of rows $x,y$ along the one of the two cycles that
contains the smaller minimum symbol) is a legal involution of $X$
that toggles whether $x,y$ lie in one column cycle of $(q,c)$, and
preserves the cycle partition of $\rho_{x,y}$ and the separation
status of every column pair with respect to $\{p,p'\}$.  Hence,
conditionally on everything determined by the other rows and by the
cycle partition of $\rho_{x,y}$,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[x,y\text{ in different column cycles of }(q,c)\bigr]=\tfrac12 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle} with $(1,2)$ replaced by $(x,y)$:
the trade swaps the entries of rows $x,y$ on the columns of the chosen
cycle $Q\ni q$, $Q\not\ni c$, so $\delta_{q,c}\mapsto\delta_{q,c}\circ(x\,y)$,
which splits or joins the column cycles through $x,y$.  It inverts $Q$
in place, so the symbol sets of the two cycles are unchanged and the
min-symbol rule re-selects $Q$: an involution.  Rows $1,2$ are
untouched, so type and mark are preserved.  Inverting $Q$ exchanges the
two arcs between $p$ and $p'$ when $Q\ni p,p'$, so separation of any
pair by $\{p,p'\}$ is preserved.  (If $Q$ contains $p$ or $p'$ the
frame changes at rows $x,y$; so frame-determined data are part of the
conditioning only when $Q$ avoids $p,p'$.)
\end{proof}

A column turn, by contrast, changes the rows of a column cycle.  Let
$(q,c)$ be a free column pair and $Z$ one of its column cycles.

\begin{lemma}[legal column turns]\label{lem:legalturn}
The turn of $Z$ (swapping the entries of columns $q,c$ in the rows of
$Z$) is a measure-preserving involution of $X$ if and only if
$|Z\cap\{1,2\}|\in\{0,2\}$; when $|Z\cap\{1,2\}|=2$ it conjugates
$\sigma$ by $(q\,c)$ and keeps the type and the mark.  It preserves the
columns $p,p'$ (hence the frame and every frame-determined family of
row pairs) and the column-cycle partition of $(q,c)$ and of every
column pair disjoint from $\{q,c\}$ (in particular of every pair of
a matching containing $(q,c)$).
For a row pair $(x,y)$ with $|Z\cap\{x,y\}|=1$ it replaces
$\rho_{x,y}$ by $\rho_{x,y}\circ(q\,c)$ (if $x\in Z$) or
$(q\,c)\circ\rho_{x,y}$ (if $y\in Z$), which toggles the
crossed/parallel status of $(x,y)$ exactly when $(q,c)$ is separated
by $\{p,p'\}$ in $\rho_{x,y}$ (different arcs when crossed, different
cycles $Q_p,Q_{p'}$ when parallel), and leaves $(q,c)$ separated
afterwards; with $|Z\cap\{x,y\}|\in\{0,2\}$ the status is unchanged.
Turns of cycles of column pairs from a fixed matching $\mathcal M$ of
the free columns pairwise commute.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A column cycle is a Latin trade (Lemma~\ref{lem:cycletrade}), so the
turn is a bijection of Latin squares and an involution, and it
preserves the cycle partition of $(q,c)$; column pairs disjoint from
$\{q,c\}$ are untouched, and so are columns $p,p'$.  Rows $1,2$ change
iff $Z$ meets them; if $Z\ni1,2$ both rows swap their entries at
$q,c$, so $\sigma'=(q\,c)\sigma(q\,c)$, whose type is that of $\sigma$
and in which $p,p'$ (fixed by $(q\,c)$) keep their distance.  If $Z$
meets exactly one of rows $1,2$, $\sigma'=\sigma\circ(q\,c)$ or
$(q\,c)\circ\sigma$, which changes the type.  The effect on
$\rho_{x,y}$ is the computation of \S\ref{sec:crossspace}; separation
is preserved by the arc computation there.  Turns of cycles of
disjoint column pairs act on disjoint sets of cells; two cycles of the
same pair have disjoint row sets.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{(c) The product bound.}  Fix a matching $\mathcal M$ of the
free columns, independent of the square, and a frame-determined family
$\mathcal G$ of row pairs $(x,y)$, $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$, whose
$x$-rows are pairwise distinct and disjoint from its $y$-rows (a block
of the ladder, where the pairs are even disjoint; or a union of
diagonals $D_c$ with distinct $x$-rows, which may share $y$-rows).
For $(x,y)\in\mathcal G$ call a column pair $(q,c)\in\mathcal M$ a
\emph{clean coordinate} of $(x,y)$ if the column cycle $Z$ of $(q,c)$
through $x$ has length at most $\ell_1$ (a fixed constant), satisfies
$|Z\cap\{1,2\}|\in\{0,2\}$, and contains no row of $\mathcal G$
other than $x$ (no other $x$-row and no $y$-row, in particular not
$y$).  Let $\mathcal T_{x,y}$ be the set of clean coordinates of
$(x,y)$ and $\eta_{x,y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal T_{x,y}}$.  By
Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn} the turns of all clean coordinates of all
pairs of $\mathcal G$ commute, generate a group acting freely on $X$,
preserve $\mathcal G$ and the sets $\mathcal T_{x,y}$, and
\[
\rho_{x,y}(\eta)\;=\;\rho_{x,y}(0)\circ\!\!\prod_{(q,c)\in\mathcal T_{x,y}:\ \eta_{x,y}(q,c)=1}\!\!(q\,c),
\]
the bit $b_{x,y}(\eta)=[\,(x,y)\text{ parallel}\,]$ depending on
$\eta_{x,y}$ only.  Let the \emph{orbit weight} of $(x,y)$ be
\[
\bar y_{x,y}\;=\;\E_{\eta_{x,y}}\Bigl[\frac{\#\{(q,c)\in\mathcal T_{x,y}:\ (q,c)\text{ separated by }\{p,p'\}\text{ in }\rho_{x,y}(\eta)\}}{|\mathcal T_{x,y}|}\Bigr]
\]
($0$ if $\mathcal T_{x,y}=\emptyset$), a function of the orbit, and
$Y_{\mathcal G}=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal G}\bar y_{x,y}$.

\begin{proposition}[product bound]\label{prop:orbit}
Conditionally on the orbit, the bits $b_{x,y}$, $(x,y)\in\mathcal G$,
are independent, and
\[
\Pr\bigl[(x,y)\text{ crossed}\bigm|\text{orbit}\bigr]\le1-\tfrac12\bar y_{x,y},
\qquad
\Pr\bigl[\text{no pair of }\mathcal G\text{ is flippable}\bigm|\text{orbit}\bigr]
\le\prod_{(x,y)\in\mathcal G}\bigl(1-\tfrac12\bar y_{x,y}\bigr)\le e^{-Y_{\mathcal G}/2}.
\]
Consequently $\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}_{\mathcal G}=\emptyset]\le\E_X[e^{-Y_{\mathcal G}/2}]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The action is free: distinct column pairs act on disjoint cells, and
two clean coordinates of different pairs at the same $(q,c)$ are the
cycles through two distinct $x$-rows, each avoiding the other, hence
different cycles; the sets $\mathcal T_{x,y}$ are orbit-invariant by
Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn} (row sets of the cycles, their meeting
$\{1,2\}$, and the frame-determined family are preserved).  The
uniform measure on $X$ restricted to an orbit is therefore uniform on
the orbit, which is the product of the cubes
$\{0,1\}^{\mathcal T_{x,y}}$.  A clean coordinate of $(x,y)$ contains
$x$ and not $y$, and a clean coordinate of another pair contains
neither $x$ nor $y$ (it avoids all $x$-rows but its own and all
$y$-rows), so $b_{x,y}$ depends on $\eta_{x,y}$ alone, whence the
independence.  Fix $(x,y)$ and
$Z=(q,c)\in\mathcal T_{x,y}$.  Whether $Z$ is separated in
$\rho_{x,y}(\eta)$ does not depend on $\eta_Z$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}: the turn of $Z$ leaves $Z$ separated), and
when it is, the two values of $\eta_Z$ give opposite bits.  Hence
$\Pr[b_{x,y}=0]\le1-\tfrac12\Pr_\eta[Z\text{ separated}]$ for every
$Z$, and averaging the right side over $Z\in\mathcal T_{x,y}$ gives
the first bound; the second is the product of the first over the
independent bits.
\end{proof}

\begin{hypothesis}[orbit genericity]\label{hyp:orbit}
With $\ell_0=n/\log^2n$: (i) for the sub-ladder $I$ of the first
$\min(K_0-1,\lceil n/8\rceil)$ ladder pairs,
$\Pr[K_0\ge\ell_0,\ Y_I<\kappa|I|]=o(1/\log n)$; (ii) for a
$B$-instance with $d_{12}=\ell\le n/4$ and $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$, with
$S=\{2\ell t:\ 0\le t\le(n/2-\ell)/(2\ell)\}$ (offsets at gaps
$2\ell$, so that the $x$-rows $\pi^{-(c+i)}(1)$, $c\in S$,
$1\le i<\ell$, are pairwise distinct, $|S|\ge n/(4\ell)$, and the
diagonals $D_c$, $c\in S$, involve at most $n/4+\ell$ rows), and
cleanliness taken with respect to all rows of
$\bigcup_{c\in S}D_c$: the event that fewer than half of the $D_c$,
$c\in S$, have $Y_{D_c}\ge\kappa(\ell-1)$ has probability
$o(1/\log n)$ (as a single event over all $\ell$).
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:Lorbit}
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit} implies \textup{(L)}, hence the weak CGW
conjecture.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Follow the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:Lwitness}.  Long ladders:
by Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit}, on $\{K_0\ge\ell_0\}$ and off the
exceptional event of (i),
$\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid\text{orbit}]\le e^{-\kappa|I|/2}\le
e^{-\kappa\ell_0/4}=o(1/\log n)$.  Short arcs: fix $\ell$ and apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit} to the family
$\bigcup_{c\in S}D_c$: its $x$-rows $\pi^{-(c+i)}(1)$ lie in the
disjoint intervals of depths $[c+1,c+\ell-1]$, $c\in S$, so they are
pairwise distinct, and they lie on the frame arc from $2$ to $1$ while
the $y$-rows $\pi^{-i}(2)$ lie on the arc from $1$ to $2$, so the two
sets are disjoint.  Given the orbit the bits of all pairs are
independent, hence so are the diagonals' flippabilities, and off the
exceptional event at least $|S|/2$ of the diagonals are flippable with
conditional probability $\ge1-e^{-\kappa(\ell-1)/2}\ge4\theta_0$,
$\theta_0:=\tfrac14(1-e^{-\kappa/2})$.  The number of flippable
offsets in $S$ then has conditional mean $\ge2\theta_0|S|$, and by
Chernoff the fraction $\theta_S$ is $\ge\theta_0$ except with
conditional probability $\le e^{-\theta_0|S|/4}\le
e^{-\theta_0\log^2n/16}$ (as $|S|\ge n/(4\ell_0)=\log^2n/4$),
summable over $\ell<\ell_0$ to $o(1/n)$.  The identity behind \eqref{eq:offsetavg}
holds for the average over $c\in S$ as well as over all $c$, so
$\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,\theta_S\ge\theta_0]
\le36\ell_0/((n-2)\theta_0)=O(1/\log^2n)$; the parts $|C_1|<n/2+\ell$
and $K_0<\ell_0$ on $A$ are as before.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What the hypothesis says.}  It replaces the witness
lemma (a lower-tail statement about rare short cycles, probability
$\ell'/n$ per pair) by a statement about a quantity that is
typically of constant size: $\bar y_{x,y}$ is the average, over the
cube of a few column turns that fix rows $y$ and $1,2$ and the whole
family, of the fraction of those turns' column pairs that are
separated by $\{p,p'\}$ in the row pair $(x,y)$.  It is a
\emph{genericity} statement --- the short column cycles of the
matching pairs through $x$ are not aligned with the arcs of
$\rho_{x,y}$ at $p,p'$ --- together with concentration of the sum over
the family; concentration of the \emph{flippabilities} themselves is
no longer required, since Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit} makes them
conditionally independent.  It still contains (CP)-type inputs: a
positive orbit weight needs the cycles of $\rho_{x,y}$ through $p,p'$
to be macroscopic (the arc-trapping input (ii) of
\S\ref{sec:crossspace}), and the genericity itself.  The pure
permutation core is: for a permutation $\rho_0$ and a matching
$\mathcal T$ of transpositions on points other than $p,p'$, with
$\tau(\eta)$ the product of a uniformly random subset of
$\mathcal T$, bound $\Pr_\eta[p,p'\text{ separated in }\rho_0\tau(\eta)]$
from below.  For an adversarial $\mathcal T$ (e.g.\ all pairs at
$\rho_0$-distance $2$, none straddling $p$ or $p'$) the probability is
$0$, so the content is that the clean coordinates of a random square
are not adversarial; and the
moves that re-randomise the clean coordinates without touching
$\rho_{x,y}$ --- the free row trades of Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair} --- have
availabilities that are again same-cycle events of generic row pairs.
This is the recursion noted in \S\ref{sec:crossspace}: every
availability is a cycle-avoidance event, and the exact identities in
$X$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair}, (F), (I), Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit})
are all fair.  What Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit} adds is that the
fairness can be \emph{compounded} over a family: a product of
conditionally independent coins, so that a constant per-pair
genericity gives exponential decay in the family size.

\paragraph{(e) The arc-trapping input $(\alpha)$: CGW's lemma for a
generic row pair in $X$, and the blocked fraction.}  The orbit weight
$\bar y_{x,y}$ vanishes when the $\rho_{x,y}$-cycles through $p,p'$
are short, so Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit} needs
$\Pr_X[|Q_p(\rho_{x,y})|\le\varepsilon n]\le C\varepsilon$ for the
ladder pairs --- CGW's short-cycle bound for the row pair $(x,y)$
inside $X$.  Run CGW's splitting/joining multigraph for the
distinguished rows $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ inside $X$.  Switches and
cross-switches of rows $x,y$ are free (they change rows $x,y$ only),
so the repairs of their splitting and joining procedures are always
available; the flip and backflip at a column pair $(q,c)$ turn the
$(q,c)$-column cycle through $x$ or through $y$ and are legal iff
that cycle meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$ rows
(Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}).  Since either cycle may be used, the
flip at $(q,c)$ is \emph{blocked} exactly when rows $1,2$ and rows
$x,y$ are \emph{interleaved} in the column-cycle structure of $(q,c)$:
in the $A$-state ($x,y$ in different cycles $Z_x,Z_y$) one of $1,2$
lies in $Z_x$ and the other in $Z_y$; in the $B$-state the four rows
alternate $x,1,y,2$ on the common cycle.  Interleaving is invariant
under the switch of rows $x,y$ (which maps $A$-crosswise to $B$-cross
and back, Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair}) and under the switch of rows $1,2$
at a column pair in different $\sigma$-cycles (same computation with
the roles exchanged; this move preserves $X$ up to $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$).
Legality is a property of the bitrade, so CGW's edge correspondence
survives the removal of the blocked edges, and the $\mu$-side flip
edges alone give, for the cycle through the mark column
($N_\lambda(p)=\ell$ splitting pairs leave $p$ in the $\ell$-piece):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jp}
\E_X\bigl[\#\{(q,c):\ q\in Q_p,\ c\notin Q_p,\ \text{not interleaved}\};\ |Q_p|=\ell\bigr]
\;\le\;2\ell\,\Pr_X\bigl[|Q_p|\ge\ell+2\bigr]\;\le\;2\ell .
\end{equation}
Hence, with $b_\ell$ the conditional expectation given $|Q_p|=\ell$ of
the fraction of interleaved pairs among the $\ell(n-\ell)$ joining
pairs,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[|Q_p(\rho_{x,y})|=\ell\bigr]\;\le\;\frac{2}{(1-b_\ell)(n-\ell)} ,
\]
i.e.\ CGW's bound $\le 4/n$ holds in $X$ up to the factor
$1/(1-b_\ell)$: $(\alpha)$ \emph{follows from} (BL): $b_\ell\le1-c$.
Data (\texttt{s13\_interleave.py}, \texttt{runs/s13/orbit/interleave50.log},
$300$ ladder pairs at $n=50$, $1.8\cdot10^5$ column pairs):
$\Pr[\text{interleaved}]=0.186$ over all pairs in different
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycles, $0.1867$ in the $A$-state and $0.1861$ in the
$B$-state (the switch invariance), $0.188$ on joining pairs of $Q_p$
and $0.183$ off them, $0.193$/$0.181$ according as $q,c$ lie in
different/the same $\sigma$-cycles; and the lengths $|Q_p|$ are
spread uniformly ($3,7,5,7,5,9,5,5,3$ instances at
$\ell=2,\dots,10$ against $6$ expected per length).  So the blocked
fraction is $\approx0.19$ and the loss is a factor $1.23$.

What (BL) needs is again a same-cycle statement for generic row pairs:
the interleaving at $(q,c)$ is changed only by trades of third rows
$z,w\notin\{1,2,x,y\}$ along $\rho_{z,w}$-cycles through exactly one
of $q,c$ ($\delta_{q,c}\mapsto\delta_{q,c}\circ(z\,w)$), which
de-interleave when $z,w$ lie on adjacent arcs between the special
rows (in the $B$-cross state) or separate $x$ from the row of
$\{1,2\}$ in its cycle (in the $A$-crosswise state); the
cross-switch of $z,w$ reverses a $\delta_{q,c}$-arc and is either
illegal (the arc contains exactly one of $1,2$), harmful (it contains
exactly one of $x,y$), or useless (it contains no special row).  A
double count of these involutions gives
$\Pr[\text{interleaved}]\le1/(1+\kappa)$ as soon as, for an
interleaved square, a $\kappa$-fraction of the row pairs $(z,w)$ in
the de-interleaving positions have $q,c$ in different
$\rho_{z,w}$-cycles --- the generic same-cycle event of
\S\ref{sec:crossspace} once more, now for the third rows at the
prescribed column pair.  This is the explicit form of the recursion:
each level's availability is the next level's genericity, and no
level is settled by an exact identity.  For the ladder pairs there is
the further point that $(x,y)$ is selected by the frame, so
$(\alpha)$ is needed conditionally on the frame, while \eqref{eq:Jp}
is stated for fixed rows $x,y$ with the frame free.  The same
blocked-edge multigraph can be run with columns $p,p'$ fixed if every
move avoids them: the flips at free column pairs do; the switch of
rows $x,y$ used to repair a $B$-pair $(q,c)$ trades along the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle selected by the min-symbol rule, which is
unavailable when that cycle is $Q_p$ or $Q_{p'}$ (one may then trade
along the other cycle, if it avoids $p,p'$, at the cost of the
involution property); and CGW's cross-switch repairs trade along
$\rho_{x,y}$-arcs that may pass through $p$ or $p'$.  This enlarges
the blocked set by a second cycle-avoidance event; we have not carried
the bookkeeping through.

\paragraph{(f) The permutation problem $(\Pi)$, and the one statement
everything reduces to.}  Fix a permutation $\rho_0$ of the columns,
two columns $p\ne p'$, and a set $\mathcal T$ of pairwise disjoint
transpositions avoiding $p,p'$; for $\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal T}$ let
$\tau(\eta)$ be the product of the transpositions with $\eta_Z=1$.
Call $Z=(q,c)$ \emph{crossing} in a permutation $\rho$ (``separated''
in \S(c) and Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}) if $\{q,c\}$ and
$\{p,p'\}$ are interleaved in the cycle structure of $\rho$: $q,c$ on
different arcs of a common cycle through $p,p'$, or $q$ in the cycle
of $p$ and $c$ in the (different) cycle of $p'$, or vice versa.  By
Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}, $\rho\mapsto\rho\circ Z$ toggles the
separation of $p,p'$ exactly when $Z$ is crossing in $\rho$, and $Z$
is crossing in $\rho\circ Z$ iff it is crossing in $\rho$.  Hence, for
$\eta$ uniform and $b(\eta)=[p,p'\text{ separated in }\rho_0\tau(\eta)]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pi}
\Pr_\eta[b=1]\;\ge\;\tfrac12\max_{Z\in\mathcal T}\Pr_\eta\bigl[Z\text{ crossing in }\rho_0\tau(\eta)\bigr]
\;\ge\;\tfrac12\,\E_\eta\Bigl[\frac{\#\{Z\text{ crossing in }\rho_0\tau(\eta)\}}{|\mathcal T|}\Bigr],
\end{equation}
and $b\equiv0$ on the cube iff no $Z$ is crossing in any
$\rho_0\tau(\eta)$.  Let $\rho_0$ be crossed, with arcs $P_1$ (from
$p$ to $p'$) and $P_2$, and form the graph whose vertices are $P_1$,
$P_2$ and the other cycles of $\rho_0$ and whose edges are the
transpositions of $\mathcal T$ joining different vertices.  Then
$b\equiv0$ iff $P_1$ and $P_2$ lie in different components.  If they
are connected by a path $P_1=V_0,V_1,\dots,V_m=P_2$ with edges
$Z_1,\dots,Z_m$, apply $Z_1,\dots,Z_{m-1}$ in order: $Z_i$, with one
end on the arc from $p$ to $p'$ and the other in $V_i$, inserts $V_i$
into that arc ($\rho\circ(q\,c)$ sends $q\mapsto\rho(c)$, runs around
$V_i$, and returns to $\rho(q)$), $P_2$ is untouched, and $Z_m$ then
joins the arc to $P_2$ and is crossing.  If they are disconnected, let
$K_1$ be the union of the vertices in the component of $P_1$ and $K_2$
that of $P_2$: every transposition of $\mathcal T$ has both ends in
$K_1$, both in $K_2$ or both outside, so $\tau(\eta)$ preserves $K_1$,
while $\rho_0(K_1)=(K_1\setminus\{p\})\cup\{p'\}$; the
$\rho_0\tau(\eta)$-orbit of $p$ therefore stays in $K_1$ until it
reaches $p'$, and it cannot close at $p$ before that, since
$\rho_0\tau(x)=p$ forces $\tau(x)=\rho_0^{-1}(p)\in P_2\subseteq K_2$.
In particular a single crossing $Z$ at $\eta=0$ gives
$\Pr_\eta[b=1]\ge\tfrac12$ on the orbit of $Z$ alone, and the
adversarial examples of \S(c) are instances of the disconnected case
(the distance-$2$ chords straddling $p$ or $p'$ join $P_1$ to $P_2$
and must be excluded there).  One may equally use one coordinate per
pair: with $Z_k$ the first clean coordinate of the $k$-th pair in a
fixed order of the matching (orbit-invariant, since cleanliness of
every matching pair is), the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit}
applied to the sub-family $\{Z_k\}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:onecoord}
\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}_I=\emptyset]\;\le\;\E_X\Bigl[\prod_{k\in I}\bigl(1-\tfrac12X_k\bigr)\Bigr],
\qquad X_k=[\,Z_k\text{ crossing in }\rho_{x_k,y_k}\,],
\end{equation}
and $(\beta)$ becomes: $\Pr_X[X_k=1]\ge c$ for most $k$, i.e.\ the
column pair selected by the column-cycle structure through $x_k$ is
interleaved with $\{p,p'\}$ in the row-cycle structure of $(x_k,y_k)$
with probability bounded below.

Every input of Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit} is now of one form.  Call two
pairs of parallel lines \emph{interleaved in} a third pair of lines
if they are interleaved in its cycle structure, as above.  Then
$(\beta)$ asks that the column pairs $\{p,p'\}$ (the mark) and
$\{q,c\}$ (a clean coordinate) be interleaved in the row pair
$(x_k,y_k)$ with probability $\ge c$; (BL), hence $(\alpha)$, asks
that the row pairs $\{1,2\}$ and $\{x,y\}$ be \emph{not} interleaved in
a joining column pair $(q,c)$ with probability $\ge c$; and the
availability of the third-row switch repairs of \S(e) is the event
that $q,c$ lie in different $\rho_{z,w}$-cycles --- with the frame
fixed, that in addition $\{p,p'\}$ and $\{q,c\}$ are not interleaved
in $(z,w)$.  So the residual is the two-sided statement
\[
\textup{(U)}\qquad c\;\le\;\Pr\bigl[\text{two given pairs of lines are interleaved in a third pair}\bigr]\;\le\;1-c
\]
in $X$ (type and mark of rows $1,2$ prescribed; the other lines
generic or selected by cycle structure), together with the two-point
same-cycle event for generic rows at the prescribed columns named in
\S(e); and the exact tools of $X$
--- the free row trades of Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair}, which preserve
interleaving of $\{1,2\}$ with the traded pair (\S(e)), and the column
turns of Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}, which are legal exactly off the
interleaved configurations --- are either blind to (U) or need (U).
Without any conditioning, (U) for four generic columns in a generic
row pair holds with explicit constants and without CGW($3/2$): the law
of $\rho_{x,y}$ is conjugation-invariant, so given its cycle type the
four columns are uniform distinct points, and the interleaving
probability is $\tfrac13A+2B$ with $A=\sum_i(m_i)_4/(n)_4$,
$B=\sum_{i\ne j}(m_i)_2(m_j)_2/(n)_4$ over the cycle lengths $m_i$;
this is at most $\tfrac13+O(1/n)$ for every type (equality at the
$n$-cycle) and at least $\tfrac13\Pr[W\mid\text{type}]^3-O(1/n)$
(Jensen: $\sum_iw_i^4\ge(\sum_iw_i^2)^3$ for $w_i=m_i/n$), hence
$\ge\tfrac1{81}-O(1/n)$ \emph{on average over the type} by Jensen
again and $\Pr[W]\ge\tfrac13$, which uses only the involutions (i),
(ii) of \S\ref{sec:crossspace}.  Per type the lower bound fails
(e.g.\ $(2^{n/2})$ gives $O(n^{-2})$): the lower half of (U) needs
macroscopic cycles.  The data ($0.19$ in every version we measured,
\S(d), \S(e)) sit inside this range.
(As an aside, the surviving direction alone does not give even a weak
form of CGW's Lemma~4.4, upper half, $\Pr[\sigma\text{ is an $n$-cycle}]\le1-c$;
it does follow from the intercalate count $\ge(1-o(1))n^2/4$ of
Kwan--Sudakov \cite{KS18}: by row symmetry $\E N_2\ge\tfrac12-o(1)$,
and $\E[N_2(N_2-1)]\le2\E N_4\le n/(n-4)$ from the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive}(i), so $\Pr[N_2\ge1]\ge\tfrac18-o(1)$.)

\paragraph{(g) (U) in its simplest conditional instances.}  Three
observations settle what (U) is and is not, in the instance ``generic
row pair $(x,y)$, the mark columns $p,p'$, two further columns $q,c$,
conditioning only on $X$''.

\emph{Generic columns reduce (U) to $(\alpha)$.}  For $(q,c)$ uniform
among the free column pairs, $\{p,p'\},\{q,c\}$ are interleaved in
$\rho_{x,y}$ for exactly $(d_1-1)(d_2-1)$ pairs (crossed) or
$(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)$ pairs (parallel) out of $\binom{n-2}2$.  So
the lower bound in (U) for generic $q,c$ is precisely the statement
that \emph{both} arcs (crossed) or \emph{both} cycles (parallel) of
$\rho_{x,y}$ at $p,p'$ are macroscopic with probability bounded below
--- the two-arc form (ii) of the arc-trapping input in
\S\ref{sec:crossspace}, nothing softer (the single-cycle bound
$\Pr_X[|Q_p|\le\varepsilon n]\le C\varepsilon$ of \S(e) does not
control the position of $p'$ on $Q_p$).  The conjugation-invariance computation of \S(f) is
the unconditional case of this, where the law of $(d_1,d_2)$ given the
cycle type is explicit.

\emph{Generic rows make (BL) trivial.}  Dually, for a column pair
$(q,c)$ and the rows $1,2$, the number of row pairs $\{x,y\}$ interleaved
with $\{1,2\}$ in $\delta_{q,c}$ is $(a_1-1)(a_2-1)$ or $(m_1-1)(m_2-1)$
(arcs between $1,2$ on their common $(q,c)$-column cycle, or the two
cycle lengths), which is at most $(n-2)^2/4<\tfrac12\binom{n-2}2(1+O(1/n))$
\emph{deterministically}.  Since rows other than $1,2$ are exchangeable
in $X$, for a fixed pair of rows $x,y$ and every column pair,
$\Pr_X[\{1,2\},\{x,y\}\text{ interleaved in }\delta_{q,c}]\le\tfrac12+O(1/n)$.
What \eqref{eq:Jp} needs is this bound conditionally on
$|Q_p(\rho_{x,y})|=\ell$ and $q\in Q_p$, $c\notin Q_p$, i.e.\ for the
row pair selected by its own row-cycle structure at $(q,c)$, and for
a ladder pair, selected by the frame; exchangeability is lost exactly
there.  The content of (BL) is thus a decorrelation between the
row-cycle structure of $(x,y)$ at $q,c,p$ and its column-cycle
position relative to rows $1,2$ --- not a bound on interleaving as
such, which is free.

\emph{Direction.}  Every lower bound on a ``different cycles'' or
``different arcs'' event that is not conditioned on the availability
of its own toggle --- $(\beta)$, the availability $q,c$ in different
$\rho_{z,w}$-cycles of the third-row repairs, CGW's
$\Pr_X[A]\ge\tfrac13$ --- is of the type of the gap direction of
CGW's lemma (the exact toggles of Lemmas~\ref{lem:switchtoggle} and
\ref{lem:rowfair} give $\tfrac12$ only conditionally on the dual pair
being separated): it requires a canonical, bounded-multiplicity repair of
the configurations in which the two points lie on one cycle, which is
what CGW's case~3 fails to provide.  Upper bounds on such events
(``merging is easy'') are the surviving direction and are available
(Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive}).  Third rows do provide repairs
in $X$: for a crossed ladder pair $(x_k,y_k)$ of a $B$-instance the
cross-switch of rows $x_k,y_k$ at $(p,p')$ is always defined ($(p,p')$
is a $B$-pair for these rows, and $d_1,d_2\ge2$ because
$\rho_{x_k,y_k}(p)=p'$ would mean $\pi(1)=2$); its column part acts on
a frame arc between $x_k$ and $y_k$, which contains exactly one of rows
$1,2$, so that row changes at $p,p'$ and the result lies in $J_B$
(with $(d_{12},d_{21})$ replaced by $(2k,|C|-2k)$); for an
$A$-instance the backflip at $(p,p')$ through $C_2$ does the same into
$J_A$.  So every ladder
pair of every instance of $X\cup J$ carries one move, the $j'$-trade
when flippable and a type-changing move when crossed; but the
type-changing moves reverse a frame arc or a frame cycle and carry
the ladder to a different family (\S(a)), so the edge count does not
close into an identity, and their inverses from the $J$ side (the
ladder being defined on $J$ by the same formula) are in general not
indexed by a ladder pair of the target --- only when the reversed arc
or cycle has length exactly $2k$.  The same multiplicity
problem defeats the third-row repair of CGW's case~3 (trade or
cross-switch rows $z,w$ on different frame arcs between $1$ and $2$):
every such pair gives an edge into $X_A\cup J$, but a target $A$-instance
receives one edge from every available pair in $C'_1\times C'_2$, and
since the trade is an involution the trade edges give the exact
identity $\E_X[\#\text{available }(z,w);B]=\E_X[\#\text{available }(z,w)\in C'_1\times C'_2;A]$,
with product weights $(d_{12}-1)(d_{21}-1)$ and $(|C_1|-1)(|C_2|-1)$
times availability fractions on the two sides, and the cross-switch
edges into $J_B$ have the same shape: fair again, in the sense of
identity (I).  We record this as the precise reason that neither CGW's
upper bound nor $(\beta)$ yields to switchings in $X$: the moves that
toggle a same-cycle bit without touching the prescribed lines exist,
but they are indexed by a free third pair of rows and so have
unbounded multiplicity.

\paragraph{(h) Two disjoint row pairs: the conditioning problem in its
cleanest form.}  Three observations from the refereeing of the
conditional paper.

\emph{(i) The column-free part of $(\alpha)$ for generic rows is
unconditional, with constants.}
\begin{proposition}[a generic row pair has a macroscopic cycle]\label{prop:genericlong}
Let $x\ne y$ be two rows of a uniform Latin square of order $n$ and
$0<\varepsilon<1/3$.  Then
$\Pr[\text{every cycle of }\rho_{x,y}\text{ has length}\le\varepsilon n]
\le2\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)$; in particular $\rho_{x,y}$ has a
cycle of length $>n/5$ with probability at least $\tfrac12$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By row symmetry take $(x,y)=(1,2)$.  Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive}(i)
gives $\E[\ell N_\ell]\le2n/(n-\ell)$ for $\ell<n$, so the expected
number of columns on cycles of length $\le\varepsilon n$ is
$\sum_{\ell\le\varepsilon n}\E[\ell N_\ell]\le\varepsilon n\cdot2n/(n-\varepsilon n)
=2\varepsilon n/(1-\varepsilon)$; the event in question is that this
number equals $n$, and Markov's inequality gives the bound.
\end{proof}
\noindent Together with the Jensen bound in \S(f)
($\tfrac13A+2B\ge\tfrac13\Pr[W\mid\text{type}]^3-O(1/n)$ and
$\Pr[W\mid\text{type}]\ge\varepsilon^2-O(1/n)$ on the event of a cycle
of length $>\varepsilon n$), the unconditional (U) for generic rows and
generic columns now has an explicit constant:
$\Pr[\{q,c\},\{q',c'\}\text{ interleaved in }\rho_{x,y}]\ge\tfrac16(1/5)^6-O(1/n)$
for four generic columns.  The constant is poor but the statement is
unconditional and uses only $|J|=2|X_A|$.  (The same Markov count
gives the version through a \emph{generic} column: the cycle of
$\rho_{x,y}$ through a uniformly random column has length
$>\varepsilon n$ with probability $\ge1-2\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)$;
$(\alpha)$ proper asks this for the mark column $p$, whose position
relative to $\rho_{1,2}$ is prescribed, and that is part of the
difficulty.)  So the residual difficulty is the conditioning on the
type of rows $1,2$, the position of the mark, and the frame selection
of the ladder rows, and its column-free first case is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tworows}
\Pr\bigl[\rho_{x,y}\text{ has a cycle of length}>\varepsilon n\bigm|\operatorname{type}(\rho_{1,2})=\lambda\bigr]\ge c
\quad\text{uniformly in }\lambda\ (\kappa(\lambda)\le K\log n),
\end{equation}
for rows $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ --- a decorrelation of the cycle types of
two disjoint row pairs.

\emph{(ii) Data.}  \texttt{s13\_rowpairs.py}
(\texttt{runs/s13/rowpairs/rowpairs\{30,50,100\}.log}; $2000$, $1500$,
$400$ squares, every square contributing the pair $(1,2)$ and the
$(n-2)/2$ disjoint pairs $(3,4),(5,6),\dots$) sees no correlation at
all: with $\mathrm{LONG}=[\text{longest cycle}>n/2]$,
$\Pr[\mathrm{LONG}(x,y)]=0.7075\pm0.003$ ($n=30$), $0.7017\pm0.002$
($n=50$), $0.695\pm0.003$ ($n=100$) unconditionally, and $0.705$,
$0.711$, $0.701$, $0.712$ given $\kappa(1,2)=1,2,3,\ge4$ ($n=30$;
$0.698,0.702,0.701,0.703$ at $n=50$; $0.717\pm0.016,0.697,0.686,0.697$
at $n=100$), $0.708$, $0.699$, $0.691$ given $\neg\mathrm{LONG}(1,2)$,
$0.697$, $0.702$, $0.686\pm0.014$ given $L_{\max}(1,2)\le n/3$,
$0.708$, $0.701$, $0.703$ given $L_{\max}(1,2)\ge0.9n$, with no
dependence on $N_2(1,2)$ either; the correlations of $\kappa$,
$L_{\max}$, $\mathrm{LONG}$ and $N_2$ across the two pairs are all
$|r|<0.008$ (naive s.e.\ $0.005$--$0.007$), and
the conditional law of $\kappa(x,y)$ given $\kappa(1,2)=k$ is the same
row for every $k$.  Numerically \eqref{eq:tworows} holds with $c$ equal
to the unconditional value for the classes of $\lambda$ that the
samples populate; the ``all cycles short'' classes
($L_{\max}(1,2)\le n/4$: $4$ resp.\ $9$ squares) are too rare to be
tested this way, so the data speak to typical $\lambda$, not to
uniformity.

\emph{(iii) What the marked-pair identity gives with rows $1,2$
fixed, and where it stalls.}  Fix rows $1,2$ (or only their type
$\lambda$) and run the marked-pair machinery for the pair $(x,y)$ with
a mark $\{q,q'\}$ on a cycle of $\rho_{x,y}$.  The switch (trade of
rows $x,y$ along a row cycle) is always legal.  The flip (turn of the
column cycle of $(q,q')$ through row $y$) is legal iff that cycle meets
$\{1,2\}$ in $0$ rows (rows $1,2$ fixed) or in $0$ or $2$ rows (type
only, by Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}); call such marked instances
\emph{legal}.  The flip bijects legal $A$-instances of the split space
onto legal $A$-instances of the joined space (the row set of the
turned cycle is preserved), and the switch bijects $J'_A$ onto
$J'_B$; so the exact statement with rows $1,2$ prescribed is
$|X'_{A,\mathrm{leg}}|=|J'_{A,\mathrm{leg}}|$ and $|J'_A|=|J'_B|$,
and nothing is known about $X'_{A}\setminus\mathrm{leg}$ or $X'_B$.
The legal restriction is not innocuous: on uniform squares
(\texttt{s13\_cleanid.py}, $n=30$, rows $(3,4)$, $238634$ marked
instances, legality in the rows-fixed sense) $\Pr[A]=0.499$ and
$\Pr[\mathrm{leg}]=0.299$ but $\Pr[\mathrm{leg}\mid A]=0.466$ against
$\Pr[\mathrm{leg}\mid B]=0.133$ (a cycle through $y$ avoiding $x$ is
shorter), so $|X'_{B,\mathrm{leg}}|/|X'_{A,\mathrm{leg}}|=0.287$, not
$\approx1$; the identity on the legal part does not determine the type
ratio.  (The numbers are identical given $\mathrm{LONG}(1,2)$ or not,
consistently with (ii).)  The same obstruction appears if one tries to
reprove Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive} inside
$X_\lambda=\{\operatorname{type}(\rho_{1,2})=\lambda\}$ for the free
pair $(x,y)$: the $k$-fold joining works verbatim on the legal joining
pairs, so one needs a constant fraction of the column pairs
$(q,q')$ of a square in $X_\lambda$ to have the column cycle through
$y$ meeting $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$ rows; for a column pair chosen
independently of $(x,y)$, exchangeability of the rows other than $1,2$
turns this into the statement that the column cycles of $(q,q')$
through rows $1$ and $2$, when distinct, do not cover almost all rows
--- a ``huge frame cycle is not certain'' statement for a generic
column pair (the joining pairs of $\rho_{x,y}$ are not independent of
$(x,y)$, so even this is a simplification, as for (BL) in \S(e)).  Its
natural proof is a splice-\emph{out}: a trade of a free row pair
$(z,w)$, $z,w$ in the big cycle, along a $\rho_{z,w}$-cycle through
$q$ but not $q'$ splits the cycle
($\delta_{q,q'}\mapsto\delta_{q,q'}\circ(z\,w)$) and is always legal
in $X_\lambda$; it is an involution for each fixed $(z,w)$, so what it
yields is the fair identity
$\E[\#\text{available }(z,w)\subset Z;\ Z\text{ big}]=\E[\#\text{available }(z,w)\in Z'_1\times Z'_2;\ \text{split}]$
with the product weights of \S(g), and a bound only if $q,q'$ lie in
different $\rho_{z,w}$-cycles for a constant fraction of the free
pairs $(z,w)$ --- (U) again, now for free rows and generic columns in
$X_\lambda$.  So (U) reproduces itself under every reduction we have
(rows$\leftrightarrow$columns, conditioned$\leftrightarrow$free), each
time with the ``different cycles'' event for a fresh pair of lines, and
a proof must start from an unconditional \emph{seed}.

\emph{The one seed we know.}  The surviving direction gives no lower
bound on any different-cycles event (\S(f), aside), but the intercalate
count does: Kwan, Sah and Sawhney \cite{KSS21} prove
$\Pr[N<(1-\delta)n^2/4]\le e^{-\Omega(n^2)}$ and
$\Pr[N>(1+\delta)n^2/4]\le e^{-\Omega(n^{4/3}(\log n)^{2/3})}$ for the
number $N$ of intercalates of a uniform square.  These bounds are
superpolynomially small, and they survive conditioning on \emph{any}
fixed first two rows $R$: the number of completions of a $2\times n$
rectangle is at least $\prod_{k=2}^{n-1}n!\,((n-k)/n)^n$ by van der
Waerden's inequality applied row by row, while $L_n\le\prod_{k\le n}(k!)^{n/k}$
(Br\'egman), and both have logarithm $n^2\log n-2n^2+O(n\log^2n)$, so
$\Pr[\text{rows }1,2=R]\ge e^{-O(n\log^2n)}$ for every $R$, hence
$\Pr[(L,P)\in\text{any given class of }X]\ge e^{-O(n\log^2n)}$ as well.
Therefore, uniformly over $\lambda$, $\alpha,\beta$ and the mark, a
uniform element of $X$ has $(1\pm\delta)n^2/4$ intercalates with
probability $1-e^{-\Omega(n^{4/3}(\log n)^{2/3})}$: an average of
$\tfrac12$ two-cycles per row pair, including the pairs the frame
selects.  What is missing is, first, a
\emph{spreading} statement --- that the intercalates are not
concentrated on $o(n^2)$ row pairs --- and second a decorrelation
from the frame, since spreading over the $\binom n2$ row pairs says
nothing about the $O(n)$ pairs the frame selects; and even then a
$2$-cycle $\{q,c\}$ of $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$ is of no direct use for
$(\beta)$: its column cycle through $x_k$ is $\{x_k,y_k\}$, which
contains $y_k$, so it is not a clean coordinate, and its turn
conjugates $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$ by $(q\,c)$ and is inert
(Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}).  The seed is a lower bound on a
different-cycles event, which is what every reduction above asks for,
but it is not yet in a usable place.  Whether a spreading statement is
within reach of the switching method of \cite{KS18} is the question we
would ask next.

\paragraph{(i) What the Kwan--Sudakov machinery gives, and a
gap-direction bound for distance-$2$ marks.}  We went through
\cite{KS18} and \cite{KSS21} with the question of \S(h) in mind.

\emph{Inventory.}  (1) Their transfer lemma (\cite[Prop.~5]{KS18},
\cite[Thm.~3.3]{KSS21}, from Br\'egman and van der Waerden): the
probability that the first $k$ rows of a uniform square equal a given
$k\times n$ rectangle varies by a factor $e^{O(n\log^2n)}$ over
rectangles.  This is the estimate of \S(h) and it means that
\emph{every} statement with failure probability $e^{-\omega(n\log^2n)}$
in the space of uniform squares holds in every class of $X$, uniformly
in $\lambda$, $\alpha,\beta$ and the mark.  (2) \cite[Thm.~3]{KS18}:
for two \emph{fixed} rows, $\E N_2\le1+o(1)$ and
$\Pr[N_2\ge t]=e^{-\Omega(t\log t)}$, proved by the flips and turns of
CGW composed into ``joins'' and a double count: this is exactly the
method of Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive}, which should be cited
next to it; like ours it is an upper bound on short cycles obtained
from the joining direction, and it says nothing in the gap direction.
(3) The lower tail $\Pr[N\le(1-\delta)n^2/4]\le e^{-\Omega(n^2)}$ of
\cite{KSS21} is proved in a different space: by Keevash's
approximation of a random Latin square by the random triangle-removal
process on a constant fraction of the cells, at a cost
$e^{n^{2-\gamma}}$ (\cite[Thm.~2.4]{KSS21}), where counts of local
substructures concentrate by a martingale argument.  It transfers lower
tails of counts of \emph{local} substructures at scale $n^2$ with
failure $e^{-\Omega(n^2)}$, and only those.  (4) \cite[Lemma~6.4]{KSS21}
localises the \emph{upper} tail to one row in a $k\times n$ rectangle
($\Pr[\text{row $1$ in $\ge t$ intercalates}]\le(k/t)^{\Omega(t)}$).

\emph{What this does and does not give.}  The frame selects
$\ge n/\log^2n$ disjoint row pairs; we would like a constant fraction
of them to carry a local witness (a short $\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through
$p$ avoiding $p'$, say).  A statement about a \emph{fixed} family $F$
of $m\le n/2$ disjoint pairs has natural failure probability
$e^{-\Omega(m)}$ in the rectangle space, which does not survive the
transfer (1) (cost $e^{O(n\log^2n)}$); a union over all families costs
$e^{O(n)}$ and does not help either; and the Keevash route (3) only
sees counts at scale $n^2$.  So the published machinery gives, inside
$X$: ``all but $o(n^2)$ row pairs have an intercalate'' (and similar
local counts), uniformly in $\lambda$ --- and nothing about a family of
$O(n)$ pairs selected by the square.  Decorrelation from the frame is
not something these methods address.

\emph{A gap-direction bound, by a rigid inverse.}  The one place where
we found a bounded-multiplicity inverse for a type-changing move is
CGW's case 3 with $\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$: there the cross-switch at
$\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ either reverses nothing or reverses the long
arc, both forms land in $S(\mu)$, and the pair that was cross-switched
is \emph{determined} by the target and the joining pair (it is
$\{\omega_{L_1}a,\omega_{L_1}b\}$ or the pair symmetric about $a$ at
distance $2$, $(a,b)$ the distance-$2$ ordering of the joining pair in
$L_1=\mathrm{flip}(M)$).  Counting edges per joining pair of the target
with this inverse (at most $4$: direct edges at $A$-pairs, switch edges
at $B$-pairs, the case-2 switch edge attributed to the backflipped
pair) gives (\texttt{paper/cgw\_gap\_note.tex}, Proposition~3,
refereed; verified by \texttt{s13\_cgw\_min2.py} at $n=7,8,10,12$ and
exactly at $n=5$, where the per-pair multiplicities are $2,3,4$):
\begin{proposition}[distance-$2$ marks]\label{prop:alpha2}
For every $\lambda$ with a part $m\ge4$ and $X=X(n,\lambda;2,m-2)$,
$\CC_n(\lambda)/\CC_n(\mu)\le2$, i.e.\ $\Pr_X[B]/\Pr_X[A]\le3$ and
$\Pr_X[A]\ge\tfrac14$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent This is an unconditional lower bound on a different-cycles
event in $X$ (rows $1,2$ apart in the column cycles of the mark),
obtained from the surviving direction plus a rigid inverse of the
type-changing move --- the pattern that \S(g) said was unavailable.
It does not extend to $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$ for a reason of
\emph{type}, not multiplicity: there the wrong-form case-3 edges land
in the $(2,m-2)$ split, and the same count gives only
$\Pr_X[A]\ge\tfrac13(1-\Pr_X[\{j,j'\}\text{ and }\{\omega j,\omega j'\}\text{ both }B])$,
which needs a lower bound on an $A$-event at one of two adjacent
marks.  By Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} the same conclusion for all
$\alpha$ would follow from $\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]\le\tfrac12$.
For the orbit method the lesson is that a lower bound on
$\Pr[(q,c)\text{ separated}]$ for a clean coordinate would follow from
a type-changing move at $(q,c)$ whose inverse is rigid; the moves of
\S(g) are not, because the third pair of rows they use is free.

\paragraph{(j) Adaptive coordinates: separation is automatic, and
$(\beta)$ disappears.}  The search for a rigid type-changing move
led instead to a change of coordinates that removes the need for one.
In \S(c) the coordinates of a ladder pair $(x,y)$ were the pairs of a
\emph{fixed} matching, chosen independently of the square, and
$(\beta)$ asked that they be separated by $\{p,p'\}$ in $\rho_{x,y}$
with positive probability.  Choose them instead \emph{along
$\rho_{x,y}$ from the mark}: for $t\ge1$ let
\[
(q^t,c^t):=(\rho_{x,y}^{\,t}(p),\ \rho_{x,y}^{\,t}(p')),
\]
and let $\mu_{x,y}:=\min(d(p\to p'),d(p'\to p))$ if $(x,y)$ is crossed
and $\min(|Q_p|,|Q_{p'}|)$ if parallel.  For $t<\mu_{x,y}$ the pair
$(q^t,c^t)$ is free ($q^t\ne c^t$, both $\notin\{p,p'\}$) and
\emph{separated by $\{p,p'\}$ in $\rho_{x,y}$ by construction}: on
different arcs when crossed, one in $Q_p$ and one in $Q_{p'}$ when
parallel.  So the turn of its column cycle $Z$ through $x$ (with $y\notin Z$)
toggles the bit (Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}) whenever it is legal ---
there is no genericity to ask for.

\begin{lemma}[the adaptive coordinate is orbit-invariant]\label{lem:adaptinv}
Let $t<\mu_{x,y}$, let $Z$ be the column cycle of $(q^t,c^t)$ through
$x$ with $y\notin Z$, and let $L'$ be the turn of $Z$.  Then
$\rho'_{x,y}=\rho_{x,y}\circ(q^t\,c^t)$, the status of $(x,y)$ toggles,
$\mu'_{x,y}=\mu_{x,y}$, and $(\rho'^{\,s}(p),\rho'^{\,s}(p'))=(q^s,c^s)$
for every $s\le t$; in particular the $t$-th candidate of $L'$ is
$(q^t,c^t)$ again, and its column cycle through $x$ is $Z$ (same row
set).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $\rho=\rho_{x,y}$, $q=q^t$, $c=c^t$.  The turn exchanges the
entries of $x$ at $q,c$ (and those of the other rows of $Z$, which do
not affect $\rho$), so $\rho'=\rho\circ(q\,c)$: $\rho'(q)=\rho(c)$,
$\rho'(c)=\rho(q)$, $\rho'=\rho$ elsewhere.  Crossed case, with
$p=u_0\to u_1\to\dots\to u_a=p'\to v_1\to\dots\to v_b=p$ and
$q=u_t$, $c=v_t$ ($t<\min(a,b)$): the cycles of $\rho'$ through $p$
and $p'$ are $u_0\to\dots\to u_t\to v_{t+1}\to\dots\to v_b=p$ and
$u_{t+1}\to\dots\to u_a=p'\to v_1\to\dots\to v_t\to u_{t+1}$, of
lengths $b$ and $a$, so $(x,y)$ is now parallel with
$\mu'=\min(a,b)=\mu$, and the paths $p\to u_s$, $p'\to v_s$ for
$s\le t$ are untouched.  Parallel case, $Q_p=(p=u_0,u_1,\dots,u_{a-1})$,
$Q_{p'}=(p'=v_0,\dots,v_{b-1})$, $q=u_t$, $c=v_t$ ($t<\min(a,b)$): the
single cycle of $\rho'$ is
$u_0\to\dots\to u_t\to v_{t+1}\to\dots\to v_{b-1}\to v_0=p'\to v_1\to\dots\to v_t\to u_{t+1}\to\dots\to u_{a-1}\to u_0$,
with $d'(p\to p')=b$, $d'(p'\to p)=a$, so crossed with $\mu'=\mu$, and
again the paths up to step $t$ are untouched.  The row set of the
turned cycle is preserved (Lemma~\ref{lem:cycletrade}).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[orbit bound with adaptive coordinates]\label{prop:adaptive}
Let $(L,P)\in X$, $I$ a frame-determined family of pairs $(x_k,y_k)$
as in \S(c) (distinct $x$-rows, disjoint from the $y$-rows, none in
$\{1,2\}$; the ladder, or a union of offset diagonals), and
$T,\ell_1\ge1$.  Call the candidate $(k,t)$ \emph{good} if
$t\le\min(T,\mu_k-1)$ and the column cycle $Z_k^t$ of $(q_k^t,c_k^t)$
through $x_k$ has length $\le\ell_1$, meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$
rows and meets the rows of $I$ only in $x_k$.  Let $t_0(k)$ be
the least good $t$ (if any), $C_k=\{q_k^t,c_k^t:\ t\le t_0(k)\}$
(all candidates $t\le\min(T,\mu_k-1)$ if there is none), and
\[
U=\bigl\{k\in I:\ t_0(k)\text{ exists and }\{q_k^{t_0(k)},c_k^{t_0(k)}\}\cap C_l=\emptyset\ \text{for all }l\in I\setminus\{k\}\bigr\}.
\]
Then the turns $T_k$ of $Z_k^{t_0(k)}$, $k\in U$, are legal, commute,
generate a free action of $\{0,1\}^U$ on $X$ that preserves the frame,
the family, every $\mu_l$, $t_0(l)$, $C_l$ ($l\in I$) and $U$; $T_k$
toggles the status of $(x_k,y_k)$ and fixes that of every other pair.
Hence
\[
\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}_I=\emptyset]\;\le\;\E_X\bigl[2^{-|U|}\bigr].
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$T_k$ changes only columns $q_k,c_k:=q_k^{t_0(k)},c_k^{t_0(k)}$ in the
rows of $Z_k$ (lengths of cycles are preserved with their row sets, so
the length condition is invariant along with the rest).  Legality: $Z_k$ meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$ rows and
$\{q_k,c_k\}\cap\{p,p'\}=\emptyset$, so the type of rows $1,2$ and the
mark are preserved (Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}); columns $p,p'$ are
untouched, so the frame and the ladder are preserved.  For $k\ne l$ in
$U$ the column pairs $\{q_k,c_k\}$, $\{q_l,c_l\}\subseteq C_l$ are
disjoint, so the turns act on disjoint cells and commute, and the
action is free.  Invariance of the data of a pair $l\ne k$ under $T_k$:
rows $x_l,y_l\notin Z_k$, so $\rho_l$, its status, $\mu_l$ and all its
candidates are unchanged; the goodness of candidate $(l,t)$ depends on
the column cycle of $(q_l^t,c_l^t)$ through $x_l$, i.e.\ on the
contents of columns $q_l^t,c_l^t$, which are not in $\{q_k,c_k\}$ for
$t\le t_0(l)$ (resp.\ for all candidates if $t_0(l)$ does not exist) by
the defining condition of $U$; hence $t_0(l)$ and $C_l$ are unchanged.
Invariance of the data of $k$ itself: by Lemma~\ref{lem:adaptinv},
$\mu_k$ and the candidates $t\le t_0(k)$ are unchanged; the candidates
$t<t_0(k)$ have columns different from $q_k,c_k$ (distinct points of
the two paths), so their cycles and non-goodness are unchanged, and
$Z_k$ itself keeps its row set, so $t_0(k)$ is still good; $C_k$ is
unchanged.  If $Z_k\ni1,2$, rows $1,2$ change at $q_k,c_k$, which
affects none of the above.  Hence $U$ is invariant, every orbit of the
cube is a cube of size $2^{|U|}$ on which the status of pair $k\in U$
is $\mathrm{bit}_k(L)\oplus\varepsilon_k$, and $\mathrm{Flip}_I=\emptyset$
forces one value of $(\varepsilon_k)_{k\in U}$; $X$ is uniform.
\end{proof}

\emph{What remains, in this coordinate system.}  Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit}
is replaced by the single statement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hypadaptive}
\E_X\bigl[2^{-|U|};\ K_0\ge\ell_0\bigr]=o(1/\log n)
\quad\text{for }I=\text{the first }\min(K_0-1,s)\text{ ladder pairs},
\end{equation}
together with its offset version for the diagonals of \S(ii) of
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit}.  No genericity constant enters: $U$ is
made of the ladder pairs that have a \emph{short clean candidate
cycle}, and $|U|\ge C\log\log n$ with probability $1-o(1/\log n)$ is
all that is needed.  The only remnant of $(\alpha)$ is the number of
candidates $\min(T,\mu_k-1)$, i.e.\ that the arcs of $\rho_k$ at $p,p'$
are longer than $T$ for a constant fraction of the pairs --- the
weakest form of arc trapping, at polylogarithmic scale.  Heuristically,
with $s=|I|$, a candidate's column cycle has length $\le\ell_1$ with
probability $\approx\ell_1/n$, is clean with probability
$\approx e^{-2s\ell_1/n}$, and the chosen pair avoids the
$\approx2Ts$ candidate columns of the other pairs with probability
$\approx(1-2Ts/n)^2$, so
\[
\E|U|\;\approx\;s\cdot\frac{T\ell_1}{n}\,e^{-2s\ell_1/n}\Bigl(1-\frac{2Ts}n\Bigr)^2
\;\asymp\;\log^2n\qquad\text{for }s=\frac n{\log^2n},\ T=\frac{\log^2n}4,\ \ell_1=\frac{\log^2n}2,
\]
far above the $C\log\log n$ required; \eqref{eq:hypadaptive} is then a
concentration statement for a count of \emph{local} configurations
(each good candidate is determined by $O(\log^2n)$ cells: $4t$ cells of
rows $x_k,y_k$ and the cycle $Z_k^t$), which is the kind of statement
the switching method is built for --- modulo, as always, the frame
selection of the rows.

\emph{Verification and data} (\texttt{s13\_adaptive.py};
\texttt{runs/s13/orbit/adaptive\{50,100\}.log}): on true-marked
instances at $n=30,50,100$, for a random $k\in U$ the turn of $Z_k$ was
applied and checked to toggle the status of $(x_k,y_k)$, to leave the
status, $\mu_l$, $t_0(l)$, $C_l$ of every other pair and the set $U$
unchanged, and to preserve the type of rows $1,2$ and the frame
($682$, $697$, $444$, $215$ turns at $n=30,50,50,100$, no failure);
the bound $\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}_I=\emptyset]\le\E[2^{-|U|}]$ holds in
every $K_0$ class.  With the small families and parameters usable at
these sizes ($T=6$, $\ell_1=6$--$10$, $|I|\le\max(3,n/8)$)
$14$--$30\%$ of the ladder pairs have a good candidate but only
$2$--$13\%$ are in $U$: the collision rule in $U$ (which forbids the
chosen pair to meet the candidate sets of \emph{all} other pairs,
$\approx2Ts$ columns) is crude, and at $n\le100$ with $2Ts\ge n$ it
removes most pairs; the asymptotic regime $2Ts/n\le\tfrac12$ is out of
reach of sampling.  (Protecting only the used pairs would need a
canonical choice of $U$ that is itself orbit-invariant; the obvious
greedy rule is not, since a turn can shrink the candidate set of an
excluded pair; the ``for all $l\in I$'' quantifier is what makes the
present $U$ invariant.)

\emph{What it costs.}  The fixed matching of \S(c) offered $\approx n/2$
coordinates per pair, of which a constant number were short and clean
and a constant fraction of those (the data say $0.19$) separated; the
adaptive rule offers $T=O(\log^2n)$ coordinates per pair, all
separated, so membership in $U$ is a \emph{rare} event per pair
($\approx T\ell_1/n$), and \eqref{eq:hypadaptive} is a lower-tail
statement about rare local events --- of the type of the witness lemma
(\S\ref{sec:witnessstatus}), with a column cycle of a free pair through
$x_k$ in place of a $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$-cycle through $p$ --- rather than
the ``typically constant size'' statement that \S(c) advertised.  The
genericity constant is gone, the rarity is not.  There is a second
regime: with $s\approx T\approx\ell_1\approx\sqrt n$ (say
$s=\sqrt n/4$), a pair has a good candidate with probability bounded
below and $\E|U|\asymp\sqrt n$, so \eqref{eq:hypadaptive} becomes a
second-moment statement about $\sqrt n$ events of constant probability
($(\gamma)$-type decorrelation) at the price of arc trapping at scale
$\sqrt n$ ($\mu_k>\sqrt n$ for a constant fraction of the pairs,
which by \eqref{eq:Jp} needs $1-b_\ell\gg n^{-1/2}$ for
$\ell\le\sqrt n$).  Either way, what is left is the behaviour of
short column cycles and arcs at the frame-selected rows.

\paragraph{(k) The first ladder pair alone: a fixed-rectangle sampler,
atypical types, and the single-pair involution.}  (Classification:
DATA; one PROVED statement that is a one-way REDUCTION of the target
to a transposed analogue not known to be easier --- not a proven lower
bound; and one PROVED alternative sufficient condition for weak CGW,
Proposition~\ref{prop:firstpairbias}, of the same kind as the reference
hypothesis.)
The minimal instance of the obstacle identified in the assessment is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:firstpair}
\Pr_X\bigl[(x_1,y_1)\text{ flippable}\bigr]\ge c,\qquad
x_1=\pi^{-1}(1),\ y_1=\pi^{-1}(2),
\end{equation}
uniformly in $\lambda,\alpha,\beta$.  (For $2\le\alpha\le m-2$ the pair
is always a pair of rows outside $\{1,2\}$: $x_1=2$ would mean
$p'=\sigma(p)$, $y_1=1$ would mean $p=\sigma(p')$; so $K_0\ge2$ always,
and the data below confirm $\Pr[K_0\ge2]=1$.)

\emph{The sampler.}  To test \eqref{eq:firstpair} and the ladder
statistics on types $\lambda$ that the uniform square never produces,
\texttt{jmfix.c} runs the Jacobson--Matthews chain on the completions
of a \emph{fixed} $2\times n$ rectangle $R=(\mathrm{id},\sigma)$: from a
proper state it chooses $(r,c,s)$ with $r\ge3$ and $s$ not in rows
$1,2$ of column $c$ (so the second row touched is also $\ge3$), and
from an improper state it chooses among the usual $2\cdot2\cdot2$
resolutions and \emph{rejects} (stays) when the chosen row is $1$ or
$2$.  The restricted move graph is undirected and every proper state
has restricted degree $(n-2)n(n-3)$, every improper state keeps its
original degree $8$ in the denominators, so the walk is a simple random
walk on the restricted graph and its stationary law is uniform on the
proper states of the component of the start.  Connectivity is not
proved; it was tested: at $n=5,6$ (types $(5)$, $(6)$, $(2,2,2)$;
$36$, $4032$, $5376$ completions, enumerated exactly) every completion
is hit from two different starts and the $\chi^2$ statistics against
uniformity are within one standard deviation of their mean
($z=+0.40,+0.96;\ +0.10,-0.22;\ -0.87,+0.40$); at $n=7$, $R=(\mathrm{id},(0\,1\cdots6))$,
$3\cdot10^5$ samples give $\Pr[B\mid R]=0.4965$ against the exact
$0.497076$ of \texttt{s13\_comp7} over all $6566400$ completions
(\texttt{s13\_fixrect.py}; logs \texttt{runs/s13/fixrect/}).  Since
rows $\ge3$ of $X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$ are exchangeable and all
marks of a given $(m,\alpha)$ in a given rectangle are equivalent under
the symmetries of $R$, $\Pr_X$ of any event determined by the mark and
the square equals its $\Pr[\,\cdot\mid R]$ averaged over the marks of
that class, which is what \texttt{s13\_fixstats.py} computes.

\emph{Data on atypical types.}  At $n=30$ ($60000$ squares per type,
thinning $2000$ moves; a second run with thinning $500$ from a
different start agrees) and $n=50$ ($40000$ squares, thinning $2500$),
for the types $(n)$, $(n/2,n/2)$, $(n-2,2)$, $(10,10,10)$,
$(4,2^{13})$, $(6,3^8)$ at $n=30$ and $(50)$, $(25,25)$, $(48,2)$,
$(4,2^{23})$, $(6,3^{14},2)$ at $n=50$, with marks at $\alpha=2$,
$\alpha\approx m/3$ and $\alpha=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ where available
($360000$ resp.\ $160000$ marked instances per class):
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Pr_X[B]\in[0.4974,0.5024]$ in every class, i.e.\ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} $\CC_n(\lambda)/\CC_n(\mu)\in[0.995,1.005]$
for every tested pair --- including $(4,2^{13})\to(2^{15})$,
$(6,3^8)\to(3^{10})$ and $(6,3^8)\to(4,3^8,2)$, types of
$\Pr_n$-probability $e^{-\Theta(n)}$.  The sampling error is
$\approx0.002$ ($n=30$) and $0.0025$ ($n=50$); the observed deviations
are of that size.  This extends the exact ratios of
Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap} ($n\le7$, $[0.985,1.0035]$) to $n=30,50$
and to extreme types, by sampling.  At $n=100$ ($12000$ squares,
thinning $10^4$, types $(100)$ and $(4,2^{48})$, sampling error
$\approx0.005$) $\Pr_X[B]\in[0.495,0.504]$ and
$\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1]\in[0.497,0.503]$.
\item $\Pr_X[(x_1,y_1)\text{ flippable}]\in[0.4988,0.5017]$ in every
class, and the same conditionally on $A$ and on $B$ (as it must be
up to the factor $\bar q$: $\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1\mid A]=\bar q\,\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1\mid B]$,
by the trade $T_1$).  At $n=7$ the value is $0.4896$, so $\tfrac12$ is
not an identity; the data say $\tfrac12+o(1)$, uniformly in the type.
\item $\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}_I=\emptyset\mid K_0=k]$ is $0.50$, $0.25$,
$0.125$ for $k=2,3,4$ in every class (independent fair bits), and the
witness frequencies at the first pair,
$\Pr[|Q_p|\le\ell',\,p'\notin Q_p]$ for $\ell'=4,8,16$, are
$0.100,0.216,0.388$ at $n=30$ and $0.060,0.134,0.261$ at $n=50$,
again the same in every class to $\pm0.002$; the mean of $|Q_p|$ is
$16.47$ at $n=30$ and $26.47$ at $n=50$ in every class.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Lower tails of the witness counts} (\texttt{s13\_wtail.py};
$n=30$: $7.2\cdot10^5$ marked instances of type $(30)$; $n=50$:
$3.6\cdot10^5$ each of $(50)$, $(25,25)$, $2.4\cdot10^5$ of $(4,2^{23})$;
$n=100$: $8\cdot10^4$ each of $(100)$, $(4,2^{48})$; thresholds
$\ell'=4,8,16,24$).  (i) Along the ladder, $\Pr[\text{no witness among
the first }K\text{ pairs}\mid K_0-1\ge K]$ equals $(1-w_1)^K$ within
sampling error for every $K$ up to $12$ ($n=30$), $20$ ($n=50$), $40$
($n=100$) and every threshold, where $w_1$ is the first-pair witness
rate ($0.100$, $0.060$, $0.030$ at $\ell'=4$); the hazard
$\Pr[\text{witness at }k\mid\text{none before }k]$ is flat in $k$ to
three digits ($0.100,0.100,\dots$ at $n=30$; $0.060$ at $n=50$; $0.030$
at $n=100$, $k\le12$); and $\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}_I=\emptyset\mid K_0-1\ge K]=2^{-K}$
within noise to $K=12$--$15$ (e.g.\ $0.00024$ vs $2^{-12}=0.00024$ at
$n=50$, $N=1.07\cdot10^5$).  (ii) Across the diagonals $D_c$ of
$B$-instances with $d_{12}=\ell\le n/4$, $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$ (which
share their $y$-rows), the number $G$ of diagonals carrying a witness
has $\Pr[G=0]$ and $\Pr[G<\E G/2]$ equal to the binomial values with
the same per-diagonal rate, within noise, for every $\ell\le12$ and
every threshold ($N\approx2000$--$13000$ instances per $\ell$).  All of
this is identical for the extreme types.  So, at $n\le100$, the witness
events at the frame-selected pairs behave as independent Bernoulli
variables with the uniform-permutation rate, along the ladder and
across the diagonals, in every type; the regime of
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness} ($\ell'\ge\log^5n$, failure $o(1/\log n)$
over $\ge n/\log^2n$ pairs) is not reachable by sampling, and these data
say nothing about it beyond the absence of any visible dependence or
type effect at the second-moment level.

So no falsification: on the types where the hypotheses could
most plausibly fail, nothing distinguishes them from the typical type
at the resolution $2\cdot10^{-3}$.  (This is \emph{not} evidence about
the $o(1/\log n)$ tails; it is evidence that the first-moment
quantities are type-independent.)

\emph{The single-pair involution.}  The adaptive coordinates of \S(j),
specialised to the one pair $(x_1,y_1)$, need neither short column
cycles nor collision-freeness, because there are no other pairs to
protect.  Write $\rho=\rho_{x_1,y_1}$, $\mu$ for the minimum of the two
arc lengths (crossed) or of $|Q_p|,|Q_{p'}|$ (parallel); then $\mu\ge2$
always, since $\rho(p)=p'$ would need $L_{y_1,p'}=L_{1,p'}$ and
$\rho(p')=p$ would need $L_{x_1,p'}=L_{2,p'}$.  For $1\le t<\mu$ let
$\{q_t,c_t\}=\{\rho^t(p),\rho^t(p')\}$ (free, distinct, separated), let
$\tau_t$ be the column permutation of the pair ($\tau_t(r)$ is the row
of $L_{r,q_t}$ in column $c_t$) and $Z_t(r)$ its cycle through $r$.
Call $t$ \emph{good} if $x_1,y_1$ lie in different cycles of $\tau_t$
and at least one of $Z_t(x_1)$, $Z_t(y_1)$ meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or
$2$ rows; let $G=\{\text{some }t<\mu\text{ is good}\}$ and, on $G$,
$t_0$ the least good $t$ and $Z$ the admissible cycle (that through
$x_1$ if admissible, else that through $y_1$).

\begin{proposition}[single-pair involution]\label{prop:firstpair}
Turning $Z$ (exchanging columns $q_{t_0},c_{t_0}$ in the rows of $Z$)
is an involution of $X\cap G$ that preserves the frame $\pi$ (hence
$x_1,y_1$), toggles the flippability of $(x_1,y_1)$ and fixes $t_0$ and
$Z$.  Consequently
\[
\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1\cap G]=\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1^{\,c}\cap G],\qquad
\bigl|\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1]-\tfrac12\bigr|\le\tfrac12\Pr_X[G^c],\qquad
\Pr_X[A],\ \Pr_X[B]\ \ge\ \tfrac14\Pr_X[G].
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Columns $p,p'$ are untouched, so $\pi$, $x_1,y_1$, the mark and the
rows outside $Z$ are unchanged; rows $1,2$ are both in $Z$ or both
outside, and if both are in, rows $1,2$ become $L_1\circ(q\,c)$,
$L_2\circ(q\,c)$, so $\sigma'=(q\,c)\,\sigma\,(q\,c)$ has the type of
$\sigma$ and, as $q,c\notin\{p,p'\}$, $\sigma'^{\alpha}(p)=p'$: the
turned square is in $X$.  (In the normalised space $S(R)$ this turn
is, after the column relabelling $(q\,c)$, the turn of the complement
of $Z$, which contains exactly one of $x_1,y_1$ as well.)  Exactly one
of the rows $x_1,y_1$ is in $Z$ (they are in different $\tau$-cycles),
so $\rho'=\rho\circ(q\,c)$ if $x_1\in Z$ and $\rho'=(q\,c)\circ\rho$ if
$y_1\in Z$.  In the first case Lemma~\ref{lem:adaptinv} applies with
$t=t_0$.  In the second, $(q\,c)\circ\rho=\rho\circ(u\,u')$ with
$u=\rho^{t_0-1}(p)$, $u'=\rho^{t_0-1}(p')$ (possibly $t_0=1$,
$\{u,u'\}=\{p,p'\}$), and the same computation as in the lemma, with
$t_0-1$ in place of $t$, shows: the $\rho$-paths from $p$ to $u$ and
from $p'$ to $u'$ are unchanged, $\rho'(u)=c$ and $\rho'(u')=q$ (the
ordered pair at step $t_0$ is reversed, the unordered pair is
$\{q,c\}$), and crossed with arcs $(a,b)$ becomes parallel with cycle
lengths $(b,a)$ and conversely.  In both cases $\mu'=\mu$ ($\rho$ has no
fixed points as $x_1\neq y_1$), the candidates are the same for
$t\le t_0$, flippability toggles, and the column pairs with $t<t_0$ are
disjoint from $\{q,c\}$, so their $\tau_t$ and their badness are
unchanged.  At $t_0$, $\tau'_{t_0}$ is $\tau_{t_0}$ with the
cycle $Z$ reversed (or $\tau_{t_0}^{-1}$ so modified), so $x_1,y_1$ are
still in different cycles, $Z$ is still its own cycle with the same
intersection with $\{1,2\}$, and the other cycle through
$\{x_1,y_1\}$ is unchanged; hence $t_0$ and $Z$ are the same in the
turned square and the turn is an involution.  The first identity
follows; the second from $\Pr[\mathrm{flip}_1]\ge\tfrac12\Pr[G]$ and
$\Pr[\mathrm{flip}_1^c]\ge\tfrac12\Pr[G]$; for the third, the trade
$T_1$ along $Q_{p'}$ at $(x_1,y_1)$ is an involution of
$X\cap\mathrm{flip}_1$ exchanging $A$ and $B$ (the frame becomes
$(x_1\,y_1)\circ\pi$), so
$\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]=\Pr[B\cap\mathrm{flip}_1^c]-\Pr[A\cap\mathrm{flip}_1^c]$,
whence $\Pr[A]\ge\tfrac12-\tfrac12\Pr[\mathrm{flip}_1^c]\ge\tfrac14\Pr[G]$,
and likewise for $B$.
\end{proof}

\emph{The first pair controls the bias.}  (Found by the internal
referee of the first draft of this paragraph; PROVED, verified exactly.)
The two involutions combine: $T_1$ exchanges $A$ and $B$ on $F:=\mathrm{flip}_1$,
and the turn exchanges $F$ and $F^c$ on $G$ while fixing $\pi$, hence $A$
and $B$.

\begin{proposition}[the first ladder pair controls the bias]\label{prop:firstpairbias}
For every $n,\lambda,\alpha,\beta$,
\[
\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A]=\E_X\bigl[(\mathbf 1_B-\mathbf 1_A)(\mathbf 1_{F^c}-\mathbf 1_F);\,G^c\bigr],
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\bigl|\Pr_X[B]-\tfrac12\bigr|\le\tfrac12\Pr_X[G^c],\quad
\Pr_X[A],\Pr_X[B]\ge\tfrac12\Pr_X[G],
\]
and $|\bar q-1|\le 2\Pr_X[G^c]/(1-\Pr_X[G^c])$.  Consequently
(Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}, which needs $|\bar q-1|\le\delta(n)$ with
$\delta=o(1/\log n)$ over all $\lambda$ and all marks) the weak CGW
conjecture follows from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hypfirstpair}
\sup_{\lambda,\alpha,\beta}\Pr_{X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)}[G^c]=o(1/\log n),
\end{equation}
an event selected by the first ladder pair (it involves the column
cycles of the pairs $\{q_t,c_t\}$ through all rows, and rows $1,2$
through admissibility; it is not local to the pair).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $B_{FG}=\Pr_X[B\cap F\cap G]$ etc.  By $T_1$ (the $k=1$ trade of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder}: an involution of $X\cap F$ with frame
$(x_1\,y_1)\circ\pi$, hence exchanging $A$ and $B$),
$B_{FG}+B_{FG^c}=A_{FG}+A_{FG^c}$.  By the turn (an involution of $X\cap G$
exchanging $F$ and $F^c$ and preserving $\pi$, hence $A$ and $B$, which
are functions of $\pi$), $B_{FG}=B_{F^cG}$ and $A_{FG}=A_{F^cG}$.  Hence
\[
\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]=(B_{F^cG}+B_{F^cG^c})-(A_{F^cG}+A_{F^cG^c})
=(B_{FG}-A_{FG})+(B_{F^cG^c}-A_{F^cG^c})
=(A_{FG^c}-B_{FG^c})+(B_{F^cG^c}-A_{F^cG^c}),
\]
which is the displayed expectation; its absolute value is at most
$\Pr[G^c]$.  ($T_1$ need not preserve $G$.)  The last inequality is
$|\bar q-1|=|\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]|/\Pr[A]$ with $\Pr[A]\ge\tfrac12(1-\Pr[G^c])$.
\end{proof}

Exact verification (\texttt{s13\_firstpair\_id.py}, all completions, all
marks): $n=5$, $(5)$, $\alpha=2,3$; $n=6$, $(6)$, $\alpha=2,3,4$, and
$(4,2)$: the identity and its two ingredients hold to all digits (e.g.\
$n=6$, $(6)$, $\alpha=3$: $\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]=-0.142857$ on both sides,
$\Pr[G^c]=0.714$); $n=7$, $2\cdot10^5$ sampled completions:
left side $-0.0052$, right side $-0.0058$ (each $\pm0.001$), exact value
of both $-0.005848$.  Data for $\Pr_X[G^c]$ with all candidates $t<\mu$:
$0.61$, $0.18$, $0.11$ at $n=7,30,50$ (type $(n)$, $\alpha=2$), i.e.\
$\approx5.5/n$; $\Pr[\mu=k]\approx4/n$ for small $k$ and
$\Pr[G^c\mid\mu=k]=0.59,0.37,0.22,0.13,0.08$ for $k=2,\dots,6$ against
$0.59^{k-1}=0.59,0.35,0.21,0.12,0.07$ (a slight positive dependence
among the candidates, which share the rows $x_1,y_1$).

\emph{Cost and comparison with the witness lemma.}
\eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} is again a lower tail of a count of events at
square-selected positions --- the obstacle is unchanged in kind.  It
decomposes as $\Pr[G^c]\le\Pr[\mu\le M]+\Pr[\mu>M,\ t=1,\dots,M\text{
all bad}]$.  The first term is an upper bound on a short arc or cycle
of $\rho_{x_1,y_1}$ at $p,p'$; nothing of the kind is proved at a
frame-selected pair (Proposition~\ref{prop:splice} bounds a different
frame event), and heuristically it is $\approx4M/n$ (data:
$\Pr[\mu=k]\approx4/n$).  The second term --- among $M$ candidates on
disjoint free column pairs not all are bad --- is the obstacle itself,
a lower bound for a local event at square-selected positions; the data
put the per-candidate probability at $0.41$ and the dependence between
candidates at a few percent, so heuristically it is $0.6^M$.  Compared
with Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness} (all of this at the level of the
heuristics and the data, nothing proved): one row pair instead of
$\ge n/\log^2n$; per-event probability $\approx0.4$ instead of
$\ell'/n$; expected count $\approx0.4\,\E\mu$ with $\E\mu=6.3,9.6,18$
at $n=30,50,100$, instead of $\log^3n$; no offset families, no splice
bound, no $K_0$ conditioning.  Neither hypothesis is known to imply the
other ($\Pr[F^c]\ge\tfrac12\Pr[G]$, so \eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} does not
give (L); Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness} says nothing about $G$), so by
the working rules \eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} is an alternative sufficient
condition.  It is the smallest \emph{sufficient} condition in these
notes; the minimal instance \eqref{eq:firstpair} is smaller but not
sufficient.

\emph{What this is and is not.}  Proposition~\ref{prop:firstpair} is
proved, and it has the attractive consequence that
$\Pr_X[G]\ge c$ would give $\Pr_X[A]\ge c/2$ for \emph{every} split,
i.e.\ a CGW-type constant bound without the gap of
Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}.  But $G$ is a transposed analogue of the
target: ``the column-pair permutation of a pair of columns selected
from rows $x_1,y_1$ by $\rho$ puts the frame-selected rows $x_1,y_1$ in
different cycles'' is flippability with rows and columns exchanged
(\S(a)), with the admissibility clause added and with the conditioning
still on rows $1,2$.  The implication is one-way
($\Pr_X[G]\ge2c$ gives \eqref{eq:firstpair}; nothing comes back), and
$G$ is not known to be easier: it is a lower bound for a local event
at positions selected by the square, which is the obstacle itself.
(One can toggle $G_t$ in turn by a trade of a row pair $(u,v)$,
$u\in Z_t(x_1)$, $v\in Z_t(y_1)$, along a $\rho_{u,v}$-cycle through
$q_t$ avoiding $c_t,p,p'$, when such a legal trade exists; we have not
checked what such a trade preserves, and we record only that it would
produce a third event of the same kind, not a bound.)  Three things are
gained: the two-sided
$|\Pr_X[\mathrm{flip}_1]-\tfrac12|\le\tfrac12\Pr_X[G^c]$ is a structural
reason for the observed value $\tfrac12$, turning ``$\tfrac12+o(1)$''
into ``$\Pr_X[G]\to1$'', a no-conspiracy statement over $\mu-1$
candidates (probability $0.88$ at $n=50$ for $t\le8$); the
constant-$c$ target follows from the single event $G_1$ ($t=1$ is
always a candidate), of probability $\approx0.41$ in the data; and,
combined with $T_1$, the same event controls $\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A]$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:firstpairbias}).  But a lower bound on
$\Pr_X[G_1]$ or on $\Pr_X[G]$ is the same problem.
What did not change: a lower bound on the probability of any local
event at a frame-selected position in $X$ remains unproved; the only
one available is Proposition~\ref{prop:alpha2}'s $\Pr_X[A]\ge\tfrac14$
at distance $2$, which comes from CGW's global joining argument, not
from a local one.

\paragraph{(l) R4: the $\nu$-tail at the first pair, and where a switching
for it stalls.}  (Classification: one small PROVED lemma, which is a
one-way REDUCTION of the $\nu$-term of \eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} to a
lower tail at frame-selected positions; NEGATIVE KNOWLEDGE about the
switching route; DATA.  Stopped by the agreed rule after one segment.)
Write $\nu$ for the arc/cycle minimum at the first pair (called $\mu$
in \S(k); the paper uses $\nu$ since $\mu$ is the split type).  The
first-pair condition \eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} splits as
$\Pr_X[G^c]\le\Pr_X[\nu\le M]+\Pr_X[\nu>M,\ t\le M\text{ all bad}]$,
and the first term is an \emph{upper} bound on a short arc or cycle of
$\rho=\rho_{x_1,y_1}$ at the mark, the direction in which switchings
usually work.  The event $\{\nu\le M\}$ is the union over $k\le M$ of
the four events $E_k(a,b)$, $a,b\in\{p,p'\}$: the $\rho$-path
$w_0=a,w_1,\dots$ first meets $\{p,p'\}$ at step $k\ge2$, at $w_k=b$.
Each such event is a ``staircase'' of $2k$ cells in rows $x_1,y_1$:
$L(y_1,w_{i+1})=L(x_1,w_i)$ for $0\le i<k$, together with the two anchor
cells $L(x_1,p)=L(1,p')$, $L(y_1,p)=L(2,p')$ that define the pair.

\begin{lemma}[a rigid always-legal switching for the staircase]\label{lem:nuswitch}
Fix $k\ge2$, $a,b\in\{p,p'\}$ and $L\in E_k(a,b)$ with path $w_0,\dots,w_k$ (recall
$x_1,y_1\notin\{1,2\}$ since $\alpha,\beta\ge2$, \S(k)).  For a row
$u\notin\{1,2,x_1,y_1\}$ let $W_u$ be the $\rho_{x_1,u}$-cycle through $w_{k-1}$, and call
$u$ admissible if $W_u\cap(\{p\}\cup\{w_0,\dots,w_{k-2}\})=\emptyset$; let $f_k(L)$ be the
number of admissible $u$.  For admissible $u$, trading rows $x_1,u$ along $W_u$ gives a
square $L'\in X$ with the same frame-selected pair $(x_1,y_1)$, and $(L,u)$ is recovered
from $(L',k,a,b)$ alone.  Consequently, writing $f_{k,a,b}$ for $f_k$ and, on
$\{\nu\le M\}$, $(k,a,b)$ for the triple realising $\nu$ (with $a=p$ if both starts do),
for every $\theta>0$,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[E_k(a,b)\cap\{f_{k,a,b}\ge\theta n\}\bigr]\le\frac1{\theta n},\qquad
\Pr_X\bigl[\nu\le M,\ f_{k,a,b}\ge\theta n\bigr]\le\frac{4M}{\theta n}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Rows $1,2$ are untouched, so $L'\in X$ (the mark and $\sigma$ are unchanged); row $y_1$ is
untouched; $p\notin W_u$ keeps the anchor $L(x_1,p)$, so $x_1,y_1$ are the frame-selected
pair of $L'$.  Inversion: $\rho'(w)$ (the column of $L'(x_1,w)$ in row $y_1$) differs from
$\rho(w)$ only for $w\in W_u$, so the admissibility condition makes the $\rho'$-path from
$a$ agree with $w_0,\dots,w_{k-1}$; the symbol $s=L(x_1,w_{k-1})=L(y_1,b)=L'(y_1,b)$ is read
off row $y_1$; $u$ is the row of $s$ in column $w_{k-1}$ of $L'$ (the trade moved $s$ from
$(x_1,w_{k-1})$ to $(u,w_{k-1})$ and changed nothing else in that column; note
$w_{k-1}\notin\{p,p'\}$ by first meeting, and $p'\in W_u$ is harmless, as nothing reads row
$x_1$ at $p'$); and
$W_u$ is the $\rho'_{x_1,u}$-cycle through $w_{k-1}$ (the trade inverts it as a cycle of
$\rho_{x_1,u}$).  Trading back returns $L$.  So
$\sum_{L\in E_k(a,b)}f_k(L)\le|X|$, which gives the first bound; the second is a union bound over
$k\le M$ and the four $(a,b)$.
\end{proof}

Checked on samples (\texttt{s13\_nutail.py}; $218101$ moves at $n=30$,
$28018$ at $n=50$, $k\le6$): every move is legal, keeps $(x_1,y_1)$ and
is inverted exactly.  The admissible fraction $f_k/n$ has mean
$0.35,0.23,0.17,0.14,0.11$ for $k=2,\dots,6$ at $n=30$ ($0.38$--$0.13$ at
$n=50$), and a lower tail: at $n=30$, $f_k<n/20$ for $0$, $0.5\%$, $2.6\%$,
$7\%$, $14\%$ of the sources with $k=2,\dots,6$.

\emph{Where it stalls.}  The lemma leaves
$\Pr_X[\nu\le M,\ f<\theta n]$, and $f<\theta n$ says that for all but
$\theta n$ rows $u$ the $\rho_{x_1,u}$-cycle through $w_{k-1}$ meets a
prescribed set of $k$ columns.  This is a lower tail of a count of
constant-probability events (the cycle through a given column of a
random permutation has length uniform on $\{1,\dots,n\}$, so it avoids
$k$ given columns with probability $\approx1/(k+1)$; the data above fit
this) at frame-selected positions ($x_1$, $w_{k-1}$ and the path) ---
of the same type as the second term, though a different event.  Two attempted repairs fail for the reasons already on
record: (i) by row exchangeability and a union bound over the
staircase's $n^{2k-2}$ free parameters, $\Pr_X[\nu\le M]$ would follow
from a \emph{fixed-position} bound ``$\Pr[Q\subseteq L\mid R]\le(C/n)^{|Q|}$
for partial squares $Q$ with $|Q|\le2M+2$ cells in two rows'' (the
staircase of $E_k(a,b)$ with its two anchors has $2k$, $2k+1$ or $2k+2$
distinct cells and $n^{2k-3}$, $n^{2k-2}$ or $n^{2k-1}$ free parameters
according as $(a,b)=(p,p)$, mixed, or $(p',p')$, and the exchangeability
factor is $(n-2)(n-3)$; then $\Pr_X[\nu\le M]\le8C^{2M+2}/n$ for
$C\ge2$); but in $S(R)$ the always-legal row
trades give only one factor $1/n$ per row (two cells in one row are
not shown to have probability $O(1/n^2)$), and column turns are legal
only on a set of choices whose size is again a lower tail of a
fixed-position count; (ii) breaking the staircase by a trade that is
allowed to move the anchor (so that the frame-selected pair changes)
loses the rigidity of the inversion.  So the lemma moves the $\nu$-term to a lower tail of the same type
as the second term (a different event, at positions selected by the
same pair); it does not show the $\nu$-term easier or harder than the
second term.

\emph{Data (R4).}  With all candidates $t<\nu$ (\texttt{s13\_firstpair\_id.py};
\texttt{runs/s13/fixrect/fp*.log}): $n\Pr_X[G^c]=5.5,\,5.5,\,5.5,\,5.4$ at
$n=30,50,100,150$ for the type $(n)$ ($\alpha=2$; $5.6$, $5.5$ for
$\alpha=n/2$ at $n=100,150$), and $5.5$ for $(4,2^{73})$ at $n=150$
($6000$ squares, $3$ marks each, thinning $n^2$).  $\Pr[\nu=k]\approx4/n$
for $k\le12$ at every $n$ ($0.040$ at $n=100$, $0.026$ at $n=150$), and
$\Pr[G^c\mid\nu=k]$ against $0.59^{k-1}$: $0.60,0.30,0.20,0.14,0.07$ vs
$0.59,0.35,0.21,0.12,0.07$ at $n=150$ --- the same at every $n$ and type.
So the first-moment picture $\Pr_X[G^c]\approx\E[0.6^{\nu-1}]\approx5.5/n$
is stable in $n$ and type; it says nothing about the $o(1/\log n)$
requirement beyond the trivial observation that $5.5/n\ll1/\log n$.

\emph{What did not change.}  No lower bound at a frame-selected position;
both terms of \eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} are lower tails of counts of
constant-probability events at such positions.  Stopped.

Divoux--Kelly--Kennedy--Sidhu.}  (Classification: NEGATIVE KNOWLEDGE,
with three literature anchors for the obstacle.)  Read against three
questions: (a) is there a fixed-cell upper bound usable in completions

\emph{Kwan--Sah--Sawhney--Simkin, Substructures in Latin squares (Israel
J.\ Math.\ 2023).}  (a) Their Lemma~3.3 is exactly the fixed-cell bound
(FC) of \S(l) --- $\Pr[L_{r,c}=s\mid Q\subseteq L]\le1/(n-2(k-1)-|Q_r|)$
for a uniform $k\times n$ \emph{rectangle} and any completable partial
array $Q$ (full prescribed rows allowed) --- proved by swapping the
entry at $(r,c)$ with another entry of the same row, which is legal for
all but $2(k-1)$ choices because the columns have only $k$ entries; the
move is never legal in a full square.  With Godsil--McKay's Theorem~4.7
($\Pr[M\subseteq L]\approx n^{-|M|}$ for small $M$ in $k\times n$
rectangles) these are their only fixed-position estimates; everything
about full squares is obtained by transfer (upper tails through
$\gamma n$-row rectangles and McKay--Wanless Prop.~4, factor
$e^{O(n\log^2n)}$; lower tails through the triangle-removal process,
Theorem~7.6, factor $e^{O(n^{2-\gamma})}$).  (b) CGW is cited once, as
background on the expected number of intercalates.  (c) No.

\emph{Allsop--Wanless, Subsquares in random Latin rectangles
(Combinatorica 2025).}  Results for all $k\le n$, including full squares,
by cycle switching.  Their Lemmas~3.8--3.10 are fixed-cell conditional
bounds, stated for uniform $k\times n$ rectangles with $k>m$ (so for
full squares in particular), whose hypotheses are structural (what else
is prescribed in the cell's row, column or symbol class), not
positional: a cell that is the only prescribed one in its row
has conditional probability $\le1/(k-m)$ (row-cycle switching, always
legal: our ``one cell per row is exactly $1/(n-2)$''); a cell that is the
only one in its column or with its symbol, $\le(1+O(m/n))/n$ (column or
symbol cycle switching, legal there because nothing is prescribed in
that column); and a cell with $\ell$ earlier prescribed cells in its row,
$\le(\ell+1)/(k-m)$, \emph{provided} the prescribed entries in the
relevant column pairs form a union of column cycles (Lemma~3.9 via
Lemma~3.3: under this closure condition the probability that the row
cycle through the new cell avoids the $\ell$ earlier columns is exactly
$1/(\ell+1)$, by a second switching that swaps the two columns on all
non-prescribed rows, legal precisely because of the closure).  For
interior cells they use the trivial bound $1$, and they write (\S3.1) that
estimating the number of columns $c'$ for which the column cycle
$\sigma(c,c',r)$ avoids prescribed rows ``seems like a difficult task in
general'' --- the forward-degree problem of \S(l), in their words.  In $X$
the closure condition for a column pair $(q,c)$ over rows $1,2$ holds
only when $\{q,c\}$ is a $2$-cycle of $\sigma$, and the complement swap
is then our product of legal turns of the other cycles (Lemma lem:legalturn),
which conjugates every $\rho_{x,y}$, $x,y\ge3$; so nothing transfers.
(b) CGW cited for intercalate counts only.  (c) Their Theorem~2.1 rests
on Kwan--Sudakov and KSS(S), not on CGW.

\emph{Divoux--Kelly--Kennedy--Sidhu, Subsquares in random Latin squares
and rectangles (J.\ Combin.\ Des.\ 2026).}  Theorem~1.6: for every
$\varepsilon,\eta>0$ there is $\gamma=\gamma(\varepsilon,\eta)>0$ such
that for $k<(\tfrac12-\varepsilon)n$ and a $\gamma n$-sparse partial
rectangle $P$ (at most $\gamma n$ entries per row, column and symbol)
with $\ell$ entries, $\Pr[P\subseteq L]=((1\pm\eta)/n)^\ell$; Lemma~3.1
is the one-cell conditional form $(1\pm\eta)/n$.  Proved by
``$2\ell$-cycle switches'' of Granet--Joos type: the forward degree is
controlled because the graph of free (column, symbol) pairs is an
almost-regular robust expander when $k<n/2$, so the number of long
alternating cycles through an edge is concentrated.  In a full square
there are no free pairs, so the method does not apply.  Their
Conjecture~1.5 (the same for all $k\le n$) and Kelly's spread conjecture
($\Pr[L\supseteq P]\le((e^2+o(1))/n)^{|P|}$ for every partial square
$P$, $k=n$) are the fixed-position statements for full squares; both
are open, and both are about \emph{sparse} prescribed sets.  Our (FC)
conditions on two full rows, which is dense: in spread language,
$\Pr_X[Q\subseteq L]=\Pr[R\cup Q\subseteq L]/\Pr[R\subseteq L]$, and a
one-sided spread bound for the numerator is useless without a matching
lower bound for $\Pr[R\subseteq L]=\CC_n(\lambda)/|\mathcal L_n|$ to a
factor $n^{O(1)}$ --- which is a (weak) form of the CGW conjecture
itself.  They note that sparsity is essentially necessary for the
two-sided statement.  (b) CGW cited for the $9n^{5/2}/2$ intercalate
bound (their Cor.~4.10, superseded by Kwan--Sudakov).  (c) No.

\emph{Upshot.}  The fixed-position estimates in the literature are
rectangle statements ($k<n/2$, where within-row swaps or long
alternating cycles are available) or conjectures for sparse prescribed
sets in full squares; the forward-degree problem for cycle switchings in
full squares with prescribed rows is named as difficult by Allsop and
Wanless and circumvented, not solved.  The right statement of our
obstacle in this language: \emph{(FC) for $O(\mathrm{polylog}\,n)$ cells
in two further rows of a uniform completion of a fixed $2\times n$
rectangle}, a dense-conditioning spread statement.  Nothing read changes
the status of any hypothesis or of the gap note's dependency list.

\paragraph{(n) Allsop--Morris 2026: $\eta$-switching is legal in $X$;
where their Theorem~3.1 does and does not transfer.}  (Classification:
analysis of a candidate tool from the literature; NEGATIVE KNOWLEDGE
on one step, with a precisely located remaining question.  Nothing
below is used by any result of ours yet.)  Allsop and Morris
\cite{AM26} prove, for a \emph{full} uniform Latin square and any
partial Latin square $P$ with $|R_P|\le\alpha n$ rows and
$|C_P|\le\beta n$ columns, $2\alpha+\beta<1$,
\[
(\delta/n)^{|P|}\le\Pr[L\supseteq P]\le(\Delta/n)^{|P|},\qquad
\delta\to1/23,\ \Delta\to23\ (\alpha,\beta\to0),
\]
(their Theorem~1.1; by Remark~1.2 any two of the three sets
$R_P,C_P,S_P$ may play the roles).  Two full rows have $|C_P|=n$ and
$|S_P|=n$, so our $R$ is outside the theorem.  The question is whether
the \emph{proof} applies to $P=R\cup Q$ with $Q$ sparse in rows $\ge3$,
i.e.\ whether the fixed-cell bound (FC) of \S(l) holds in $X$ (which,
by the isotopy invariance of \S(k), is the same as in $S(R)$ for events
whose prescribed symbols are read from rows $1,2$ at the mark).

\emph{The switching.}  For a cell $(r,c)$ and a second row $r'$, let
$s=L(r,c)$, $s'=L(r',c)$, $c'$ the column with $L(r,c')=s'$ and $r''$ the
row with $L(r'',c')=s$.  Their $\eta$-switch $\eta_L(r,r',c)$ replaces
$(r,c,s),(r',c,s'),(r,c',s')$ by $(r,c,s'),(r',c,s),(r,c',s)$ and
otherwise changes only rows $r'$ and $r''$ (Type One: $r''=r'$, an
intercalate flip; Type Two: two row-cycle switches; Type Three: a
cross-switch whose column part has length $2$).  Reversibility
(their Lemma~2.8): at most $n$ preimages if $\eta$ is of Type One, at
most one otherwise.  They note it needs the row $r''$ to exist, so it is
a tool for full squares only.  \emph{In $X$ (and in $S(R)$) it is legal whenever
$r,r',r''\notin\{1,2\}$}: rows $1,2$ are never touched, so $\sigma$, the
type and the mark are unchanged; the only cells of $P=R\cup Q$ it can
destroy are $Q$'s cells in row $r$ at column $c'$, which excludes at
most $|C_Q|$ choices of $r'$, and $r''\in\{1,2\}\cup R_Q$ excludes at
most $2+|R_Q|$ more (the maps $r'\mapsto c'\mapsto r''$ are injective);
and $r'\notin\{1,2\}\cup R_Q$ is required as well, since Types Two and
Three rewrite row $r'$ at many columns.  So the forward count of their Claim~1 and
Lemma~4.1 is $n-O(|Q|+j)$: the full-row conditioning costs nothing
here, because the switch displaces the target cell within its row by
two free rows and its column part (a cross-switch of column length
$2$ in Type Three) touches only those rows.  Two bookkeeping changes
are needed throughout: the excluded column set ``$C_P$'' (which in
their proof keeps structure columns $c_{j+1}$ away from cells of $P$)
becomes $C_Q\cup\{p,p'\}$, so that the sets $Y_j$ are defined with
$c_{j+1}\notin C_Q\cup\{p,p'\}\cup\{c_\ell\}$, and the excluded row set
becomes $\{1,2\}\cup R_Q$.

\emph{Which space.}  The analysis below is in $X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$
(type and mark conditioning), not in $S(R)$ with $R$ literally fixed:
in $S(R)$ no column turn may touch rows $1,2$, while in $X$ a turn of a
column pair $\{q,c\}\not\ni p,p'$ along a union of cycles meeting
$\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$ rows is legal (Lemma lem:legalturn).  This is
the space the application needs: the anchors
$L(x_1,p)=L(1,p')$, $L(y_1,p)=L(2,p')$ of \S(l) are cells whose
prescribed symbols are read from rows $1,2$ at columns $p,p'$, which no
legal move changes, so they behave as fixed cells throughout, and
$\Pr_X$ of the staircase events is what \S(l) bounds.

\emph{Their Theorem 3.1} ($\Pr[\eta_L(r,r',c)\text{ of Type One}\mid L\supseteq P]\le D/n$,
$D\to22$) is what turns Lemma~2.8 into a per-cell bound $\Delta/n$
(Lemma~4.1).  Its proof has four claims; we checked each in $X$.
Claim~1 (an $\eta$-switching recursion
$p_{j-1}\le(np_j+1)/(n(1+p_j-2\alpha-\beta)-3j-2)$): transfers with
the bookkeeping above, $\alpha=(2+|R_Q|)/n$, $\beta=(|C_Q|+2)/n$ (the
maps $x\mapsto y\mapsto x'$ are injective, so the exclusions cost
$2|R_P|+|C_P|+3j+3$ as in their count).  Claims~2 and~3
(cross-switches $\zeta(c_2,c_3,x_u,x_v)$ and row-cycle switches
$\rho(x_i,x_j,c_3)$ among the free rows $x_i$ of the column cycle
$\gamma_L=\gamma_L(c_2,c_3,r_1)$): the row-cycle switches are between
free rows, legal; the cross-switch exchanges columns $c_2,c_3$ in the
rows strictly between $x_u$ and $x_v$ on $\gamma_L$ (and changes
$x_u,x_v$ along a partial row cycle), so in $X$ it is legal iff that
segment contains both or neither of rows $1,2$ --- in their setting
automatic because $c_2,c_3\notin C_P$, in ours a restriction on the
pairs $(u,v)$ (e.g.\ to one arc of $\gamma_L$ between the rows $1,2$
lying on it, of which one holds at least a third of the free rows).
The restricted pair relation is no longer symmetric, so the double
count of Claim~2 has to be rewritten; by Remark~2.7 the rows $1,2$ keep
their positions on $\gamma_L$ under the cross-switch, so the arcs are
preserved.  We expect this to change the constants of Claims~2--3 by
a bounded factor and nothing else, but we have not rewritten it.
Claim~4 ($p_2=O(1/n)$): from $L\in X_3$ and a free row $x\notin\gamma_L$,
switch the row cycle $\rho_L(r_3,x,c_3)$ (legal); if that cycle hits
column $c_2$ they first switch the column cycle $\gamma_L(c_2,c_3,x)$
through $x$, which in $X$ is legal only if it meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$
or $2$ rows.  Repairs available in $X$: if neither of rows $1,2$ is
on $\gamma_L$, turn the union of the $(c_2,c_3)$-cycles through
$x$, $1$ and $2$ (legal as $c_2,c_3\notin\{p,p'\}$; avoids
$r_1,r_2,r_3$; inverse determined, since a turn preserves the cycle
partition and the case is read off the target); if both are on
$\gamma_L$, the cycle through $x$ avoids them.  \emph{The
one configuration we cannot handle}: exactly one of rows $1,2$ lies on
$\gamma_L$ (the cycle through $r_1$, which must not be turned because
$(r_1,c_2)$ is a structure cell) and the chosen $x$ lies on the cycle
of the other; then no legal column turn separates $c_2$ from $c_3$ in
$\rho_{r_3,x}$, and replacing the move by an $\eta$-switch brings back
the Type-One multiplicity $n$, i.e.\ the Claim~1 recursion that alone
gives only $p_0=O(n^{-1/2})$.  So in $X$ their argument gives
$\Pr_X[\text{Type One}\mid Q]=O(n^{-1/2})$ unconditionally (Claim~1
iterated to $j\asymp\sqrt n$, which they note is the best Claim~1 alone
can give; through Lemma~4.1 this is only $C/\sqrt n$ per cell, not
(FC)), and the $O(1/n)$ of Theorem~3.1 except that the forward count
of Claim~4 is reduced, for the sources in that configuration, by the
number of free rows on the $(c_2,c_3)$-cycle through the other of
rows $1,2$.  That loss must be controlled in aggregate, as their
$\Sigma_1$ is; a Claim~2--3-style averaging on that other cycle (row
switches between its free rows are legal and cut it, Lemma~2.4(1)) is
the natural attempt and has not been tried.  The question is about
the cycle structure of a square-selected column pair at rows $1,2$
--- our obstacle in miniature, but now confined to one step of an
otherwise working argument.

\emph{What (FC) in $X$ would give.}  By \S(l)(i), (FC) with constant
$\Delta$ for $Q$ with $\le2M+2$ cells in rows $3,4$ gives
$\Pr_X[\nu\le M]\le8\Delta^{2M+2}/n$, which is $o(1/\log n)$ for
$M\le(\log n)/(4\log\Delta)$; this is the first term of the first-pair
condition \eqref{eq:hypfirstpair}.  The second term (among the first
$M$ candidates not all are bad) is not a fixed-cell event and is not
reached by (FC); but $\eta$-switching is legal in $X$ with a
\emph{deterministic} forward degree $n-O(1)$ and changes row $r$ only
at $c,c'$.  (It is not rigid: Type One has up to $n$ preimages, which is
why Theorem~3.1 is needed.  A plain trade of row $r$ with a free row is
rigid with the same deterministic degree, but rewrites row $r$ along a
whole cycle, so it yields one factor $1/n$ per row and nothing for a
second prescribed cell in the same row; the confinement to $c,c'$ is
what $\eta$ adds.  The turns of \S(b)--(j) have random forward degrees.)
The question for the authors, assuming the transfer of Claims~2--3 is
written out: does Theorem~3.1 hold when $P$ contains two complete rows
that the switchings never touch, i.e.\ can Claim~4(ii) be repaired in
the configuration above?

\paragraph{(o) Kwan--Petrova--Sawhney 2025: parities, stable
intercalates, and three consequences for us.}  (Classification:
literature; two corrections to the gap note; one program sketch,
not a result.)  \cite{KPS25} prove Cameron's conjecture: the row
parities of a uniform Latin square are asymptotically $n$ independent
fair coins, jointly with column and symbol parities subject to the
Janssen--Zappa constraint (their Theorem~1.3), via a new approximation
lemma (Lemma~4.7: a property of the triangle-removal process holding
with probability $1-\varepsilon$ holds for a random subset of a random
Latin square with probability $1-\varepsilon e^{O(n\log^2n)}$) and a
``stable intercalate'' re-randomisation: a canonical collection
$I_T(L)$ of disjoint intercalates, invariant under switching any
subset of itself, so that switching a uniformly random subset leaves
$L$ uniform and re-randomises the parities through linear algebra over
$\mathbb F_2$; the collection is shown (Lemma~8.3) to meet every
large-enough set of rows, columns and symbols simultaneously, with
overwhelming probability.

(i) \emph{Gap note.}  Theorem~1.3(4) contains the Cavenagh--Wanless
parity theorem as a special case, so that theorem is true independently
of the gap; but the written proof of Theorem~1.3(3)--(4) uses
Cavenagh--Wanless as a black box for the mod-$2$ equidistribution, with
Remark~6.6 sketching an easy independent re-proof by switching a
$2\times3$ and a $3\times2$ subrectangle.  So the formal dependence on
the gap runs CGW $\to$ CW16 $\to$ KPS Theorem~1.3(3)--(4), and is
presumably removable (we have not checked the sketch).  Their written
deduction of Theorem~1.3(1)--(2) goes through (4), hence also through
CW16; but Theorem~1.3(5) (the large deviation principle) is deduced
from their Theorem~6.4 and Lemma~6.5(ii) (Sanov) with no use of CW16,
and the rate function has its unique zero at $(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$,
so the law of large numbers (1) follows from (5) without CW16.  Hence,
by row exchangeability,
$\Pr[\text{rows }1,2\text{ have the same parity}]=\tfrac12+o(1)$
independently of the gap, superseding CGW's Theorem~4.3 (which gave
$[\tfrac14,\tfrac34]$ and is affected by the gap).  (The earlier
version of this paragraph attributed (1) to the \S2.4 sketch of
\cite{KPS25}; the cold read of 2026-10-03 pointed out that the written
proof routes (1) through (4).)

(ii) \emph{The parity marginal of the weak conjecture.}  Rows $1,2$
have the same parity iff $\sigma=\rho_{1,2}$ is even.  So KPS prove
$\Pr_n[\sigma\text{ even}]=\tfrac12+o(1)$, while under $\QQ_n$ (uniform
derangements) the same holds; i.e.\ $d_{TV}$ between the parity
marginals of $\PP_n$ and $\QQ_n$ tends to $0$.  This is the first
rigorous instance of the weak conjecture for a nontrivial functional
of the type, and it should be stated in the paper.  It does not bear
on adjacent pairs $\lambda,\mu$ (which have opposite parities), so it
gives nothing for $\bar q(\mu\to\lambda)$ individually, only for the
global average $\sum_{\lambda\text{ even}}\PP_n(\lambda)\to\tfrac12$.

(iii) \emph{A route around frame selection.}  The KPS method is our
orbit method with the two missing inputs supplied from outside: a
canonical, switch-invariant family of independent toggles is exactly
the structure of Proposition prop:orbit / prop:adaptive; what they can
prove and we cannot is that the family is rich \emph{at every set of
rows simultaneously} (so in particular at square-selected rows) ---
because a property that holds for all row sets at once is a single
event of overwhelming probability, which the approximation lemma
transfers.  For our problem one would need: (a) the approximation lemma
for completions of a fixed rectangle $R$ (plausible: the row-by-row
side of their proof can start from $R$; not checked); (b) a stable
family of toggles acting on the flippability bits, where a toggle at
rows $(x_k,w)$, columns $(q,c)$ acts on $b_k$ iff $(q,c)$ is separated
by the mark in $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$ --- so the genericity input $(\beta)$
reappears, now as the question whether among the many stable
intercalates at row $x_k$ some have a separated column pair; (c)
possibly the re-randomisation itself to make $(\beta)$ hold: switching
stable intercalates in rows $x_k$ or $y_k$ changes $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$ by
transpositions and could randomise the arc structure at the mark.  This
is a program for people who own that machinery, not a result; we
record it as the most concrete external route we have seen to the
frame-selection half of the obstacle.  (The $n$-cycle question of \S4 of
the gap note is not reached by it either: $\Pr[(n)]\approx1/n$ is not
an overwhelming-probability property.)

\paragraph{(p) What the conjecture is not needed for: the multiplication
group of a random loop (user-directed diversion, 2026-10-03).}
(Classification: PROVED, unconditional; a counting argument whose
first step and whose treatment of $S_{n/2}\wr S_2$ were sketched by
S.~Eberhard in comments on Cameron's blog \cite{Cam15}; the block-system
count for general imprimitive groups and the union over primitive
groups below are what make it a proof.  It does not touch the
conjecture or the gap.)  CGW's Lemma~6.2 records a consequence of
Conjecture~6.1 communicated by Cameron: that the rows of almost all
reduced Latin squares --- the left translations of almost all loops ---
generate $S_n$, so that almost all loops have trivial character theory
in the sense of Smith.  For quasigroups (unreduced squares) this is
Cameron's theorem \cite{Cam92}: the first row of a uniform square is a
uniform permutation, and by {\L}uczak--Pyber \cite{LP93} a uniform
permutation lies in no transitive proper subgroup other than $A_n$ with
probability $1-o(1)$.  For loops the first row is the identity and the
second row is not a uniform derangement, which is where
Conjecture~6.1 enters; Cameron \cite{Cam15} states the loop case as
open and routes it through the second-row conjecture (the 2016
comment thread contains Eberhard's sketch, which nobody there wrote
out or disputed).  The owner's question was whether the loop
statement is easier than the conjecture.  It is: it needs no
information about the law of the second row at all.  Throughout, a
``uniform random loop'' is uniform on labelled loops with identity
element $1$, i.e.\ on reduced squares, as in \cite{CGW08}.

\emph{Setting.}  Let $L$ be a uniform Latin square of order $n$ with rows
$r_1,\dots,r_n\in S_n$ (column $\mapsto$ symbol), and let
$\sigma_i=r_1^{-1}r_i$, so that $(\sigma_1=\mathrm{id},\sigma_2,\dots,\sigma_n)$
are the rows of the uniform square $L^0$ with first row the identity
obtained by relabelling symbols.  Permuting the rows of $L^0$ so that
its first column is the identity gives a uniform reduced square (each
reduced square arises from exactly $(n-1)!$ squares $L^0$) with the
same set of rows; so $H(L):=\langle\sigma_2,\dots,\sigma_n\rangle\le S_n$
is the left multiplication group of a uniform random loop of order
$n$.  $H(L)$ is transitive: for columns $c,s$ some row has
$\sigma_i(c)=s$.  Let $\mathcal L_n$ be the set of Latin squares of
order $n$ and $L(n)=|\mathcal L_n|$; then $|\{L^0\}|=L(n)/n!$.

\begin{proposition}[the multiplication group of a random loop]\label{prop:loops}
$\Pr[H(L)\not\supseteq A_n]\le e^{-(\log2-o(1))n^2}$.  Consequently, by
H\"aggkvist--Janssen \cite{HJ96}, $\Pr[H(L)=S_n]\ge1-3c^n$ for some
$c<1$ and all large $n$; since the full multiplication group
$\mathrm{Mlt}(Q)$ contains $H(L)$, almost all loops have
$\mathrm{Mlt}(Q)=S_n$, which is $2$-transitive, so by Smith's theory
their character theory is trivial: the conclusion of
\cite[Lemma~6.2]{CGW08} holds unconditionally.
\end{proposition}

The proof uses four external inputs: van der Waerden's permanent bound
in the form $L(n)\ge(n!)^{2n}/n^{n^2}$ and Br\'egman's in the form
$L(a)\le\prod_{k=1}^{a}(k!)^{a/k}$ \cite[Ch.~17]{vLW}; the theorem of
Praeger and Saxl \cite{PS80} that a primitive permutation group of
degree $n$ not containing $A_n$ has order less than $4^n$; and Pyber's
bound \cite{Pyb93} that $S_n$ has at most $2^{O(n^2)}$ subgroups (now
$2^{n^2/16+O(n^{3/2})}$, \cite{RT25}).  From the first two,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lbounds}
\log L(n)\ge n^2\log n-2n^2,\qquad
\log L(a)\le a^2\log a-2a^2+a\bigl(\tfrac12\log^2a+2\log a+3\bigr)
\end{equation}
for all $n,a\ge1$: the first from $n!\ge(n/e)^n$; the second from
$\log k!\le k\log k-k+\log k+1$, which gives
$\sum_{k\le a}(\log k!)/k\le\sum_{k\le a}(\log k-1)+\sum_{k\le a}(\log k+1)/k
\le(a\log a-a+\log a+1)-a+(\tfrac12\log^2a+1)+(\log a+1)$.

\begin{proof}
Since $H=H(L)$ is transitive, $H\not\supseteq A_n$ means that $H$ is
imprimitive, or primitive with $|H|<4^n$ \cite{PS80}.

\emph{Imprimitive.}  Let $\Pi$ be a partition of $[n]$ into $b$ blocks
$C_1,\dots,C_b$ of size $a$, $1<a<n$, $ab=n$, and suppose every
$\sigma_i$ preserves $\Pi$: every row of $L^0$ maps $\Pi$ to $\Pi$.  We
count, more generally, the Latin squares all of whose rows map $\Pi$ to
a fixed symbol partition $\Pi'$ with blocks $S_1,\dots,S_b$ (we need
only $\Pi'=\Pi$).  Each row $r_i$ of such a square induces a bijection
$\pi_i\colon[b]\to[b]$, $r_i(C_j)=S_{\pi_i(j)}$.  Fix $j,j'$ and let
$I_{jj'}=\{i:\pi_i(j)=j'\}$.  In a column $c\in C_j$ the $a$ symbols
of $S_{j'}$ each occur once, and they occur only in rows of
$I_{jj'}$, each of which contributes one symbol of $S_{j'}$ to column
$c$; hence $|I_{jj'}|=a$, and the subarray $I_{jj'}\times C_j$ is an
$a\times a$ Latin square on the symbols $S_{j'}$.  Conversely any
choice of an $n\times b$ array $(\pi_i(j))$ whose rows are permutations
of $[b]$ and whose columns contain each value exactly $a$ times,
together with one Latin square of order $a$ for each of the $b^2$
pairs $(j,j')$ (on the row set $I_{jj'}$, column set $C_j$ and symbol
set $S_{j'}$, all now determined), is a Latin square all of whose rows
map $\Pi$ to $\Pi'$.  So the number of such squares is at most
$(b!)^n\,L(a)^{b^2}$; the squares $L^0$ with $H\le\mathrm{Stab}(\Pi)$
are among those with $\Pi'=\Pi$, and there are $L(n)/n!$ squares
$L^0$, so
\[
\Pr\bigl[H\le\mathrm{Stab}(\Pi)\bigr]
\le\frac{(b!)^n\,L(a)^{b^2}}{L(n)/n!}.
\]
There are $n!/((a!)^bb!)\le n!$ partitions $\Pi$ with blocks of size
$a$, and fewer than $n$ divisors $a$.  By \eqref{eq:Lbounds}, with
$b=n/a$ and $(b!)^n\le b^{nb}$,
\[
\log\frac{n!\,(n!)\,(b!)^nL(a)^{b^2}}{L(n)}
\le 2n\log n+\frac{n^2}{a}\log b+\Bigl(n^2\log a-2n^2+\frac{n^2}{a}\bigl(\tfrac12\log^2a+2\log a+3\bigr)\Bigr)-n^2\log n+2n^2
\]
\[
=-n^2\log b+\frac{n^2}{a}\Bigl(\log b+\tfrac12\log^2a+2\log a+3\Bigr)+2n\log n .
\]
If $a\ge\sqrt n$ the middle term is $O(n^{3/2}\log^2n)$ and the bound
is $-n^2\log b+o(n^2)\le-n^2\log2+o(n^2)$.  If $2\le a<\sqrt n$ then
$b>\sqrt n$, $(n^2/a)\log b\le\tfrac12n^2\log b$ and
$(\tfrac12\log^2a+2\log a+3)/a\le\tfrac52$ (the maximum is at $a=2$), so the
bound is $\le-\tfrac12n^2\log b+\tfrac52n^2+2n\log n\le-\tfrac14n^2\log n+3n^2$,
which is $\le-n^2\log2$ for $n\ge e^{15}$.  Summing over $\Pi$ and
$a$: $\Pr[H\text{ imprimitive}]\le n\,e^{-(\log2-o(1))n^2}$, the
$o(1)$ being $O(\log^2n/n)$ from $a=n/2$ (numerically the bound is
vacuous below $n\approx100$ and reaches $e^{-0.61n^2}$ at $n=10^3$).

\emph{Primitive.}  For a fixed subgroup $K\le S_n$, the squares $L^0$
with all rows in $K$ number at most $|K|^{n-1}$.  Summing over the
subgroups $K$ with $|K|<4^n$, of which there are at most $2^{Cn^2}$
\cite{Pyb93},
\[
\Pr\bigl[H\text{ primitive},\ H\not\supseteq A_n\bigr]
\le\frac{2^{Cn^2}\,4^{n(n-1)}}{L(n)/n!}
\le\exp\bigl(-n^2\log n+(C\log2+\log4+2)n^2+n\log n\bigr),
\]
which is $e^{-(1-o(1))n^2\log n}$.  Adding the two cases gives the
first claim.  For the second: $H\le A_n$ iff every $\sigma_i$ is even
iff all rows of $L$ have the parity of $r_1$, an event of probability
$2\Pr[L\text{ all-even}]\le2c^n$ for some $c<1$ \cite{HJ96}
(relabelling symbols by a transposition is a bijection between
all-even and all-odd squares); and $H\supseteq A_n$, $H\not\le A_n$
force $H=S_n$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Remarks.}  (i) Nothing about the law of the second row is used.
The event $H\le K$ is the intersection of the $n-1$ events
$\sigma_i\in K$, $i\ge2$ (the identity row lies in every subgroup and
contributes nothing --- which is exactly why Cameron's one-row argument
is unavailable for loops); the strength of the bound
($e^{-\Omega(n^2)}$ against the $n^{-c}$ of a {\L}uczak--Pyber argument)
comes from these $n-1$ conditions being imposed simultaneously.  The
crude count $|K|^{n-1}$ against $L(n)/n!=(n!)^{n-1}e^{-n^2+o(n^2)}$
settles every $K$ of index $\ge e^{(1+\delta)n}$; for the two families
of smaller index, $A_n$ and $S_{n/2}\wr S_2$, one must use the Latin
constraints inside $K$ (the block decomposition, which shows the
structured squares pay a larger Latin-constraint cost, and
H\"aggkvist--Janssen).  This is the ``many rows'' slack of the owner's
question in its purest form, and it is not available for
Conjecture~6.1, which concerns one row given the identity row.  (ii) The same argument with $G=\langle r_1,\dots,r_n\rangle$
in place of $H$ (and $\Pi'=\Pi$) reproves Cameron's quasigroup theorem
\cite{Cam92} without {\L}uczak--Pyber, with the same error bound.
(iii) Eberhard's comments \cite{Cam15} contain the first step
--- $|K|^n$ against $L(n)=(n!)^n/e^{n^2+o(n^2)}$, so that a single $K$
of index $\ge e^{(1+\delta)n}$ contributes $e^{-\delta n^2+o(n^2)}$ ---
followed by ``thus we need only worry about'' $A_n$ and
$S_{n/2}\wr S_2$, and a row-by-row Br\'egman argument for the latter.
The ``thus'' is a gap: the union over the $e^{\Theta(n^2)}$
subgroups of index between $e^{n}$ and $e^{(1+\delta)n}$ (which
include $S_{n/3}\wr S_3$, $S_{n/4}\wr S_4,\dots$) is not addressed
there.  The block-system count above handles every imprimitive $K$ at
once (by a different method from his), and the Praeger--Saxl and Pyber
bounds are what make the union over primitive $K$ legitimate.
(iv) Data (\texttt{s13\_loopgroup.py}; \texttt{runs/s13/loops/}): among
the $56$ reduced squares of order $5$, $50$ have $H=S_5$ and $6$ have
$H=C_5$ (the six cyclic subgroups of $S_5$, each the row set of exactly
one reduced square, the sorted Cayley table of $\mathbb Z_5$; a
sharply transitive set containing the identity inside $D_5$ or $F_{20}$
must avoid every element with a fixed point, so no other proper
transitive group occurs; these six are also the all-even reduced
squares of order $5$); among the $9408$ of order $6$, $7776$ have
$S_6$, $72$ have $A_6$, and $1560$ ($16.6\%$) are imprimitive (block
sizes $2$ and $3$, orders $6$ to $48$); in Jacobson--Matthews samples
(regenerated 2026-10-09 with recorded parameters: $3000$ squares at
every $7\le n\le16$, thinning $20n^2$, burn-in $200\cdot20n^2$, proper
states at a fixed grid; \texttt{runs/s13/loops/sample*.log}) the
number of squares with $H=A_n$ was $37,25,18,4,1,4,0,1,0,0$ for
$n=7,\dots,16$ and every other $H$ was $S_n$ except two squares at
$n=8$ with $H=S_2\wr S_4$ (the earlier samples, with unrecorded
thinning, had $63,37,13,3$ at $n=7,8,9,10$ and $S_n$ only at $n=12,16$).1\%,1.2\%,0.4\%,0.1\%$ and then
$0$.  (v) Classification and cost: PROVED, unconditional, but a

\paragraph{(q) The Allsop--Morris per-cell bound with two complete rows:
the $\nu$-tail of the first-pair hypothesis is $O(\Delta^{2M}/n)$.}
(Classification: PROVED (two referee passes; the first found that
Claim~$2'$ had been stated per arc, which is false, and must be stated
in aggregate --- corrected below with no change in constants); a
one-way reduction of Hypothesis~\eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} to its second
term.  Written 2026-10-03, as the in-house item ``write out Claims~2--3''
of the cold read; in writing them out, the configuration of \S(n) in
which Claim~4(ii) failed turned out to be avoidable.)  Throughout this
section the setting is $S(R)$: a fixed $2\times n$ Latin rectangle $R$
occupying rows $1,2$, a partial Latin square $Q$ with cells in rows
other than $1,2$ such that $R\cup Q$ is a partial Latin square, and $L$
uniform on $\{L\in\mathcal L_n: L\supseteq R\cup Q\}$.  Nothing moves
rows $1,2$; the mark plays no role.  We follow the notation of
\cite[\S\S2--4]{AM26} and refer to their lemmas by number.  Call a row
\emph{special} if it is $1$ or $2$, and write
$E=\{1,2\}\cup R_Q$ for the excluded rows.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{AM26}, Theorem~3.1, with two complete rows]\label{thm:AM31two}
Let $R,Q$ be as above with $|R_Q|+6\le\alpha n$, $|C_Q|\le\beta n$ and
$2\alpha+\beta<1$.  Let $r,r'\notin\{1,2\}$, $r\ne r'$, $c\in[n]$, and
suppose $Q$ has no cell in row $r'$ outside column $c$ (so $r'\in R_Q$
is allowed only through a cell $(r',c,\cdot)\in Q$; this is what the
proof of the per-cell bound below needs).  Then, for
$n\ge n_0(\alpha,\beta)$,
\[
\Pr\bigl[\eta_L(r,r',c)\text{ is of Type One}\;\big|\;L\supseteq R\cup Q\bigr]\le\frac{D}{n},
\qquad D=D(\alpha,\beta)\to62\text{ as }\alpha,\beta\to0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

The constant is not optimised; what matters is that it is absolute.
The proof is that of \cite{AM26} with the following changes, which we
now describe in full: the row-exclusion set $R_P$ becomes $E$ and the
column-exclusion set $C_P$ becomes $C_Q$ (the cells of $R$ are never
touched because no switch below changes rows $1,2$); the cross-switches
of their Claim~2 and the row switches of their Claim~3 are restricted
to pairs of rows lying in a common \emph{arc} (defined below), so that
no column part of a switch passes through a special row; and the
statistic $\nu(L)$ of their Claims~3--4 is replaced by one that also
counts the free rows on the $(c_2,c_3)$-cycles through the special
rows, so that the column-cycle switch of their Claim~4 is always
applied to a cycle avoiding rows $1,2$.

\emph{The recursion (their p.~14) and Claim~1.}  Define $Y_0$, $r_j,c_j,s_j$,
$X_j$, $Y_j$, $p_{j-1}$ exactly as in \cite{AM26}, with
$R_P:=E$ and $C_P:=C_Q$ in the definition of $Y_j$ (so
$r_{j+1}\notin E\cup\{r_\ell\}$, $c_{j+1}\notin C_Q\cup\{c_\ell\}$).
Claim~1, $p_{j-1}\le(np_j+1)/(n(1+p_j-2\alpha-\beta)-3j-2)$, holds
verbatim: its switch $\eta_L(r_j,x,c_j)$ changes only rows $r_j,x,x'$,
all outside $\{1,2\}$ when $x,x'\notin E$, so rows $1,2$ are untouched
and $L'\supseteq R$; the cells of $Q$ it could touch are $(r_j,y)$ with
$y\in C_Q$, $(r_j,c_j)$ (not a $Q$-cell: $c_j\notin C_Q$ for $j\ge2$,
and for $j=1$ the only $Q$-cell allowed in row $r_1$ is in column
$c_0\ne c_1$) and cells in rows $x,x'\in R_Q$, excluded exactly as in
their count ($|C_Q|+j+1$ choices of $x$ with $y\in C_Q\cup\{c_\ell\}$,
$|E|+j+1$ with $x'\in E\cup\{r_\ell\}$, by injectivity of
$x\mapsto y\mapsto x'$); the reversibility count (their Lemma~2.8) is a
statement about Latin squares and is unchanged.  So
$|Y_j|/|X_j|\ge(n-2|E|-|C_Q|-3j-3)/(np_j+1)$, which is their (3)--(4)
with $\alpha n\ge|E|+1$, $\beta n\ge|C_Q|$.

\emph{Setting for Claims~2--4.}  For $L\in X_3$ let $Q(L)$ be the set of
cells of $Q$ together with the ten structure cells
$\{(r_\ell,c_\ell),(r_{\ell+1},c_\ell),(r_\ell,c_{\ell+1}):0\le\ell\le2\}\cup\{(r_3,c_3)\}$;
a row is \emph{free} if it is not in $E\cup\{r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3\}$, and
$f\ge n-|E|-4\ge n(1-\alpha)$ is the number of free rows.  (The row
$r_0$ need not be free and may lie on any cycle; its cells
$(r_0,c_2),(r_0,c_3)$ are not in $Q(L)$ and are never read by the
recursion, which reads only the ten structure cells, so a switch may
change them.)
Consider the partition of the rows into $(c_2,c_3)$-column cycles.  Three
facts from \cite{AM26} remain true: $\gamma_L=\gamma_L(c_2,c_3,r_1)$ is
one of these cycles; $\{r_2,r_3\}$ is another (an intercalate, since
$s_2=s_3$); and $c_2,c_3\notin C_Q$.  Call a cycle \emph{active} if it
is $\gamma_L$ or contains a special row (so there are between one and
three active cycles, and $\{r_2,r_3\}$ is never active); let
$\mathcal S(L)$ be the set of free rows on active cycles and
$g(L)=f-|\mathcal S(L)|$ the number of free rows on inactive cycles.
Every inactive cycle other than $\{r_2,r_3\}$ avoids rows $1,2$ and
the rows $r_1,r_2,r_3$, so by Lemma~2.4 of \cite{AM26} (or directly)
switching it changes no cell of $Q(L)$ and no cell of $R$.

Give each active cycle $D$ a \emph{reference row}: $r_1$ if $D=\gamma_L$,
otherwise row $1$ if $1\in D$, otherwise row $2$; list the rows of $D$
as $x'_1,x'_2,\dots,x'_m$ in cyclic order from the reference row
$x'_1$ (the order of \cite[p.~16]{AM26}, read from column $c_2$), and
let the free rows of $D$ in this order be $x_1,\dots,x_d$.  The
special rows of $D$ other than the reference row (at most two) cut the
position sequence into \emph{arcs}; a pair $(i,j)$, $i<j$, of free
positions is a \emph{same-arc pair} if no special row and no
reference row lies strictly between $x_i$ and $x_j$ in the list.  The
free rows of $D$ are the disjoint union of the arcs' free rows; an
active cycle has at most three arcs, and all active cycles together at
most three (if $\gamma_L$ contains both special rows there is no other
active cycle; if it contains one, the other special row's cycle has
one arc; if none, the special rows lie on one cycle with two arcs or
on two cycles with one arc each).  As in \cite{AM26}, a triple
$(L,i,j)$ is \emph{good} if $\rho_L(x_i,x_j,c_3)$ does not hit column
$c_2$.

\emph{Claim~$2'$ (good same-arc pairs, in aggregate).}  For $a\ge4$ let
$\mathcal A_a$ be the set of triples $(L,D,A)$ with $L\in X_3$, $D$ an
active cycle of $L$ and $A$ an arc of $D$ with exactly $a$ free rows,
and $N_a=|\mathcal A_a|$.  Then
\[
\sum_{(L,D,A)\in\mathcal A_a}\#\{\text{good pairs }(i,j)\text{ with }x_i,x_j\in A\}
\ \ge\ N_a\,\frac{a(a-1)(a-3)}{9(a-2)}\ \ge\ N_a\,\frac{(a-3)^2}{9}.
\]
(The statement is about the class $\mathcal A_a$, exactly as their
Claim~2 is about $A_k$: a single arc can have no good pair at all, and
on the sampled squares below some do.)

\emph{Proof.}  This is the proof of their Claim~2 run inside the arcs
of size $a$.
For $(i,j)$ in $A$ let $W_A(i,j)$ be the pairs $(u,v)$ in $A$ with
exactly one of $u,v$ in $(i,j)$ and $\{u,v\}\cap\{i,j\}=\emptyset$;
$|W_A(i,j)|=(j-i-1)(a-(j-i)-1)$ in arc coordinates, so
$\sum_{(i,j)\subseteq A}|W_A(i,j)|=a(a-1)(a-2)(a-3)/12$ and
$\max|W_A|\le(a-2)^2/4$ (their (5)--(6) with $n-k$ replaced by $a$).
For a bad $(L,i_0,j_0)$ in $A$ and $(u,v)\in W_A(i_0,j_0)$: if
$(L,u,v)$ is good, map to $(L,u,v)$ (Kind~1); if bad, cross-switch on
$\zeta_L(c_2,c_3,x_u,x_v)$ (Kind~2).  The cross-switch is well defined
(the pair is bad and $u,v$ are not adjacent in the list, since a
position of $\{i_0,j_0\}$ separates them); it changes rows $x_u,x_v$,
which are free, and columns $c_2,c_3$ in the rows strictly between
$x_u$ and $x_v$ in the list, which are rows of $D$ that are neither
special nor the reference row (the pair is same-arc) nor $r_2,r_3$ (not
on $D$); such rows may include $r_0$ or rows of $R_Q$, whose cells in
columns $c_2,c_3\notin C_Q$ are not in $Q(L)\cup R$; hence it changes no
cell of $R$ and no cell of $Q(L)$, and $L'\in X_3$ with the
same $r_\ell,c_\ell,s_\ell$.  By their Lemma~2.6 the row set of $D$ is
unchanged, by their Remark~2.7 the list of $L'$ is that of $L$ with
the segment strictly between $x_u$ and $x_v$ reversed, so the special
and reference rows keep their positions, $A$ is an arc of $L'$ with
the same row set, $(u,v)\in W_A(i',j')$ for the new positions
$(i',j')$ of $x_{i_0},x_{j_0}$, and $(L',i',j')$ is good by
Lemma~2.6(2); so $(L',D',A')\in\mathcal A_a$ with the same row sets.
The inverse counts are as in \cite{AM26}: a good $(L',D',A',i',j')$ has
at most $|W_{A'}(i',j')|$ Kind-1 preimages ($(i,j)\in W_{A'}(i',j')$,
which is symmetric) and at most $|W_{A'}(i',j')|$ Kind-2 preimages (one
for each $(u,v)\in W_{A'}(i',j')$, by their Lemma~2.5 and Remark~2.7,
the inverse cross-switch again lying inside $A'$).  Summing over
$\mathcal A_a$: $\sum_{\text{bad}}|W|\le2\sum_{\text{good}}|W|$, whence
$N_a\,a(a-1)(a-2)(a-3)/12=\sum_{\text{all}}|W|\le3\sum_{\text{good}}|W|\le3\cdot\#\{\text{good}\}\cdot(a-2)^2/4$.  $\square$

\emph{Claim~$3''$ (free rows off the active cycles).}
$\sum_{L\in X_3}g(L)\ge f|X_3|/30$ for $f\ge501$.

\emph{Proof.}  Double count the row switches $\rho_L(x_i,x_j,c_3)$ over
$L\in X_3$, active cycles $D$ of $L$ and good same-arc pairs $(i,j)$ on
$D$.  \emph{Forward.}  Such a switch exchanges the entries of the free
rows $x_i,x_j$ on the columns of their row cycle through $c_3$, which
does not contain $c_2$; it changes no cell of $R$ or $Q(L)$, so
$L'\in X_3$ with the same $r_\ell,c_\ell,s_\ell$ and the same columns
$c_2,c_3$.  The $(c_2,c_3)$-column permutation changes only at rows
$x_i,x_j$ (whose $c_2$-entries are unchanged and whose $c_3$-entries
are exchanged), so every cycle other than $D$ is unchanged and $D$
splits into two cycles (their Lemma~2.4(1); directly: the new column
permutation is the old one composed with the transposition of the two
exchanged symbols, which splits the cycle): one consists of the rows of
the list outside the segment between $x_i$ and $x_j$ together with
$x_i$, and contains the reference row and every special row of $D$;
the other, $S$, consists of the rows strictly between $x_i$ and $x_j$
in the list together with $x_j$ (with the list read from column $c_2$
as above), and contains no special row because $(i,j)$ is a same-arc
pair.  Hence the active
cycles of $L'$ are those of $L$ with $D$ replaced by $D\setminus S$,
$S$ is inactive in $L'$ ($S$ contains neither $r_1$ nor a special row,
so it is not $\gamma_{L'}$: if $D=\gamma_L$ then $\gamma_{L'}=D\setminus S\ni r_1$,
otherwise $\gamma_{L'}=\gamma_L$), $x_j\in S$ and $x_i\in D\setminus S$,
and $g(L')=g(L)+|S\cap\text{free}|$.  Let $F(L)$ be the number of such
switches from $L$ (pairs $(D,(i,j))$; distinct pairs of rows give
distinct switches, and a pair of rows lies on one cycle).  Summing
Claim~$2'$ over $a$, with $h(a)=((a-3)_+)^2/9$,
\[
\sum_{L\in X_3}F(L)\ \ge\ \sum_{a\ge4}\ \sum_{(L,D,A)\in\mathcal A_a}\#\{\text{good pairs in }A\}
\ \ge\ \sum_{a\ge4}N_ah(a)\ =\ \sum_{L\in X_3}\ \sum_{A}h(|A|),
\]
and for each $L$, since the arcs of the active cycles partition
$\mathcal S(L)$ into at most three parts,
$\sum_Ah(|A|)\ge\frac1{27}(\sum_A(|A|-3)_+)^2\ge\frac1{27}((f-g(L)-9)_+)^2$.
\emph{Backward.}  If $L'$ arises from $(L,D,i,j)$ then $L$ is obtained
from $L'$ by switching $\rho_{L'}(x_i,x_j,c_3)$ (their Fact~2.1), so
$L$ is determined by the unordered pair $\{x_i,x_j\}$, in which one
row lies in $\mathcal S(L')$ and the other in $S$, an inactive cycle
of $L'$; the number of preimages of $L'$ is therefore at most
$|\mathcal S(L')|\,g(L')\le f\,g(L')$.  \emph{Conclusion.}  With
$\bar g=|X_3|^{-1}\sum g(L)$, Jensen's inequality on the convex function
$t\mapsto((f-t-9)_+)^2$ gives
$|X_3|\,((f-\bar g-9)_+)^2/27\le\sum_L\sum_Ah(|A|)\le\sum_LF(L)\le f\sum_{L'}g(L')=f|X_3|\bar g$.
If $\bar g<f/30$ then $(f-\bar g-9)^2>(29f/30-9)^2\ge27f\cdot f/30=0.9f^2$
for $f\ge501$, a contradiction.  $\square$

\emph{Claim~$4'$.}  $p_2\le60/f\le60/(n(1-\alpha))$ for $f\ge501$.

\emph{Proof.}  Their proof with the set of admissible rows $x$ changed.
For $L\in X_3$ let $x$ be a free row on an inactive cycle ($g(L)$
choices).  Let $\rho=\rho_L(r_3,x,c_3)$.  If $\rho$ does not hit column
$c_2$, switch on $\rho$ to obtain $L'$; otherwise first switch on the
$(c_2,c_3)$-cycle $\gamma_L(c_2,c_3,x)$ through $x$ to obtain $L''$, then
on $\rho_{L''}(r_3,x,c_3)$ to obtain $L'$.  The cycle through $x$ is
inactive, so it avoids rows $1,2$, $r_1$ (on $\gamma_L$) and $r_2,r_3$
(the intercalate), and $c_2,c_3\notin C_Q$: switching it changes no
cell of $R$ or $Q(L)$, so $L''\in X_3$ with the same data, and
$\rho_{L''}(r_3,x,c_3)$ does not hit $c_2$ (their Lemma~2.3(1); directly:
the switch replaces the row permutation of $(x,r_3)$ by its composition
with the transposition of $L[x,c_2],L[x,c_3]$, two symbols of one
cycle, which splits that cycle with the two symbols, hence the columns
$c_2,c_3$, on different cycles).  In either case
the final row switch involves the free rows $r_3,x$ (both outside
$\{1,2\}\cup R_Q$) along a row cycle avoiding $c_2$, so it changes no
cell of $R$ or of $Q(L)\setminus\{(r_3,c_3)\}$ and gives
$L'\in Y_2\setminus X_3$, exactly as in \cite{AM26}.  So there are at
least $\sum_Lg(L)\ge f|X_3|/30$ switches from $X_3$ to $Y_2\setminus X_3$.
Their inverse count applies unchanged: from $L'$ the row $r''$ with
$L'[r'',c_3]=s_2$ equals $x$, and in each of the two cases $L$ is
determined, by switching back $\rho_{L'}(r_3,r'',c_3)$ and, in the
second case, then the $(c_2,c_3)$-cycle of $L''$ through $r''$, which is
the same set of rows as before; so each $L'$ has at most $2$ preimages.
Hence $|Y_2\setminus X_3|\ge f|X_3|/60$ and
$p_2=|X_3|/|Y_2|\le1/(1+f/60)\le60/f$.  $\square$

\emph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:AM31two}.}  Claim~1 twice from
$p_2\le60/(n(1-\alpha))$, as in their (16): with
$\phi_j(x)=(nx+1)/(n(1+x-2\alpha-\beta)-3j-2)$ non-decreasing for
$j=o(n)$, $p_1\le\phi_2(p_2)\le(60/(1-\alpha)+1)/(n(1-2\alpha-\beta)-8)$
and $p_0\le\phi_1(p_1)\le(np_1+1)/(n(1-2\alpha-\beta)-5)$, so
$p_0\le D(\alpha,\beta)/n$ with
$D(\alpha,\beta)=(60/(1-\alpha)+2+o(1))/(1-2\alpha-\beta)^2\to62$.  $\square$

\begin{corollary}[per-cell bound and (FC) in $S(R)$]\label{cor:FCtwo}
With $R,Q$ as in the theorem, for a row $r\notin\{1,2\}$ (in $R_Q$ or
not) and any $(c,s)$ with no cell $(r,c,\cdot)$ in $Q$,
$\Pr[L[r,c]=s\mid L\supseteq R\cup Q]\le\Delta/n$ with
$\Delta=(D+1)/(1-2\alpha-\beta)+o(1)\le66$ for $\alpha,\beta$ small; consequently, for every
partial Latin square $Q$ on rows other than $1,2$ with
$|R_Q|,|C_Q|\le\varepsilon n$ ($\varepsilon$ small),
\[
\Pr\bigl[Q\subseteq L\;\big|\;L\supseteq R\bigr]\le\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{n}\Bigr)^{|Q|}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\emph{Proof.}  The first statement is \cite[Lemma~4.1]{AM26} with
$\mathcal R=\{r\}$: its switch $\eta_L(r,r_1,c)$, $r_1\notin E\cup\{r\}$,
avoids the cells of $R\cup Q$ when $c_1\notin C_Q$ and $r_2\notin E$ (at
most $|C_Q|+|E|$ excluded choices of $r_1$), changes only rows
$r,r_1,r_2\notin\{1,2\}$, and the inverse count uses
Theorem~\ref{thm:AM31two} for the partial Latin squares
$Q'=Q\cup\{(r_1,c,s),(r,c,s')\}$ with $r'=r_1$: $r_1\notin E$, so the
only cell of $Q'$ in row $r_1$ is $(r_1,c,s)$, in column $c$, as the
theorem allows, and $\alpha,\beta$ increase by $O(1/n)$ (the sum (19) of
\cite{AM26} is over $r'\notin\mathcal R\cup E$ here, the only rows that
arise as $r'(L')$).  The second follows by
conditioning on the cells of $Q$ one at a time.  $\square$

\begin{corollary}[the $\nu$-tail]\label{cor:nutail}
For every $\lambda,\alpha,\beta$ and every $M$ with $2M+2\le\varepsilon n$,
$\Pr_X[\nu\le M]\le8\Delta^{2M+2}/n$.  In particular
$\Pr_X[\nu\le C\log\log n]=O((\log n)^{2C\log\Delta}/n)=o(1/\log n)$.
\end{corollary}

\emph{Proof.}  As in \S(l): $\{\nu\le M\}$ is the union over $2\le k\le M$
and $a,b\in\{p,p'\}$ of the events $E_k(a,b)$ that the
$\rho_{x_1,y_1}$-path $w_0=a,w_1,\dots$ first meets $\{p,p'\}$ at step
$k$, at $w_k=b$ (\S(l) takes $\nu\ge2$ from \S(k)).  On $E_k(a,b)$ the
square contains the cells $(x_1,p,L(1,p'))$, $(y_1,p,L(2,p'))$ (the
anchors, whose symbols are determined by $R$) and
$(x_1,w_i,t_i),(y_1,w_{i+1},t_i)$ for $0\le i<k$, where $t_i=L(x_1,w_i)$
and $w_1,\dots,w_{k-1}\notin\{p,p'\}$ are distinct; all these cells lie
in the two rows $x_1,y_1\notin\{1,2\}$ (\S(k)).  By the exchangeability
of rows $3,\dots,n$ under $\Pr_X$ (equivalently, in each $S(R)$),
$\Pr_X[E_k(a,b)]=(n-2)(n-3)\Pr_X[E_k(a,b),\ (x_1,y_1)=(3,4)]$, and the
latter event is contained in the union, over the choices of
$w_1,\dots,w_{k-1}$ and of the symbols $t_i$ not forced by the anchors,
of the events $\{L\supseteq Q_{w,t}\}$ with $Q_{w,t}$ the partial Latin
square of the cells listed, placed in rows $3,4$.  For $(a,b)=(p,p)$,
mixed, $(p',p')$ the cells number $2k$, $2k+1$, $2k+2$ and the free
parameters $n^{2k-3}$, $n^{2k-2}$, $n^{2k-1}$ respectively, so by
Corollary~\ref{cor:FCtwo} (applied in each $S(R)$, $R$ of type
$\lambda$; $\Pr_X$ is the average over these $R$ and the marks, all
rectangles of a type having the same number of completions) each case
contributes at most $(n-2)(n-3)\,n^{2k-3+t}(\Delta/n)^{2k+t}\le\Delta^{2k+t}/n\le\Delta^{2k+2}/n$,
$t\in\{0,1,2\}$.  Summing over $k\le M$ and the four $(a,b)$:
$\Pr_X[\nu\le M]\le4\sum_{k\le M}\Delta^{2k+2}/n\le8\Delta^{2M+2}/n$ for
$\Delta\ge2$.  $\square$

\emph{What this does and does not do.}  The first-pair condition
\eqref{eq:hypfirstpair} is $\Pr_X[G^c]=o(1/\log n)$ uniformly, and
$\Pr_X[G^c]\le\Pr_X[\nu\le M]+\Pr_X[\nu>M,\ t=1,\dots,M\text{ all bad}]$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:nutail} disposes of the first term for
$M=C\log\log n$ (any $C$).  So the weak conjecture now follows from the
\emph{hazard hypothesis} alone:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyphazard}
\sup_{\lambda,\alpha,\beta}\ \Pr_X\bigl[\nu>M,\ \text{the first $M$ candidates are all bad}\bigr]=o(1/\log n)
\quad\text{for some }M=M(n)\le C\log\log n,
\end{equation}
which holds if the conditional probability that candidate $t$ is bad,
given $\nu>t$ and that candidates $1,\dots,t-1$ are bad, is at most
$1-c$ for $t\le M$.  This is a one-way reduction (rule~1): the second
term is not a fixed-cell event and is not reached by (FC); it is a
lower bound on the probability of ``$x_1\not\sim y_1$ under $\tau_t$
with an admissible cycle'' at a square-selected column pair, i.e.\ the
obstacle, now with the short-arc term removed.  By Remark rem:fewparts
(paper) only types with fewer than $16\log n$ parts are needed.  What
changed in the obstacle: it is no longer ``in either direction''; the
upper-bound half (short structures at the frame-selected pair) is
done, by the Allsop--Morris machinery, and only lower bounds at
square-selected positions remain.  Cost: constants ($\Delta\le66$ here
against their $23$) and the hypothesis $|R_Q|,|C_Q|\le\varepsilon n$,
irrelevant for $|Q|=O(\log\log n)$.

\emph{Why the \S(n) obstruction disappeared.}  In \S(n) the Claim-4
move used every free row $x$ off $\gamma_L$, and failed when the
$(c_2,c_3)$-cycle through $x$ contained exactly one special row.  The
repair is to use only rows $x$ on cycles containing no special row,
and to prove (Claim~$3''$) that there are $\Omega(n)$ of them on
average, by the same cutting argument as their Claim~3 applied
simultaneously to $\gamma_L$ and to the cycles through the special
rows, with the cuts confined to arcs so that special rows are never
moved.  The price is in the constants only.  The transfer uses no move
that touches rows $1,2$; in particular it holds in $S(R)$, not only in
$X$, so the mark and the isotopy argument of \S(k) are not needed.

\paragraph{(r) The hazard term: the switching identity, and what a
chain of toggles would need (reformulation; negative knowledge; data).}
This paragraph records the first attempt on the hazard hypothesis
\eqref{eq:hyphazard}, the only remaining condition.  Its proved content
is a double-counting identity; the rest is a reason why the natural
switching chain cannot give \eqref{eq:hyphazard} without a lower bound
of the kind that is the core problem, and measurements of the
quantities such a chain would have to bound.  (A first draft of this
paragraph claimed more --- that the chain fails for a reason intrinsic
to the measure; a referee pointed out that the identity below makes
that impossible, and the draft was withdrawn.)

\emph{Setting.}  Fix the frame $R$ (rows $1,2$), a mark $(p,p')$, and
write $(x_1,y_1)$ for the first ladder pair, $\rho=\rho_{x_1,y_1}$,
$\nu$ for the hitting index of \S(k), and $(q_t,c_t)=(\rho^tp,\rho^tp')$
for the column pair of candidate $t<\nu$.  Candidate $t$ is \emph{good}
if $x_1,y_1$ lie on different $(q_t,c_t)$-cycles and one of the two
cycles meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$ rows (admissibility); otherwise
\emph{bad}, which splits into \emph{joined} ($x_1\sim y_1$ on the
$(q_t,c_t)$-cycle $D_t$) and \emph{separated-inadmissible}.  Let
$E_j=\{\nu>j,\ 1,\dots,j\text{ bad}\}$ and
$F_j=\{\nu>j,\ 1,\dots,j-1\text{ bad},\ j\text{ good}\}$.  Write $h_j=\Pr_X[j\text{ good}\mid E_{j-1},\nu>j]=\Pr_X[F_j]/(\Pr_X[E_j]+\Pr_X[F_j])$
for the hazard at $j$.  Since $\Pr_X[E_{j-1}]\ge\Pr_X[E_j]+\Pr_X[F_j]$,
the bound $\Pr_X[E_j]\le C\Pr_X[F_j]$ for $j\le M$ gives
$\Pr_X[E_M]\le(C/(C+1))^M$, which is \eqref{eq:hyphazard} for
$M=C'\log\log n$ (the event in \eqref{eq:hyphazard} is $E_M$ itself);
and $h_j\ge c$ is the same as $\Pr_X[E_j]\le\frac{1-c}{c}\Pr_X[F_j]$.

\emph{The toggle and the identity.}  For $L\in E_j$ with $j$ joined, a
\emph{toggle} is a row switch $\rho(z,z',c_j)$ of two free rows
$z,z'\notin\{1,2,x_1,y_1\}$ on $D_j$, with $z,z'$ interleaving
$x_1,y_1$ in the cyclic order of $D_j$, whose footprint (the row cycle
of $(z,z')$ through $c_j$) avoids $q_j$ --- an Allsop--Morris good pair.
By their Lemma~2.4(1) it separates $x_1$ from $y_1$ on the
$(q_j,c_j)$-cycles; it does not touch rows $1,2,x_1,y_1$, so it stays
in $S(R)$ and leaves $\rho$, $\nu$ and every $(q_t,c_t)$ unchanged.  It
is \emph{ok} if candidate $j$ is good afterwards (separated and
admissible) and \emph{preserving} if moreover candidates $1,\dots,j-1$
are still bad, i.e.\ the result lies in $F_j$.  Conversely, for $L'\in
F_j$ a \emph{merge} is a row switch $\rho(z,z',c_j)$ with $z$ a free
row on the $(q_j,c_j)$-cycle $Z_j$ through $x_1$, $z'$ a free row on
the cycle $Z'_j$ through $y_1$, and footprint avoiding $q_j$; it joins
$Z_j$ and $Z'_j$ with $z,z'$ at the junctions, hence interleaving
$x_1,y_1$, and it is \emph{preserving} if the result lies in $E_j$
(candidates $1,\dots,j-1$ still bad; $j$ is joined automatically).
Preserving toggles and preserving merges are inverse to each other
(a switch of footprint containing $q_j$ conjugates the column
permutation and leaves every candidate's status unchanged), so with $f_j(L)$ the number of
preserving toggles of $L\in E_j$ (zero when $j$ is
separated-inadmissible) and $b_j(L')$ the number of preserving merges
of $L'\in F_j$, double counting gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hazardratio}
\frac{\Pr_X[E_j]}{\Pr_X[F_j]}
=\frac{\mathbb E_X[\,b_j\mid F_j]}{\mathbb E_X[\,f_j\mid E_j]}\,,\qquad
b_j\le|Z_j||Z'_j|\le n^2/4 .
\end{equation}
This is a reformulation: $h_j\ge c$ exactly when
$\mathbb E_X[b_j\mid F_j]\le\frac{1-c}{c}\,\mathbb E_X[f_j\mid E_j]$.  What is new in it is only
that both sides are counts of local moves inside $S(R)$, so that the
obstacle can be stated as a comparison of two cycle statistics in two
conditioned ensembles.

\emph{What a proof would need, and why the obvious bounds do not give
it.}  (a) With the trivial bound $b_j\le n^2/4$ one needs $\mathbb
E_X[f_j\mid E_j]\ge c\,n^2$.  Since $f_j\le$ the number of interleaving
pairs on $D_j$, of order $|D_j|^2$, this requires that the
column cycle through $x_1$ at the square-selected pair $(q_j,c_j)$ has
length $\Theta(n)$ with conditional probability bounded below under
$\Pr_X[\,\cdot\mid E_j]$.  Union bounds of the type used for
Corollary~\ref{cor:nutail} exclude only lengths $O(\log n)$ (a cycle of
length $k$ is a fixed-cell event with $2k$ cells and $n^{2k-1}$
placements), giving $\mathbb E_X[f_j\mid E_j]\gtrsim(\log n)^2$ against
$n^2$: useless.  This is a necessary condition only: $f_j$ also
requires the interleaving pairs to be \emph{good} (footprint avoiding
$q_j$) in positive density, which is a separate lower bound (see
\S(t), where it is shown not to follow from the length bounds).
Empirically $d_j$, the number of toggles, is $\approx0.04\,n^2$
(below).
(b) Even granting (a), the trivial backward bound cannot give
\eqref{eq:hyphazard}, because $f_j$ decays geometrically in $j$.  An
ok toggle separates $x_1,y_1$ at a joined earlier candidate $s<j$
exactly when its footprint contains exactly one of $q_s,c_s$ (then the
$(q_s,c_s)$-permutation $\pi_s$ is replaced by $\pi_s\circ(z\,z')$ or
$(z\,z')\circ\pi_s$) and $z,z'$ interleave $x_1,y_1$ on $D_s$;
goodness of $s$ additionally requires admissibility, which can change
in either direction, and a separated-inadmissible $s$ can become good
through admissibility alone.  The footprint is a row cycle of length
$\approx0.35\,n$ and $D_s$ has length $\Theta(n)$, so the heuristic says the flip
probability per earlier candidate is a constant $\varepsilon>0$.  The
data (measured $\varepsilon\approx0.055,\,0.048,\,0.046$ at
$n=30,50,100$ from $j=2$) decrease slowly with $n$, also at fixed
footprint fraction, and do not decide whether $\varepsilon(n)$
stabilises; the rate at fixed footprint fraction $x$ is
$\propto x(1-x)$, as ``exactly one of $q_s,c_s$'' predicts.  What
follows assumes $\varepsilon(n)$ bounded below; if instead
$\varepsilon\lesssim1/\log n$ then $(1-\varepsilon)^{C\log\log n}\to1$
and only (a) remains.  The preserving fraction among ok toggles is
$\approx(1-\varepsilon)^{j-1}$, and with $b_j\le n^2/4$ the best the
chain can give is $\Pr_X[E_j]\le(1-c(1-\varepsilon)^{j-1})\Pr_X[E_{j-1}]$,
whose product satisfies
\[
\prod_{j\le M}\bigl(1-c(1-\varepsilon)^{j-1}\bigr)\ \ge\
\exp\Bigl(-\frac{c}{\varepsilon(1-c)}\Bigr)
\qquad(\log(1-x)\ge-x/(1-c)\text{ for }x\le c)
\]
for every $M$: a positive constant, never $o(1/\log n)$.  A $K$-step
aggregate (from $E_M$ to the squares with exactly $K$ good candidates
among the first $M$, heuristically forward
$\approx(M-K)c_1n^2(1-\varepsilon)^{M}$ per square and backward
$\le(K+1)c_2n^2$) gains at most
$\max_K\binom MK r^K\le\max_K(eMr/K)^K=e^{Mr}$ with
$r=(c_1/c_2)(1-\varepsilon)^M$, i.e.\ a factor bounded uniformly in
$M$.  The a priori hope that preservation would decay like $1/j$
(which would have given $\Pr_X[E_M]\lesssim M^{-c}$, enough to close
the gap for $c\le1$ and to prove the conjecture for $c>1$) is
contradicted by the data for $j\le8$.
(c) The geometric decay is not the obstacle.  The identity forces
$\mathbb E_X[b_j\mid F_j]$ to decay at the same rate whenever the
hazard is flat, and it does (data): a merge that flips an earlier
candidate to good is not preserving, by the same mechanism.  So the
chain is viable only with a backward bound of the form $\mathbb
E_X[b_j\mid F_j]\le C n^2(1-\varepsilon)^{j-1}$: either fewer merges
on $F_j$ (short cycles there; the data rule this out, $\approx0.03n^2$
merges) or a \emph{lower} bound on the fraction of merges that flip
some earlier candidate --- a lower bound on ``footprint hits exactly
one of $q_s,c_s$ and $z,z'$ interleave on $D_s$'', again a
$\Theta(n)$-scale cycle statement in a conditioned ensemble, of the
same kind as (a).  Conclusion: every version of the chain we can
formulate needs a lower bound at scale $n$ on cycle structure at
square-selected positions, in $\Pr_X[\,\cdot\mid E_j]$ or in
$\Pr_X[\,\cdot\mid F_j]$ (a direct comparison of the two conditional
expectations, say by a coupling of the two conditioned ensembles, is
not excluded by anything here; we have no such coupling).  The two
losses that must be matched are measured below and are matched, with
ratio $\approx1.5$, and the preserving fractions among merges equal
those among toggles to $\pm0.03$ at every $j$ --- the direct evidence
for (c); but nothing proved reaches scale $n$.

\emph{Variants.}  (i) Toggles with short footprints have small
$\varepsilon$ (measured $0.01$ for footprint $\le0.1n$) and
$\varepsilon\to0$ only for footprints $o(n)$; a lower bound on the
number of interleaving good pairs with footprint $o(n)$ is a lower
bound on the number of short row cycles through $c_j$ in the
conditioned ensemble, for which only upper bounds are available (via
(FC)).  (ii) Moves touching rows $x_1,y_1$ (cross-switches) change
$\rho$ and with it every candidate; we see no way to compare $E_j$
and $F_j$ through them.  (iii) The separated-inadmissible bad
candidates ($15$--$17\%$ of the bad ones) need a different toggle (one
that changes which cycle meets a special row); not analysed, and not
needed for the conclusion.  (iv) We expect that two-sided fixed-cell
bounds with constants other than $1$ (cor:FCtwo and a hypothetical
lower counterpart) cannot give constant lower bounds for
``different cycles'' events, which are not fixed-cell events; not
argued here.

\paragraph{(s) Scale-$n$ lower bounds at a square-selected position by
insert/cut switching (one lemma proved; the first-candidate hazard
\emph{not} reduced to it, see \S(t); data).}
This paragraph is the outcome of an attempt to compare the two sides of
\eqref{eq:hazardratio} directly.  No such comparison was found.  What
was found instead is that the first of the two obstacles of \S(r), the
absence of lower bounds at scale $n$ on cycle lengths at
square-selected positions, is not an obstacle: a switching of
Allsop--Morris type, with the inverse count pinned by the marked rows
$x_1,y_1$ and summed dyadically over length scales, gives such lower
bounds with probability $1-O(\delta)$ at scale $\delta n$.  We state
and prove the lemma for the first candidate, where there is no
preservation problem, record what it gives for the hazard at $j=1$,
and say where $j>1$ stands.

\emph{Setting.}  As in \S(r): $R$, $(p,p')$, $(x_1,y_1)$, $\rho$,
$\nu$, $(q_1,c_1)=(\rho p,\rho p')$; special rows $1,2,x_1,y_1$; a row
is \emph{free} if not special.  $E_1^{\rm j}=\{\nu>1,\ x_1\sim y_1
\text{ on the }(q_1,c_1)\text{-cycle }D\}$ (the joined part of $E_1$),
$\ell=|D|$, and for $L\in E_1^{\rm j}$ the two arcs of $D$ between
$x_1$ and $y_1$ have sizes $a,a'$ with $a+a'=\ell$.  A
$(q_1,c_1)$-cycle is \emph{inactive} if it contains no special row;
$g(L)$ is the number of rows on inactive cycles.  All probabilities
are $\Pr_X$ (or $\Pr_{S(R)}$ for fixed $R$ and mark; the argument is
the same).

\emph{Lemma A (insert/cut).}  There are absolute constants $C_0$,
$n_0$ such that for $n\ge n_0$, $8\le k\le n/100$ and
$1/n\le\delta\le1/100$,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[\ell\in[k,2k]\ \big|\ E_1^{\rm j}\bigr]\ \le\ C_0\,\frac kn,
\qquad
\Pr_X\bigl[\min(a,a')\in[k,2k],\ \ell\ge\delta n\ \big|\ E_1^{\rm j}\bigr]\ \le\ C_0\,\frac{k}{\delta^2n} .
\]
Consequently, with $k_0=\lfloor\log n/(4\log\Delta)\rfloor$ ($\Delta$
from Corollary~\ref{cor:FCtwo}) and $\delta\le1/100$,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[\ell\le\delta n\text{ or }\min(a,a')\le\delta^3 n\ \big|\ E_1^{\rm j}\bigr]
\ \le\ 8C_0\delta+\frac{5(\Delta^5+k_0\Delta^2)}{\sqrt n\,\Pr_X[E_1^{\rm j}]}.
\]
(If $\Pr_X[E_1^{\rm j}]$ is small the first-candidate hazard is large
anyway; no lower bound on it is proved or needed.)
($C_0=6000$ will do; $n_0\ge\Delta^{32}$ is forced by the matching of
(i) and (iii) at $k_0\ge8$, so the lemma is purely asymptotic.)


\emph{Proof.}  \emph{(i) The cycle length.}  Fix $k$ and let
$\mathcal C_k=E_1^{\rm j}\cap\{\ell\in[k,2k]\}$.  \emph{Moves.}  For
$L\in\mathcal C_k$, a free row $z\in D$ and a row $z'$ on an inactive
cycle $C$: if the row cycle $\rho_L(z,z',c_1)$ avoids $q_1$, switch
it; otherwise first switch the column cycle $C$ (columns $q_1,c_1$,
rows of $C$) to obtain $L''$, in which $\rho_{L''}(z,z',c_1)$ avoids
$q_1$ (\cite[Lemma~2.3(1)]{AM26} after re-basing the row cycle at a
third of its columns, which exist since $z,z'$ lie on different
$(q_1,c_1)$-cycles; directly as in the proof of Claim~$4'$ of \S(q),
since $C\not\ni z$), and then switch it.  Call the
result $L'$.  The column switch changes only rows of $C$, which are
not special; the row switch changes only rows $z,z'$.  Hence rows
$1,2,x_1,y_1$ are unchanged, so $L'\in S(R)$ with the same mark, first
pair, $\rho$, $\nu$ and candidates.  By \cite[Lemma~2.4(2)]{AM26}
(row switch of two rows on different column cycles along a row cycle
avoiding $q_1$) $D$ and $C$ merge into one $(q_1,c_1)$-cycle
$D'=D\cup C$, and every other cycle is unchanged; directly: column
$q_1$ is unchanged and the $c_1$-entries of $z,z'$ are exchanged, so
the column permutation is composed with the transposition of the two
symbols $L[z,c_1],L[z',c_1]$, which splices $C$ into $D$ immediately
after $z$ in the list; so $L'\in E_1^{\rm j}$ with
$\ell'=\ell+|C|\ge\ell+2$, and the cyclic order of the special rows on
$D$ is preserved.  The number of moves from $L$ is at least
$(\ell-4)\,g(L)$.  \emph{Inverse count.}  Let $L'\in E_1^{\rm j}$.  If
$L'$ arises from $(L,z,z')$ then $L$ is recovered from $(L',z,z')$ by
switching $\rho_{L'}(z,z',c_1)$ (\cite[Fact~2.1]{AM26}: the same set
of cells) and then, in the two-step case, switching the
$(q_1,c_1)$-cycle through $z'$; so $L'$ has at most $2N$ preimages,
$N$ the number of unordered pairs $\{z,z'\}$ of free rows on $D'$ such
that switching $\rho_{L'}(z,z',c_1)$ splits $D'$ into a piece $P\ni
x_1,y_1$ with $|P|\in[k,2k]$ and a piece $S$.  By
\cite[Lemma~2.4(1)]{AM26} $S$ consists of the rows strictly between
$z$ and $z'$ in the list together with $z'$, and $P=D'\setminus S$ is
a contiguous arc of $D'$ ending at $z$; so $P$ determines $\{z,z'\}$,
and $P$ is a contiguous arc of length $m\in[k,2k]$ containing $x_1$,
of which there are at most $m$ for each $m$.  Hence $N\le\sum_{m=k}^{2k}m\le3k^2$
and $L'$ has at most $6k^2$ preimages.  \emph{Lower bound on $g$.}
Claim~$3''$ of \S(q), with special rows $1,2,x_1,y_1$ and with its
switching (row switches of good same-arc pairs on the cycles through
rows $1,2$ other than $D$, and the cross-switches of Claim~$2'$ on
those cycles) applied inside the class $\mathcal C_k$, which it
preserves (it touches neither $D$ nor a special row, so $\ell$,
$E_1^{\rm j}$, the mark and the candidates are unchanged), gives
$\mathbb E_X[g\mid\mathcal C_k]\ge(n-2k)/31$ for $n\ge n_0$, $k\le
n/100$: the free rows off $D$ number $n-\ell-O(1)\ge n-2k-4$, they
lie on the switched active cycles or on inactive cycles, the forward
count is at least $\frac1{27}((n-2k-g-13)_+)^2$ in aggregate and the
backward count at most $n\,g(L')$, and Jensen's inequality closes as
in \S(q).  \emph{Conclusion.}  Double counting the moves,
$|\mathcal C_k|\,(k-4)\,\mathbb E_X[g\mid\mathcal C_k]\le6k^2\,|E_1^{\rm j}|$,
so $\Pr_X[\ell\in[k,2k]\mid E_1^{\rm j}]\le 6k^2\cdot31/((k-4)(n-2k))\le 400k/n$
for $k\ge8$, $k\le n/100$.

\emph{(ii) The arcs.}  Let $\mathcal C'_k=E_1^{\rm j}\cap\{a\in[k,2k],\ \ell\ge\delta n\}$
($a$ the arc from $x_1$ to $y_1$ in the list order; the other arc by
symmetry).  If $k>\delta n/4$ or $\delta n<240$ the claimed bound is
$\ge1$ and there is nothing to prove, so assume $k\le\delta n/4$ and
$\delta n\ge240$.  \emph{Transfer moves.}  The other arc has
$\ell-a\ge\delta n-2k\ge\delta n/2$ rows and at most two frame rows,
so it contains a special-row-free stretch of at least $\delta n/6-1$
free rows; let $B=B(L)$ be the longest such stretch (ties broken by
position), with $b=|B|$.  For a good same-arc pair $(w,w')$ in $B$
(footprint $\rho_L(w,w',c_1)$ avoiding $q_1$) and a free row $z$ of
the $x_1$--$y_1$ arc (at least $k-3$ choices): switch
$\rho_L(w,w',c_1)$, which by \cite[Lemma~2.4(1)]{AM26} cuts the rows
strictly between $w$ and $w'$ together with $w'$ off as a new cycle
$S$ containing no special row, hence inactive, and leaves $x_1,y_1$
and the $x_1$--$y_1$ arc untouched; then perform the insert move of
(i) at the pair $(z,z')$ with $z':=w'$ (one- or two-step as there).
The result $L'$ is in $E_1^{\rm j}$ with $\ell'=\ell$, $a'=a+|S|\ge a+2$,
the same cyclic order of special rows, and $S$ spliced in after $z$,
ending at $w'$ (in the two-step case the column switch reverses $S$
as a cycle, and the splice still ends at $z'=w'$).  Claim~$2'$ of
\S(q), applied to the class of triples $(L,D,B(L))$, $L\in\mathcal C'_k$,
$|B(L)|=b$ --- closed under its cross-switches, which act inside $B$,
touch no special row, keep $B$ as a set (\cite[Remark~2.7]{AM26}) and
change neither $\ell$ nor $a$ --- gives at least $(b-3)^2/9\ge\delta^2n^2/400$
good pairs in $B$ in aggregate (using $\delta n\ge240$), so at least
$(k-3)\delta^2n^2/400$ moves per square of $\mathcal C'_k$ on average.
\emph{Inverse count.}  From $L'$: the segment $S$ to be cut out of
the $x_1$--$y_1$ arc leaves a prefix and a suffix of that arc of total
length in $[k,2k]$, at most $(2k)^2$ choices, and determines the pair
$(z,z')$ ($z$ the row before $S$, $z'=w'$ its last row); undoing the
insert takes the row switch at $(z,z')$ and, in the two-step case, the
column switch of the cycle through $z'$ (two alternatives); undoing
the cut is the row switch at $(w,w')$ with $w'$ known and $w$ one of
at most $n$ rows (no two-step alternative).  So at most $8k^2n$
preimages.  \emph{Conclusion.}
$\Pr_X[a\in[k,2k],\ \ell\ge\delta n\mid E_1^{\rm j}]\le8k^2n\cdot400/((k-3)\delta^2n^2)\le6000\,k/(\delta^2n)$
for $k\ge8$.

\emph{(iii) Short lengths.}  $\{\ell=m\}\cap E_1^{\rm j}$ is the
union over the positions of $x_1,y_1$ ($n^2$), of $q_1,c_1$ ($n^2$),
of the $m-2$ other rows of the cycle ($n^{m-2}$), of the position of
$y_1$ on it ($m-1$) and of the $m-1$ free symbols ($n^{m-1}$;
$L[y_1,q_1]=L[1,p']$ is forced) of fixed-cell events with $2m+2$ cells
(the $2m$ cells of the cycle and the anchors $(x_1,p),(y_1,p)$), so by
Corollary~\ref{cor:FCtwo} and the exchangeability of rows
$3,\dots,n$, as in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:nutail}, it has
probability at most $(m-1)n^{2m+1}(\Delta/n)^{2m+2}=(m-1)\Delta^{2m+2}/n$.  For $\{a=m\}\cap E_1^{\rm j}$
the path from $x_1$ to $y_1$ along the cycle has $m+1$ rows and
$2m+2$ cells, and $c_1$ must be pinned by the further cells
$(x_1,p')$ and $(y_1,c_1)$ with the forced symbol: $2m+5$ cells,
$n^{2m+4}$ placements, probability at most $\Delta^{2m+5}/n$.  Summing
over $m\le k_0$ (at most $2k_0\Delta^{2k_0+2}/n\le2k_0\Delta^2/\sqrt n$ and
$2\cdot2\Delta^{2k_0+5}/n\le4\Delta^5/\sqrt n$), and (i) over the
dyadic $k\in[k_0,\delta n]$ ($\le800\delta$) and (ii) over the dyadic
$k\in[k_0,\delta^3n]$ for each arc ($\le2\cdot12000\delta$), gives the
consequence.  $\square$

\emph{Remarks on the proof.}  (1) The mechanism is that of Claim~$4'$:
$\Omega(k\,n)$ moves out of the class, $O(k^2)$ into any square, the

\paragraph{(t) The first-candidate step does not close: cross-switches
toggle interleaving, an invariant configuration, and what
exchangeability gives at fixed positions (negative knowledge; one

Attempting to prove the first-candidate hazard from Lemma~A, as
\S(s) proposed, fails at its last step, and the failure is
instructive.  We record it, the reason no variant of the present
toolkit repairs it, and the fixed-position statement that the same
toolkit \emph{does} give, which locates the loss exactly.

\emph{(1) Cross-switches toggle interleaving.}  Setting of \S(s):
$L\in E_1^{\rm j}$, $D$ the $(q_1,c_1)$-cycle through $x_1,y_1$, free
rows of $D$ listed cyclically.  A pair $\{z,z'\}$ of free rows of $D$
is \emph{interleaving} if exactly one of $x_1,y_1$ lies strictly
between them (on either side), and \emph{good} if its row cycle through
$c_1$ avoids $q_1$.  Let $(u,v)$ be a bad interleaving pair and $\zeta$
the cross-switch $\zeta_L(q_1,c_1,x_u,x_v)$ in the direction whose open
segment $\Sigma$ contains, say, $x_1$ and no frame row (legal: it
changes the $(q_1,c_1)$-entries of the rows of $\Sigma$, hence of
$x_1$, which replaces $\rho=\mathrm{pos}_{y_1}\circ L[x_1,\cdot]$ by
$\rho\circ(q_1\,c_1)$; since $p,p'\notin\{q_1,c_1\}$ the two
$\rho$-paths from $p$ and $p'$ keep their first steps and exchange
their tails after step $1$, so $\nu$, the first pair, and every
candidate $\{\rho^tp,\rho^tp'\}$ as an unordered pair are unchanged;
if $\Sigma$ contains $y_1$ instead, $\rho$ becomes $(q_1\,c_1)\circ\rho$
and the two paths are exchanged from step $1$ on, with the same
conclusion).  By \cite[Lemma~2.6, Remark~2.7]{AM26} the row set of $D$
is unchanged, the list is that of $L$ with $\Sigma$ reversed, and the
goodness of every pair $\{i,j\}$ disjoint from $\{u,v\}$ with exactly
one element in $\Sigma$ is toggled.  But for such a pair the
interleaving status is toggled as well: $i\in\Sigma$ and $x_1\in\Sigma$
exchange their relative order under the reversal, while $j$ and $y_1$
outside $\Sigma$ keep theirs, and the four points
$i,x_1,j,y_1$ alternate around the cycle before the switch iff they do
not alternate after it.  Pairs disjoint from $\{u,v\}$ with both elements in $\Sigma$, or
both outside, keep both their goodness (\cite[Lemma~2.6(1)]{AM26}) and
their interleaving status.  So the cross-switch maps the pairs
straddling $\Sigma$ bijectively onto themselves, exchanging
\emph{(good, interleaving)} with \emph{(bad, non-interleaving)} and
\emph{(bad, interleaving)} with \emph{(good, non-interleaving)}.  In
the Claim-$2'$ scheme run on the interleaving family, with the
auxiliary pairs $(u,v)\in W(i_0,j_0)$ taken interleaving, the Kind-2
image of a bad interleaving pair is therefore a good
\emph{non-interleaving} pair, outside the family, and the counting
does not close; with the auxiliary pairs taken same-stretch (segment
free of all special rows, which preserves interleaving), the Kind-2
images stay in the family but the Kind-1 targets, the good auxiliary
pairs themselves, are good same-stretch pairs, outside it; and no
mixed sub-family of $W(i_0,j_0)$ has a usable lower bound on its size.
(A cross-switch at a bad same-arc pair in the long direction, with
$\Sigma\supseteq\{x_1,y_1\}$, legal when that arc has no frame row,
toggles goodness and preserves interleaving; but its auxiliary pair is
non-interleaving, so the Kind-1 targets leak out of the family just
the same.)  This is the error in the earlier version of \S(s).

\emph{(2) An invariant configuration (heuristic).}  Write $\pi$ for
the row-successor map along a $(q_1,c_1)$-cycle.  Pairs $\{z,\pi(z)\}$
are always bad: the shared symbol $L[z,c_1]=L[\pi(z),q_1]$ puts $q_1$
and $c_1$ on one cycle of the row permutation of the pair.  Call a
square $L\in E_1^{\rm j}$ with rows $1,2\in D$ \emph{polarised} if:
a pair of free rows of $D$ at cyclic distance $\ge2$ is good iff it is
non-interleaving; pairs at distance $\ge2$ inside an inactive cycle
are good; every inactive cycle $C$ has an \emph{affinity} to one arc
of $D$ (the arc $\text{arc}_k$ such that the pairs $C\times\text{arc}_k$
are all good and $C\times\text{arc}_{3-k}$ all bad); and pairs between
two inactive cycles are good iff the cycles have the same affinity.
On a polarised square $f_1=0$.  Each move of the toolkit preserves
polarisation up to the $O(n)$ pairs that contain a switched row (the
rows $1,2$ stay on $D$ under every move): (a) a cross-switch at a bad
interleaving pair, by (1), exchanges (bad, interleaving) with (good,
non-interleaving) among the pairs straddling $\Sigma$ and fixes the
status of pairs inside $D$ not straddling it; the rows of $\Sigma$ have
their $(q_1,c_1)$-entries swapped, which toggles their goodness with
every row off $D$, and since the reversal also moves
$\Sigma\cap\text{arc}_1$ into $\text{arc}_2$ and vice versa this
matches the affinities; (b) a cross-switch at a bad same-stretch pair
would toggle goodness without changing interleaving, but on a
polarised square no same-stretch pair at distance $\ge2$ is bad, and
adjacent pairs never straddle a legal segment; (c) the column switch
of an inactive cycle $C$ toggles the goodness of every pair
$C\times(\text{rows off }C)$ (\cite[Lemma~2.3(1)--(2)]{AM26}, or the
transposition argument of Claim~$4'$: only one row of the pair has its
$(q_1,c_1)$-entries swapped) and preserves it for pairs inside $C$
(both rows swapped), so it reverses the affinity of $C$ and preserves
polarisation; (d) an insert of Lemma~A at a good pair $(z,z')$ puts
$C$ into $z$'s arc (\cite[Lemma~2.4(2)]{AM26}), which by the
affinity is the arc $C$ is good with, after which the pairs
$C\times(\text{other arc})$ are interleaving and bad, the pairs
$C\times(\text{same arc})$ non-interleaving and good, the pairs
inside $C$ good, and the pairs $C'\times C$ for other inactive $C'$
become $C'\times\text{arc}_k$ pairs with the status the inter-cycle
rule prescribed, as required (a two-step insert first reverses the
affinity of $C$, consistently with the two-step rule); (e) a cut
produces an inactive $S$ from a same-stretch good pair, and $S$ has
the affinity of the arc it came from; (f) a transfer is a cut
followed by an insert.  Polarisation is the opposite of the truth
(below), but every identity or inequality obtained by double counting
these moves over a class closed under them is satisfied by a
hypothetical measure concentrated on polarised squares, and an
argument concluding $f_1>0$ on a positive fraction of $E_1^{\rm j}$
would have to exploit the $O(n)$ exceptional pairs per move against
the $\Theta(n^2)$ pairs in the counts.  We record as negative
knowledge, heuristic and not a theorem: \emph{we see no counting
argument built from cross-switches, inserts, cuts, transfers and
inactive column switches that proves a positive density of good
interleaving pairs on $E_1^{\rm j}$}; the first-candidate hazard is
not reduced to Lemma~A, and the missing input is a lower bound on good
pairs \emph{across the $x_1$--$y_1$ cut}.  (The ``cut goodness'' is
the first-candidate form of ``lower bounds at square-selected
positions'': Lemma~A supplies the lengths, not the crossing good
pairs.)

\emph{(3) What exchangeability gives at fixed positions.}  The
statement we want at the square-selected pair is available at a
\emph{fixed} pair, by a one-line use of row exchangeability on top of
Claim~$3''$:

\emph{Proposition T.}  Let $R$ be a $2\times n$ Latin rectangle (rows
$1,2$), $n\ge504$, let $q\ne c$ be columns and $x\ne y$ rows in
$\{3,\dots,n\}$.  Then
$\Pr_{S(R)}[x\text{ and }y\text{ lie on the same }(q,c)\text{-column cycle}]\le1-\tfrac1{30}+\tfrac3n$.
For a uniform Latin square of order $n\ge502$ (no $R$) and arbitrary
rows $x\ne y$ the bound is $1-\tfrac1{30}$.

\emph{Proof.}  Let $D_x$ be the $(q,c)$-cycle through $x$ and
$\ell_x=|D_x|$.  For a permutation $\tau$ of $\{3,\dots,n\}$ fixing
$x$, $L\mapsto\tau L$ (permuting rows) is a bijection of $S(R)$ with
$y\in D_x(L)$ iff $\tau y\in D_x(\tau L)$; averaging over $\tau$,
$\Pr[y\in D_x]=\mathbb E\,|D_x\cap\{3,\dots,n\}\setminus\{x\}|/(n-3)\le\mathbb E[\ell_x-1]/(n-3)$.
The proof of Claim~$3''$ of \S(q) applies verbatim with special set
$\{1,2,x\}$, $Q=\emptyset$ and the class $S(R)$ (active cycles: $D_x$
and the cycles through rows $1,2$, at most three arcs; the switching
is the row switches of good same-arc pairs on them and the
cross-switches of Claim~$2'$, none of which moves a special row), and
gives $\mathbb E[g]\ge f/30$ for the number $g$ of rows on cycles
containing no special row, $f=n-3\ge501$.  Since $\ell_x\le n-g$,
$\mathbb E[\ell_x-1]\le n-1-(n-3)/30$ and the bound follows.  For a
uniform Latin square take the special set $\{x\}$, $f=n-1\ge501$, and
average over all $\tau$ fixing $x$: $\Pr[y\in D_x]=\mathbb E[\ell_x-1]/(n-1)\le1-1/30$.  $\square$

\emph{Corollary.}  For a uniform Latin square and a fixed column
pair, the cycle $D_x$ through a fixed row satisfies
$\mathbb E[n-\ell_x]\ge(n-1)/30$; hence, with $M$ the length of the
longest cycle of the pair, $\mathbb E[n-M]\ge(n-1)/60$ (as
$\sum_C|C|(n-|C|)\le2n(n-M)$) and
$\Pr[n-M\ge n/120]\ge1/120-O(1/n)$: with probability bounded below,
the rows off the longest cycle of the pair number at least $n/120$.
(This is in the ``coarse'' direction that \S4 of the gap note records
as not reached by the surviving material of \cite{CGW08}; it follows
from the switching of Claims~2--3 of \cite{AM26} re-run with a single
special row, plus row exchangeability.  It is not an improvement for the type $(n)$
itself, for which the gap note's $\Pr_F((n))\le5/6+o(1)$ via
intercalates is better than the $1-1/60$ implied here; it does not
approach $\Pr[(n)]=o(1)$.)

\emph{Where the loss is.}  Proposition~T is exactly the
first-candidate statement with $(q,c)$ and $y$ \emph{fixed}: it needs
no good pair across any cut, because $y$ is exchangeable with the
other free rows and the question ``is $y$ on $D_x$'' becomes ``how
long is $D_x$'', which Claim~$3''$ answers.  In the ladder, $y_1$ is
selected by the cell $(y_1,p)$ and the candidate columns
$q_1=\mathrm{pos}_{y_1}(L[1,p'])$, $c_1=\mathrm{pos}_{y_1}(L[x_1,p'])$
are functions of row $y_1$; conditioning on the columns destroys the
exchangeability of $y_1$, and conditioning on less leaves the columns
random.  Every attempt to restore a generic second row (a third free
row $w$ with its own pair $(q_w,c_w)$; a row switch of $y_1$ with a
free row along a footprint avoiding $p,q_1,c_1$, which preserves the
event; a switch along a footprint through $q_1$, which moves the
candidate) either leaves the event untouched or moves the candidate
pair with it.  So the first-candidate hazard is Proposition~T with the
exchangeability of $y$ removed, and the switching toolkit, which
proves Proposition~T, does not prove it; the missing cut-crossing
goodness is the precise residue of square selection at $j=1$.
