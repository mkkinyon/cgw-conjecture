\section{The ladder identity: the bias is carried by the squares with
no flippable ladder pair}\label{sec:ladder}

Session 13, after the component enumeration of \S\ref{sec:s13comp7}.
This section proves an exact identity that replaces the whole
chain \S\ref{sec:spectralexact}--\S\ref{sec:s13comp7} (pool, master
formula, annealed bias, transfer, trade chain) by a single
combinatorial statement.  Notation as in \S\ref{sec:s13trapped}:
$(L,P)\in X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$, $P=\{j,j'\}$, frame
$\pi=\pi_P$ ($L(\pi(r),j')=L(r,j)$), $C_r$ the $\pi$-cycle of row $r$,
$\rho_{x,y}$ the column permutation of rows $x,y$
($L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$), $(x,y)$ \emph{flippable} iff $j,j'$ lie
in different cycles of $\rho_{x,y}$, and the \emph{$j'$-trade} at
$(x,y)$ exchanges rows $x,y$ on the $\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through $j'$,
which replaces $\pi$ by $(x\,y)\circ\pi$ if $(x,y)$ is flippable and by
$(x\,y)\circ\pi\circ(x\,y)$ otherwise.  $B=\{1\sim2\}$ (rows $1,2$ in a
common frame cycle), $A$ its complement, $\mathbf 1_B$ the bit; for $B$-instances $d_{12}$ and
$d_{21}$ are the numbers of $\pi$-steps from $1$ to $2$ and from $2$ to
$1$.

\begin{definition}[ladder]\label{def:ladder}
Put $K_0=\min(|C_1|,|C_2|)$ on $A$ and $K_0=\min(d_{12},d_{21})$ on
$B$, and for $1\le k<K_0$ let
\[
x_k=\pi^{-k}(1),\qquad y_k=\pi^{-k}(2),\qquad
T_k=\text{the $j'$-trade at }(x_k,y_k).
\]
The pairs $\{x_k,y_k\}$ are the \emph{ladder} of $(L,P)$, and
$\mathrm{Flip}(L,P)=\{k<K_0:(x_k,y_k)\text{ flippable}\}$.
\end{definition}

The rows $x_1,\dots,x_{K_0-1}$ are distinct rows of $C_1\setminus\{1\}$
(on $A$) or of the arc from $2$ to $1$ (on $B$), the $y_k$ likewise in
$C_2\setminus\{2\}$ or the arc from $1$ to $2$; so the $2(K_0-1)$
ladder rows are distinct and avoid rows $1,2$, and $K_0\ge2$: on $A$
because $\pi$ is a derangement, on $B$ by the admissibility of the mark
($\pi(1)\ne2$, $\pi(2)\ne1$).

\begin{theorem}[ladder identity]\label{thm:ladder}
For every $n$, $\lambda$ and split $\alpha+\beta=m$ with
$\alpha,\beta\ge2$,
\[
\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A]\;=\;\Pr_X\bigl[B,\ \mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\bigr]
-\Pr_X\bigl[A,\ \mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\bigr],
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\bigl|\Pr_X[B]-\tfrac12\bigr|\;\le\;\tfrac12\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset].
\]
The same identity holds with $X$ replaced by $S(R)\times\{P\}$ for any
fixed admissible pair of first rows $R$ and mark $P$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $(L,P)\in X$ and write $K=\{1,\dots,K_0-1\}$.  The ladder depends
on $(L,P)$, so the $T_k$ are not maps of $X$; the statement to prove is
that $(L,P)\sim T_S(L,P)$, $S\subseteq K$ (the trades at the
unordered pairs of $S$, in any order), is an equivalence relation on
$X$ whose classes have exactly $2^{|K|}$ elements and on which the bit
is $\mathbf 1_B(T_S(L,P))=\mathbf 1_B(L,P)+|S\cap\mathrm{Flip}|\bmod2$.
This follows once we know that each $T_k$ is an involution of $X$
which preserves $K_0$, the set of unordered ladder pairs and the
flippability of each of them, that $T_k$ toggles the bit exactly when
$k\in\mathrm{Flip}$, and that trades at disjoint row pairs commute:
then the same trades, at the same unordered pairs with the same
flippabilities, are available from every point of a class (symmetry
and transitivity), the $2^{|K|}$ points $T_S(L,P)$ are distinct
(rows $x_k,y_k$ are changed by $T_k$ and by no other $T_i$), and the
bit formula holds.  If $\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset$ exactly half of the
subsets $S$ have $|S\cap\mathrm{Flip}|$ odd, so $B$ and $A$ each
contain half of the class; summing over classes gives the identity,
and the bound follows from $\Pr[B]+\Pr[A]=1$.

\emph{$T_k$ is a well-defined involution of $X$.}  The $j'$-trade at
$(x_k,y_k)$ changes only rows $x_k,y_k\notin\{1,2\}$, so $\sigma$ and
the mark are untouched and $(L,P)$ stays in $X$; it is an involution
because the traded columns are again the $\rho'_{x_k,y_k}$-cycle
through $j'$ (\S\ref{sec:s13trapped}).

\emph{Effect on the frame.}  If $k\in\mathrm{Flip}$ the new frame is
$\pi'=(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi$.  On $A$ this merges $C_1$ and $C_2$: the
new cycle runs $1\to\pi(1)\to\dots\to x_{k+1}\to y_k\to\pi(y_k)\to\dots
\to2\to\dots\to y_{k+1}\to x_k\to\dots\to1$, so $d'_{12}=|C_1|$ and
$d'_{21}=|C_2|$ (reading $x_{|C_1|}=1$, $y_{|C_2|}=2$).  On $B$,
$x_k$ lies on the arc from $2$ to $1$ and $y_k$ on the arc from $1$ to
$2$ (as $k<\min(d_{12},d_{21})$), and the split into
$(x_k,\pi(x_k),\dots,\pi^{-1}(y_k))$ and $(y_k,\dots,\pi^{-1}(x_k))$
puts $1$ ($k$ steps after $x_k$, fewer than the $d_{12}$ steps from
$x_k$ to $y_k$) in the first cycle and $2$ in the second: the new
state is in $A$ with $|C'_1|=d_{12}$, $|C'_2|=d_{21}$.  In both cases
$K_0$ is preserved and the bit toggles.  If $k\notin\mathrm{Flip}$ the
new frame is $(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi\circ(x_k\,y_k)$: the cycle partition
is unchanged up to exchanging the labels $x_k,y_k$, so $K_0$ and the
bit are preserved.

\emph{The ladder is preserved.}  Let $\pi'=(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi$ (the
conjugation case is similar and easier).  Since
$\pi'^{-1}(r)=\pi^{-1}((x_k\,y_k)(r))$, the preimage chains of $1$ and
$2$ under $\pi'$ agree with those under $\pi$ up to depth $k$ and are
exchanged beyond it: $\pi'^{-i}(1)=x_i$ and $\pi'^{-i}(2)=y_i$ for
$i\le k$, while $\pi'^{-(k+1)}(1)=\pi^{-1}(y_k)=y_{k+1}$ and then
$\pi'^{-i}(1)=y_i$, $\pi'^{-i}(2)=x_i$ for $k<i<K_0$.  So the unordered
pairs $\{x_i,y_i\}$, $i\in K$, are the ladder of $T_k(L,P)$.  In the
conjugation case the same computation with $(x_k\,y_k)\circ\pi\circ(x_k\,y_k)$
gives $\pi'^{-i}(1)=(x_k\,y_k)(x_i)$, which again permutes the pairs
$\{x_i,y_i\}$ among themselves (only $i=k$ is affected, by exchanging
$x_k,y_k$).

\emph{Flippability is preserved and the $T_k$ commute.}  $T_k$ changes
rows $x_k,y_k$ only, and the flippability of $(x_i,y_i)$, $i\ne k$,
depends only on rows $x_i,y_i$, which are different rows.  Likewise
$T_i$ and $T_k$ act on disjoint pairs of rows, each along a column set
determined by its own two rows, so $T_iT_k=T_kT_i$.  (After $T_k$ the
pair $\{x_i,y_i\}$ may have changed its index, but the trade at an
unordered pair does not depend on the index or on the order of the
two rows.)

The statement for fixed $R$ holds because every $T_k$ fixes rows $1$
and $2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[the conjecture reduces to the ladder]\label{cor:ladder}
Write $f(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)=\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]$.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked}, $\bar q(\mu\to\lambda)-1=(\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A])/\Pr_X[A]$
and $\Pr_X[A]\ge(1-f)/2$, so $|\bar q-1|\le2f/(1-f)$: $\mathrm{EQ}(\delta)$
holds with $\delta(n)=\max_{\lambda,\alpha\ne\beta}2f/(1-f)$.  If this
$\delta(n)\to0$, Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} gives
\[
\dtv(\PP_n,\QQ_n)=O\Bigl(\log n\cdot\max_{\lambda,\alpha\ne\beta}
f(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)+n^{-2/3}\Bigr).
\]
In particular the weak CGW conjecture follows from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L}
\textup{(L)}\qquad \Pr_X\bigl[\text{no ladder pair is flippable}\bigr]=o(1/\log n)
\quad\text{uniformly in }\lambda,\alpha,\beta .
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[what (L) asks, and what the data say]\label{rem:ladderdata}
(i) The ladder consists of $K_0-1$ pairs of \emph{disjoint} rows,
$K_0=\min(|C_1|,|C_2|)$ resp.\ $\min(d_{12},d_{21})$, and a pair is
flippable iff columns $j,j'$ lie in different cycles of its row
permutation --- the complement of the conjugate
(rows$\leftrightarrow$columns) of the bit itself.  (L) does not ask these coins to be fair, only that they
do not all fail.

(ii) Exact enumeration at $n=7$ (\texttt{s13\_comp7.c}, four
instances) confirms the identity to all digits
($\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]=-0.005848$ and $\Pr[B,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]
-\Pr[A,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=-0.005848$ for $\lambda=(7)$,
$\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{2,5\}$; $+0.003509$ for $\{3,4\}$; $-0.004651$
for $(5,2)$; $0$ for a cross-cycle pair), where however
$\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=0.40$--$0.42$: the bound only bites for
large $n$.  On uniform squares with true marks
(\texttt{s13\_ladder.py}, \texttt{runs/s13/f1/ladder\{30,50,100\}.log};
$9000$, $7200$, $3000$ instances):
\[
\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid K_0]=2^{-(K_0-1)}
\]
within statistical error for $K_0\le6$ and consistent with it beyond
($n=50$: $0.480,0.265,0.142,0.070,0.036,0.030,0.002$ for $K_0=2..8$
against $0.5,0.25,0.125,0.0625,0.031,0.016,0.008$), the ladder coins
are flippable with frequency $0.4990$, $0.5002$, $0.4993$, and
$\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=0.125,\ 0.079,\ 0.041$ against
$\E[2^{-(K_0-1)}]=0.124,\ 0.077,\ 0.038$.  Since $\Pr[K_0=k]\approx4/n$
for small $k$ and $\sum_{k\ge2}2^{-(k-1)}=1$, this is $\approx4/n$
($0.133,0.080,0.040$): if the coins are fair and independent, (L)
holds with $O(1/n)$ and the conjecture would follow with
$\dtv=O(\log n/n)$.  The heuristic rests on uniformity: for
isotopes of $\mathbb Z_n$ every $\rho_{x,y}$ is an $n$-cycle and
$\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset$ always.

(iii) \emph{Cost of a short ladder.}
$\Pr[A,K_0\le\ell]\le\Pr[|C_1|\le\ell]+\Pr[|C_2|\le\ell]\le72\ell/n$
for $\ell\le n/2-1$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped}.  On $B$ the
ladder identity itself gives
$\Pr[B,K_0\le\ell,\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset]
=\Pr[A,K_0\le\ell,\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset]\le72\ell/n$, and the
switching of Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} gives
$\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\le n/2]\le4/n$ (and the same for $d_{21}$):
from such a square pick $y$ strictly inside the arc from $1$ to $2$
($\ell-1$ choices) and $x$ in a free cycle $C_x$ ($n-|C_1|\ge n/2$
choices), turn $C_x$ if $(x,y)$ is not flippable, and $j'$-trade; the
arc becomes $1\to\dots\to\pi^{-1}(y)\to x\to(C_x)\to y\to\dots\to2$, so
$d_{12}$ grows by $|C_x|$ and nothing else changes; given the target,
the position of $x$ on the new arc ($\ell-1$ choices) and the binary
``turn back'' pin the move, so there are at most $2(\ell-1)$
preimages.  What is missing is
$\Pr[B,\ K_0\le\ell_0,\ |C_1|>n/2,\ \mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=O(\ell_0/n)$:
a short arc inside a cycle covering most rows, with no flippable
ladder pair; there the free rows may be absent and the flippability
of a pair inside $C_1$ cannot be toggled by a turn.  \emph{To do.}

(iv) \emph{Local witnesses.}  A ladder pair is flippable as soon as
the $\rho_{x_k,y_k}$-cycle through $j$ is short and avoids $j'$ (or
the one through $j'$ is short and avoids $j$).  On real squares the
length of the $j$-cycle of a ladder pair is spread like a uniform
variable on $\{2,\dots,n\}$ ($\Pr[\le\ell]=0.021,0.081,0.18,0.38$ for
$\ell=2,5,10,20$ at $n=50$ against $(\ell-1)/(n-1)=0.020,0.082,0.18,0.39$;
$0.010,0.041,0.092,0.19$ at $n=100$), and among instances with
$K_0\ge n/4$ a flippable ladder pair with $j$-cycle of length $\le10$
exists in $93\%$ ($n=50$) and $95\%$ ($n=100$), $\le20$ in $99.6\%$
and $99.8\%$.  A non-flippable pair has $j'$ on its $j$-cycle, hence
no $j$-cycle avoiding $j'$ of any length, so
$\{\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset,K_0\ge\ell_0\}$ is contained in the event
below; granting the missing part of (iii) with $\ell_0=n/\log^3n$
(total cost $O(1/\log^3n)$), (L) follows from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lloc}
\Pr\Bigl[K_0\ge\tfrac n{\log^3n}\ \text{and no ladder pair has a
$j$-cycle of length}\le\log^5n\text{ avoiding }j'\Bigr]=o(1/\log n),
\end{equation}
a statement about at least $n/\log^3n$ disjoint row pairs each of
which carries such a cycle with probability $\approx\log^5n/n$
(expected number $\gtrsim\log^2n$ witnesses).  Short cycles through a given
column in the row permutation of a given row pair are substructures
of polylogarithmic size, so \eqref{eq:Lloc} is the kind of statement
that the switching method of Kwan--Sudakov \cite{KS18} and its
successors is built for: a second-moment bound needs
$\operatorname{Cov}(W_k,W_{k'})=o(\Pr[W_k]\Pr[W_{k'}]/\log n)$ for
disjoint pairs $k\ne k'$ (with $\E\sum_kW_k\gtrsim\log^2n$ the
diagonal term is already $O(1/\log^2 n)$), in the space of completions
of two rows and two columns.  No fairness of any global statistic is needed anywhere.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[relation to the earlier programme]
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} is the orbit identity of the trade chain
(\S\ref{sec:s13tradechain}(b)) specialised to the abelian subgroup
generated by the ladder trades, for which the orbit structure is
explicit: on each orbit the bit is \emph{exactly} fair unless no ladder
pair is flippable.  Compared with the pool of intercalates
(Theorem~\ref{thm:master}) the ladder has deterministic bit-changing
pairs and an exact bias formula, at the price of needing the pairs
to be flippable; compared with the trade chain it needs no mixing.
The quenched version (fixed $R$) reduces the fairness Question of
\texttt{paper/fairness\_question.tex} to the quenched form of (L):
$|2\Pr[B\mid R]-1|\le\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid R]$, and since the
ladder classes refine the components of the trade chain, also
$|2p(K)-1|\le\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid K]$ for every component
$K$.  Items (I), (II), (III), (T),
(F1), (F2) and the component question are superseded by (L).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Offset ladders, and what (L) needs}\label{sec:ladderoffset}

The ladder is one of a family.  For an integer $c$ (an \emph{offset}) put
\[
K_c=\begin{cases}\min(|C_1|-c,\ |C_2|)&\text{on }A,\\
\min(d_{21}-c,\ d_{12})&\text{on }B,\end{cases}
\qquad
D_c=\bigl\{(x_{c+i},\,y_i)=(\pi^{-(c+i)}(1),\pi^{-i}(2)):\ 1\le i<K_c\bigr\},
\]
so $D_0$ is the ladder and for $c>0$ the $x$-row is $c$ steps deeper
than the $y$-row ($x$-depths $c+1,\dots,c+K_c-1$ stay inside
$C_1\setminus\{1\}$, resp.\ the arc from $2$ to $1$; $y$-depths
$1,\dots,K_c-1$ inside $C_2\setminus\{2\}$, resp.\ the arc from $1$ to
$2$).  We take $c\ge0$; the families with the $y$-row deeper are
obtained by exchanging the roles of rows $1$ and $2$ (and of
$C_1,C_2$, $d_{12},d_{21}$) throughout.

\begin{theorem}[offset ladders]\label{thm:ladderoffset}
For every offset $c\ge0$, on the domain $\mathcal D_c=\{K_c\ge2\}$
(where $D_c\ne\emptyset$) the $j'$-trades at the pairs of $D_c$ are commuting
involutions that preserve $K_c$, the unordered pairs of $D_c$ and their
flippabilities, and toggle the bit iff the traded pair is flippable.
Hence, with $\mathrm{Flip}_c$ the set of flippable pairs of $D_c$,
\[
\Pr[B,\mathcal D_c]-\Pr[A,\mathcal D_c]
=\Pr[B,\mathcal D_c,\mathrm{Flip}_c=\emptyset]-\Pr[A,\mathcal D_c,\mathrm{Flip}_c=\emptyset].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identical to Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder}; only the bookkeeping changes.
Let $\pi'=(x_{c+i}\,y_i)\circ\pi$.  The preimage chain of $1$ under
$\pi'$ agrees with that of $\pi$ up to depth $c+i$ and then continues
along the chain of $2$: $\pi'^{-(c+i+t)}(1)=y_{i+t}$; symmetrically
$\pi'^{-(i+t)}(2)=x_{c+i+t}$ ($t\ge1$).  So the pair of $D_c$ at
$y$-depth $i'>i$ is $\{y_{i'},x_{c+i'}\}$, unchanged as a set, and the
pairs at depth $i'<i$ are untouched: $D_c$ is preserved.  (The same
computation shows that trading a pair at depths $(k,k')$ sends a
deeper pair at depths $(K,K')$ to depths $(K'+k-k',\,K-k+k')$, whose
offset is $2(k-k')-(K-K')$; it is preserved iff $K-K'=k-k'$, so only
families of constant offset, i.e.\ the $D_c$, are preserved.)  On $A$
the trade merges $C_1,C_2$ into a cycle with
$d'_{12}=|C_1|-(c+i)+i=|C_1|-c$ and $d'_{21}=|C_2|+c$, so
$K'_c=\min(d'_{21}-c,d'_{12})=\min(|C_2|,|C_1|-c)=K_c$; on $B$ the pair
separates rows $1,2$ ($c+i<d_{21}$ puts $x_{c+i}$ on the arc from $2$
to $1$, $i<d_{12}$ puts $y_i$ on the arc from $1$ to $2$) and gives
$|C'_1|=d_{12}+c$, $|C'_2|=d_{21}-c$, again
$K'_c=\min(d_{12},d_{21}-c)=K_c$.  No pair of $D_c$ is frame-adjacent:
$y_i=\pi(x_{c+i})$ would need $d_{21}=c-1$ (or $\pi(1)=2$ when $c=0$)
and $x_{c+i}=\pi(y_i)$ would need $d_{21}=c+1$, both excluded on
$\mathcal D_c$.  Commutation,
preservation of flippability and the orbit count are as before.
\end{proof}

Exact enumeration at $n=7$ ($\lambda=(7)$, $\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{2,5\}$)
confirms the identities for $c=0,1,2,3$ to all digits
($\Pr[B,\mathcal D_c]-\Pr[A,\mathcal D_c]=-0.005848,\ +0.001462,\
+0.003509,\ -0.001316$, equal to the right-hand sides).

\paragraph{Using the offsets to price short arcs.}  Fix $\ell\ge2$ and
consider $B$-instances with $d_{12}=\ell$.  For $0\le c\le n/2-\ell$
and $d_{21}\ge c+\ell$ one has $K_c=\ell$, so $D_c$ has $\ell-1$ pairs,
and the offset-$c$ trade at a flippable pair of $D_c$ lands in $A$
with $|C_1|=\ell+c\le n/2$ and $|C_2|=d_{21}-c\ge\ell$.  Within a
class of $\mathcal D_c$ all $B$-members have the same $(d_{12},d_{21})$
and all $A$-members the same $(|C_1|,|C_2|)=(d_{12}+c,d_{21}-c)$, and
$\mathrm{Flip}_c$ is a class invariant, so Theorem~\ref{thm:ladderoffset}
class by class and Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offsetprice}
\Pr\bigl[B,\ d_{12}=\ell,\ d_{21}\ge c+\ell,\ \mathrm{Flip}_c\ne\emptyset\bigr]
=\Pr\bigl[A,\ |C_1|=\ell+c,\ |C_2|\ge\ell,\ \mathrm{Flip}_c\ne\emptyset\bigr]
\le\frac{18}{n-\ell-c-1}\le\frac{36}{n-2}.
\end{equation}
Averaging over $c$: if $\theta(L,P)$ denotes the fraction of offsets
$c\in[0,n/2-\ell]$ whose diagonal $D_c$ contains a flippable pair,
then for every $\theta_0>0$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:offsetavg}
\Pr\bigl[B,\ d_{12}=\ell,\ |C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,\ \theta\ge\theta_0\bigr]\le\frac{36}{(n-2)\,\theta_0},
\end{equation}
since on $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$ one has $d_{21}=|C_1|-\ell\ge n/2\ge c+\ell$
for every such $c$, so $\E[\theta\,\mathbf 1_E]$ is the average over
$c$ of the left side of \eqref{eq:offsetprice}, and Markov's
inequality applies.  Together with the splice-in bound
$\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|<n/2+\ell]\le4/(n-2\ell+1)\le8/n$ for
$\ell\le n/4$ (the argument of Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iii) with
$n-|C_1|>n/2-\ell$ free rows), this prices every short arc except on
the event $\{\theta<\theta_0\}$.

\paragraph{The single remaining input.}  Say that a pair of rows
$(x,y)$, $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$, carries a \emph{witness} if the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through column $j$ has length at most
$\ell'=\log^5n$ and does not contain $j'$ (then $(x,y)$ is flippable).
A frame-adjacent pair ($y=\pi(x)$, so $\rho_{x,y}(j)=j'$) never
carries one; the families below contain no such pair.
For a pair of rows of a random Latin square the length of the
$j$-cycle is spread like a uniform variable on $\{2,\dots,n\}$
(Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iv)), so a witness has probability
$\approx\ell'/n$.

\begin{hypothesis}[witness lemma]\label{hyp:witness}
Let $(L,P)$ be uniform on $X$.  Let $\mathcal G$ be one of the
following families of \emph{blocks} of pairs of rows, determined by
the frame: (i) the ladder, each pair a block of its own; (ii) for a
$B$-instance with $d_{12}=\ell\le n/4$ and $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$, the
diagonals $D_c$, $0\le c\le n/2-\ell$, each a block of $\ell-1$ pairs.
Let $G_{\mathcal G}$ be the number of blocks containing a pair that
carries a witness, and
$\mu_{\mathcal G}=\sum_{\mathcal B\in\mathcal G}\min\bigl(\tfrac12,\ell'|\mathcal B|/n\bigr)$.
Then there is a constant $\kappa>0$ such that
\[
\Pr\bigl[G_{\mathcal G}<\kappa\,\mu_{\mathcal G}\ \text{ and }\ \mu_{\mathcal G}\ge\log^2n\bigr]=o(1/\log n).
\]
(This is annealed: an exceptional set of frames of probability
$o(1/\log n)$ is allowed.  For a single pair of non-adjacent rows the
witness probability is $\approx\ell'/n$, Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iv),
so $\kappa\mu_{\mathcal G}$ is a constant fraction of the expected
count if blocks are nearly independent given their shared rows.)
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{proposition}[(L) follows from the witness lemma]\label{prop:Lwitness}
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness} implies \textup{(L)}, hence the weak CGW
conjecture.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $\ell_0=n/\log^2n$.  By Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder},
$|\Pr[B]-\Pr[A]|\le\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]\le
\Pr[K_0\ge\ell_0,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]+\Pr[A,K_0<\ell_0]+\Pr[B,K_0<\ell_0]$.

\emph{Long ladders.}  On $\{K_0\ge\ell_0\}$ take family (i):
$\mu\ge(\ell_0-1)\ell'/n\ge\log^2n$, so $G\ge\kappa\mu\ge1$ except on an
event of probability $o(1/\log n)$, and $G\ge1$ means
$\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset$.

\emph{Short cycles.}  $\Pr[A,K_0<\ell_0]\le72\ell_0/n=72/\log^2n$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped}.

\emph{Short arcs.}  $\Pr[B,K_0<\ell_0]\le\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}\bigl(\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell]+\Pr[B,d_{21}=\ell]\bigr)$;
we treat $d_{12}=\ell$, and $d_{21}=\ell$ is the same argument with the
roles of rows $1,2$ exchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} covers
$C_2$, and the offset families become those with the $y$-row deeper).
Fix $\ell$.  The part with $|C_1|<n/2+\ell$ costs $\le8/n$.  On
$|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$ take family (ii), with $N=\lfloor n/2-\ell\rfloor+1\ge n/4$
blocks: $\mu=N\min(\tfrac12,(\ell-1)\ell'/n)\ge\tfrac14\min(n/2,(\ell-1)\ell')\ge\log^2n$,
so except on an event $F_\ell$ of probability $o(1/\log n)$ the
fraction of offsets whose diagonal contains a flippable pair is
$\theta=G/N\ge\theta_0(\ell):=\kappa\min(\tfrac12,(\ell-1)\ell'/n)$.  By
\eqref{eq:offsetavg},
\[
\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,\theta\ge\theta_0(\ell)]
\le\sum_{\ell-1\le n/(2\ell')}\frac{36\,n}{(n-2)\,\kappa\,(\ell-1)\ell'}
+\sum_{n/(2\ell')<\ell-1<\ell_0}\frac{72}{(n-2)\kappa}
=O\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{\ell'}+\frac{\ell_0}{n}\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\frac1{\log^2n}\Bigr),
\]
and $\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}8/n\le8/\log^2n$.  The exceptional events: the
family (ii) is a function of the frame, and the events $\{d_{12}=\ell\}$
are disjoint, so $\sum_\ell\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,F_\ell]
=\Pr\bigl[\bigcup_\ell\{B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,F_\ell\}\bigr]$ is
the probability of a single failure event of the hypothesis, which is
$o(1/\log n)$.
Altogether $\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]=o(1/\log n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the witness lemma is, and is not]
(i) It is a statement about substructures of polylogarithmic size
(a cycle of length $\le\log^4n$ of a row-pair permutation through a
given column), in the space of completions of two rows and two
columns, for families of pairs selected by the frame.  It asks for a
lower bound on their expected number and a lower-tail
concentration (a second moment with relative variance $o(1/\log n)$
suffices) --- the territory of
the switching method \cite{KS18}: a switching that shortens or lengthens
the $j$-cycle of $\rho_{x,y}$ by a bounded amount, applied in the
conditional space, compares the counts of squares with
$j$-cycle lengths $m$ and $m+1$ and gives the uniform spread, and the
same switchings applied to two pairs at once control the covariance.
(ii) It asks nothing about long cycles, same-cycle events, or the
fairness of any global statistic.  (iii) Rigid squares ($\mathbb Z_p$
isotopes and their relatives, where no row pair has a short cycle)
enter only through the failure probability, which must be
$o(1/\log n)$; for them the hypothesis is simply the statement that
they are rare.  (iv) The constants: $\ell'=\log^5n$ and
$\ell_0=n/\log^2n$ are one admissible choice; the proof needs
$\ell_0\ell'/n\ge\log^2n$, $\ell_0/n=o(1/\log n)$ and
$\log n/\ell'=o(1/\log n)$.  (v) The block form is what the
short-arc argument uses: the number of offsets with a witness, not the
number of witnesses, since the diagonals of different offsets are
dependent through the shared rows $y_i$.  (vi) The hypothesis is
stated only for the two families used; a version for arbitrary
frame-determined families would be false, since frame-adjacent pairs
never carry a witness.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Status of the witness lemma: literature and data}\label{sec:witnessstatus}

\paragraph{Data.}  \texttt{s13\_witness.py} (\texttt{runs/s13/f1/witness50.log}:
$9000$ instances at $n=50$).  The single-pair witness frequency for
ladder pairs is $0.079,0.168,0.314$ for $\ell'=5,10,20$ against
$(\ell'-1)/(n-1)=0.082,0.184,0.388$ (the deficit is the probability
that $j'$ lies on the short cycle, $\approx\ell'/n$).  For two
disjoint ladder pairs $\E[WW']/p^2=1.03,1.00,0.99$ ($1.7\cdot10^5$
samples).  The number $G$ of ladder pairs with a witness and the number
$S$ of rows $x$ preceding row $1$ whose pair with $y_1=\pi^{-1}(2)$
carries a witness (the ``star'' family, all pairs sharing one row)
both have variance equal to the binomial value within the resolution
of the data (ratios $0.95$--$1.1$ in bins of $K_0$, resp.\ of the star
size; the inflation in the smallest bins is the spread of $K_0$ within
a bin).  So at $n=50$ the witness indicators behave as independent
events of probability $\approx\ell'/n$, both across disjoint pairs and
across pairs sharing a row, to within a few percent --- the hypothesis
holds with margin in the data.

\paragraph{Literature (session-13 scout; see the references).}  Nothing
in print gives the witness lemma, but the tools are there.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item Cavenagh--Greenhill--Wanless \cite{CGW08} work with switchings
inside two rows (flip, backflip, cross-switch), uniformly over an
arbitrary prescription of a set of full columns, and prove that the
cycle-type law of a row pair is within a factor $3/2$ of the
derangement law per merge/split (their Theorem~3.13), within $n^{3/2}$
overall for types with $\le\frac65\log n$ cycles (Corollary~4.6),
$\Pr[\text{single $n$-cycle}]\in[2n^{-2},2n^{-2/3}]$ (Lemma~4.4), and
whp more than $(\log n)^{1-c}$ cycles (Theorem~4.11).  Their
Theorem~3.13 is exactly $\Pr_X[A]\ge1/3$ (\S\ref{sec:cgwcycles}), and
summed over types it gives the unconditional lower-bound clause of the
witness lemma with the right dependence on $\ell'$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles}); what it does not give is the
conditional version (two prescribed columns on which the two rows
carry distinct symbols) or the concentration.
\item Allsop--Morris \cite{AM26} give universal bounds
$(\delta/n)^{|P|}\le\Pr[L\supseteq P]\le(\Delta/n)^{|P|}$ for partial
squares $P$ on $\le\alpha n$ rows and $\le\beta n$ columns, $2\alpha+\beta<1$.
Summed over the patterns of a $j$-cycle of length $\ell$ this gives
$\Pr[\text{length }\ell]\in[\delta^{2\ell},\Delta^{2\ell}]/n$: the
right order $1/n$ for each $\ell$, with an exponential-in-$\ell$ slack;
whether $\delta=\Delta=1+o(1)$ for small $P$ is stated as open there,
and that is exactly the witness lemma's lower-bound clause.  Their
hypotheses do not allow full prescribed columns together with
prescribed rows, so the conditioning on rows $1,2$ and columns $j,j'$
would have to be handled separately (the switchings never touch those
cells, so this should be bookkeeping).
\item Lower tails: Kwan--Sudakov and Kwan--Sah--Sawhney bound lower
tails of the \emph{total} intercalate count with $\exp(-n^{c})$ errors,
by comparison with Latin rectangles and the McKay--Wanless completion
ratio $e^{O(n\log^2n)}$; that technique is useless for a count of
polylogarithmic mean.  For the witness lemma one needs either a second
moment with pairwise covariances of relative size $o(1/\log n)$
(switchings on two pairs at once, exact to $1+O(\mathrm{polylog}/n)$)
or a Poisson-type lower tail from matched creation/destruction
degrees, as in the Kwan--Sudakov ratio method.
\item Nothing is known about any ``two given columns in different
cycles'' event with constant probability, in any conjugate form; the
witness lemma deliberately avoids such events.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Verdict.}  (L), hence the weak CGW conjecture, is reduced
to a local statement (Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:witness}) whose two clauses
--- $\Theta(\ell'/n)$ lower bounds for short $j$-cycles of prescribed
row pairs in the space of completions of two rows and two columns, and
lower-tail concentration of their count across a frame-determined
family --- are true in the data with margin and are of the kind the
two-row switching method of \cite{CGW08,KS18} is designed for.  The
unconditional form of the first clause follows from \cite{CGW08}
(Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} below); the conditional form and
the concentration are what remains.

\subsection{The unconditional witness bound follows from CGW's switching theorem}
\label{sec:cgwcycles}

The lower-bound clause of the witness lemma, for a fixed pair of rows
of a uniform square without any conditioning, is a consequence of a
theorem that has been in print since 2008.  Cavenagh, Greenhill and
Wanless \cite{CGW08} (Lemma 3.12, Theorem 3.13) prove, for adjacent
types $\lambda$ (one part $m=\alpha+\beta$) and $\mu$ (that part split
into $\alpha\ne\beta$), that
\[
\tfrac12\;\le\;\frac{|S(\lambda)|\,\lambda_m\,m}{|S(\mu)|\,\mu_\alpha\mu_\beta\,\alpha\beta}
\;\le\;\tfrac32 ,
\]
uniformly over $\lambda,\alpha,\beta$ (and over any prescription of a
set of full columns on which rows $1,2$ carry the same symbols).  In
the notation of \S\ref{sec:marked} the middle term is $|X|/|J|$ for
$X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$ and $J=J(n,\mu;\alpha,\beta)$, so by
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} ($|J|=2|X_A|$) the CGW theorem is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgwA}
\tfrac13\;\le\;\Pr_X[A]\;\le\;1
\qquad\text{for every }n,\lambda,\alpha,\beta\quad\text{(lower bound: CGW($3/2$), see Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap})}
\end{equation}
(for $\alpha=\beta$ CGW's second form of the lemma and the $\alpha=\beta$
conventions of \S\ref{sec:marked} give the same middle term $|X|/|J|$):
rows $1,2$ lie in different frame cycles of a marked pair with
probability at least $1/3$.  The bound is attained: at $n=5$,
$(5)\to(2,3)$, $|X_A|=1440$, $|X_B|=2880$, $\Pr_X[A]=1/3$ exactly, and
CGW's $3/2$ is sharp there.  (The upper bound $\Pr_X[A]\le1$ is the
trivial direction $|J|\le2|X|$ of our identity; CGW's constant $1/2$
is its switching-count shadow.)  The conjecture asks for $\Pr_X[A]=\frac12+o(1/\log n)$.

\begin{remark}[a gap in the published proof of CGW's Lemma~3.12]\label{rem:cgwgap}
The lower bound in \eqref{eq:cgwA} is CGW's upper bound $3/2$, and
its proof requires every splitting pair of a $\lambda$-square to
carry exactly two edges.  Case~3 of their splitting procedure
(``$\{j,j'\}$ and $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$ both $B$-pairs:
cross-switch with respect to $\{\omega j,\omega j'\}$, then backflip
with respect to $\{j,j'\}$'') does not produce an element of
$S(\mu,F)$ when $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$.  Indeed the rows-$1,2$ part of
the cross-switch at a $B$-pair $(J,J')$ with $\omega^\alpha J=J'$ is
forced by the proof of their Lemma~3.10 (row $1$ keeps the symbol set
$\{1\circ\omega^kJ:1\le k\le\alpha\}$ on the cells
$\{\omega^kJ:1\le k<\alpha\}\cup\{J'\}$, and in every interior column
the two rows are swapped), which gives $1'\circ\omega^kJ=1\circ\omega^{k+1}J$
for $1\le k<\alpha$, $1'\circ J'=1\circ\omega J$, $2'\circ\omega^kJ=1\circ\omega^kJ$,
$2'\circ J=1\circ J'$; hence $\omega'(J)=\omega^{\alpha-1}J$,
$\omega'(\omega^kJ)=\omega^{k-1}J$ for $2\le k<\alpha$, $\omega'(\omega J)=J'$:
the cross-switch \emph{reverses the $\omega$-arc from $J$ to $J'$}.
With $(J,J')=(\omega j,\omega j')$ the reversed arc has $j'=\omega^{\alpha-1}J$
in its interior, so afterwards $\omega'(j)=\omega j$ and
$\omega'(\omega j)=j'$: the pair $\{j,j'\}$ is at distance $2$, and the
backflip at it splits the $(\alpha+\beta)$-cycle into parts $2$ and
$\alpha+\beta-2$.  (For $\alpha=2$ the arc has a single interior
column and nothing changes, so the proof is complete for
$\min(\alpha,\beta)=2$ --- hence at $n\le5$, where it is sharp.)  The
joining rule~4 fails symmetrically, and the two multigraphs are not
equal.  Independent check: \texttt{s13\_cgwcase3.py} implements the
cross-switch (rows $1,2$ as above, columns $j,j'$ swapped on the
$\delta_{j,j'}$-arc from row $2$ to row $1$, which is what balances
the corner cells), verifies that it is a Latin square of the same type
in every instance, and finds that case~3 lands in the wrong type in
$2676$ of $2676$ instances at $n=9,12$ with $\min(\alpha,\beta)\ge3$
(a referee subagent found the gap first, with its own implementation).
We found no published correction (the text we use is the journal
version, DOI 10.1002/rsa.20216, and the example on its p.~293 exhibits
the reversal: $\omega=(1234567)$, $j=2$, $j'=5$, $\alpha=3$, and after
the cross-switch $\omega'=(1243567)$).

\emph{A partial repair.}  A cross-switch at a $B$-pair $(J,J')$ at
any distance $a\ge2$ is a type-preserving trade (the proof of
Lemma~3.10 does not use $a=\alpha$), it reverses the $\omega$-arc from
$J$ to $J'$, and it toggles $\{j,j'\}$ iff exactly one of $j,j'$ lies in
the interior of that arc, say at position $i$; the distance from $j$
to $j'$ then changes by $a-2i$.  So the distance-preserving toggles
of $\{j,j'\}$ are exactly the cross-switches at pairs \emph{symmetric
about $j$ or about $j'$} ($a=2i$), and they exist as $B$-pairs or not.
When $\alpha$ is even and $\alpha<2\beta$, the pair
$R=(\omega^{\alpha/2}j,\omega^{3\alpha/2}j)$ --- symmetric about $j'$,
at distance $\alpha$, with $j$ outside its arc --- always gives an
edge: if $R$ is $B$, cross-switch at $R$ and backflip at $\{j,j'\}$
(now an $A$-pair at distance $\alpha$); if $R$ is $A$, backflip at
$R$ itself.  Symmetrically for $\beta$ even and $\beta<2\alpha$.
(\texttt{s13\_cgwfix.py}: type $\mu$ in all $3928$ covered instances
at $n=10,13$.)  The $\mu$-side multiplicities change (an $A$-pair's
backflip may serve two splitting pairs), so the constant $3/2$ would
have to be recomputed.  When $\alpha,\beta$ are both odd, every
distance-preserving toggle has even arc length $a\ne\alpha,\beta$, so
if all the symmetric pairs are $A$-pairs no one-cross-switch repair
exists and we see no canonical repair; the same holds for
$\alpha\ge2\beta$ with $\beta$ odd.  The gap is therefore open in
those classes, which the reduction chains do use.

\emph{Our own machinery bears on the missing direction.}  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder}, $\Pr_X[B]-\Pr_X[A]=\Pr_X[B,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]-\Pr_X[A,\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]\le\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]$,
so $\Pr_X[B]\le\tfrac12+\tfrac12\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset]$: CGW's
$3/2$, i.e.\ $\Pr_X[B]\le2/3$, follows from
$\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}\ne\emptyset]\ge1/3$ --- a constant-probability
version of (L), e.g.\ (CP) with $c\ge1/3$ for a single ladder pair.
Conversely nothing in the present notes uses CGW($3/2$) on the main
line.

\emph{What survives:} the direction
$|S(\lambda)|N_\lambda\ge\tfrac12|S(\mu)|N_\mu$, which by
Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} is the trivial $|X|\ge|X_A|$; CGW's
Lemma~4.1 upper bound ($|S(\lambda,F)|(m-1)!/(|S((m),F)|\gamma(\lambda))\le2^{\kappa-1}$),
Lemma~4.4's lower bound $\Pr[(m)]\ge2/m^2$, and the upper bounds of
Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} (the factor $2$, hence
$\E[N_\ell]\le(4+o(1))/\ell$).  \emph{What is now conditional} on the
truth of CGW's $3/2$ --- call it \textup{CGW($3/2$)}, equivalently
$\Pr_X[B]\le2/3$ for all $(\lambda,\alpha,\beta)$, a weak form of our
own target $\Pr_X[B]=\tfrac12+o(1/\log n)$ --- is: the lower bound
$\tfrac13$ in \eqref{eq:cgwA}; the lower bounds (factor $\tfrac23$) of
Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} and the witness bound
$(\tfrac23-o(1))(\ell'-1)/n$; the unconditional (CP) constant $2/5$
of \S\ref{sec:crossspace}; the ``$\ge\tfrac13$'' of \eqref{eq:CPcols};
CGW's $\Pr[(n)]\le2n^{-2/3}$ and Corollary~4.5, hence the tail bound
$\PP_n(\kappa>A\log n)=O(n^{-2/3})$ used in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} --- now re-derived from the surviving
direction in Proposition~\ref{prop:tailsurvive}.  The
data ($\Pr_X[B]\approx\tfrac12$ throughout) leave no doubt about the
statement, and the exact tables at $n\le11$ verify it there.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[the number of cycles, from the surviving direction only]\label{prop:tailsurvive}
Let $L$ be a uniform Latin square of order $n$, $\sigma$ the column
permutation of rows $1,2$, $N_\ell$ its number of $\ell$-cycles and
$\kappa=\sum_\ell N_\ell$.  Then, using only the identity
$|J|=2|X_A|$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:marked} and $|X_A|\le|X|$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(i)] $\E[\ell N_\ell]\le2n/(n-\ell)$ for every $\ell$; in
particular $\E[N_\ell]\le4/\ell$ for $\ell\le n/2$.
\item[(ii)] For every $k\ge1$, with $(\kappa)_k=\kappa(\kappa-1)\cdots(\kappa-k+1)$,
\[
\E\bigl[(\kappa)_k\bigr]\;\le\;2^{k-1}\sum_{\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k\ge2}\frac{\E[mN_m]}{\ell_1\cdots\ell_k}\Big|_{m=\ell_1+\cdots+\ell_k}
\;\le\;2^{k-1}\Bigl(4+\frac{k^2}{\log n}\Bigr)(\log n)^k .
\]
\item[(iii)] For $A\ge3$, $\Pr[\kappa\ge A\log n]\le(7+\log n)\,n^{-\log((A-1)/2)}$,
which is $O(n^{-2}\log n)$ for $A\ge16$;
and $\Pr[\text{all cycles of }\sigma\text{ have the same length }\ell<n]\le4d(n)/n$,
$d(n)$ the number of divisors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For $\beta\ne\ell$ the joining instances $(L,\{j,j'\})$ with $j$ in
an $\ell$-cycle and $j'$ in a $\beta$-cycle number $\ell N_\ell\beta N_\beta$
per square, the splitting instances (pairs at distance $\ell$ on an
$(\ell+\beta)$-cycle) number $(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}$, and
$|J|=2|X_A|\le2|X|$ gives
$\E[\ell N_\ell\,\beta N_\beta]\le2\E[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}]$; for
$\beta=\ell$ the same count with $\ell^2\binom{N_\ell}{2}$ and
$\ell N_{2\ell}$ gives $\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]\le4\ell\E[N_{2\ell}]$.
Summing the first over $\beta\ne\ell$ ($\sum_\beta\beta N_\beta=n$) and
adding the second (its right side is the missing summand $m=2\ell$):
$\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]+\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]\le2\E[\sum_{m\ge\ell+2}mN_m]\le2n$.  Hence
$n\E[\ell N_\ell]=\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]+\E[\ell^2N_\ell(N_\ell-1)]+\ell\E[\ell N_\ell]
\le2n+\ell\E[\ell N_\ell]$.

(ii) Count the instances $(L,(C_1,j_1),\dots,(C_k,j_k))$: $C_i$
pairwise distinct $\sigma$-cycles of $L$ of lengths $\ell_i$ and
$j_i\in C_i$; there are $\ell_1\cdots\ell_k$ of them per ordered
$k$-tuple of distinct cycles with these lengths.  Join $C_1$ and $C_2$
at the pair $\{j_1,j_2\}$ by a joining step: flip if the pair is an
$A$-pair; if it is a $B$-pair, first trade the row cycle $C_2$ of $j_2$
(a switch in the sense of Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle}, whose proof
applies to the row cycle through either column of the pair; it inverts
$C_2$ in place and does not touch the cycle of $j_1$), then flip.  The
flip replaces $\sigma$ by $\sigma\circ(j_1\,j_2)$ on rows $1,2$, so
the result has $j_1,j_2$ on one cycle of length $\ell_1+\ell_2$ with
$j_2=\sigma'^{\ell_2}(j_1)$ whether or not the trade was applied
(CGW Lemma~3.3), every other cycle of $\sigma$ (in particular
$C_3,\dots,C_k$) untouched, and the map is at most two-to-one (the
backflip at $\{j_1,j_2\}$ returns $L$ or the traded square).  Repeat
with $\{j_1,j_3\}$, \dots, $\{j_1,j_k\}$, always trading the cycle of
the new column when needed, so that the cycle containing $j_1$ is never
inverted: after step $i$ every earlier $j_r$ is shifted by exactly
$\ell_{i+1}$.  After $k-1$ steps all $j_i$ lie on one cycle of length
$m=\sum\ell_i$ at the positions $j_i=\sigma'^{\ell_i+\cdots+\ell_k}(j_1)$,
determined by $j_1$ and $(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)$, and the composite is at
most $2^{k-1}$-to-one.  (With CGW's min-symbol rule the trade may
invert the merged cycle and the positions are not determined; the
count would then be $2^{2k-3}$-to-one.)  Hence the number of instances with lengths
$(\ell_i)$ is at most $2^{k-1}\sum_{L'}mN_m(L')$, i.e.\
$\E[\ell_1\cdots\ell_k\,T(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_k)]\le2^{k-1}\E[mN_m]$ with
$T$ the number of ordered $k$-tuples of distinct cycles with these
lengths; summing over $(\ell_i)$ gives the first bound, since
$\sum_{(\ell_i)}T=(\kappa)_k$.  For the second, split at $m\le n/2$,
where $\E[mN_m]\le4$ by (i) and
$\sum_{m\le n/2}\sum_{\sum\ell_i=m}\prod\ell_i^{-1}\le(\sum_{\ell\le n/2}\ell^{-1})^k\le(\log n)^k$,
and $m>n/2$, where some $\ell_i\ge m/k$, so
$\sum_{\sum\ell_i=m}\prod\ell_i^{-1}\le k\cdot(k/m)(\log n)^{k-1}$, and
$\sum_{m>n/2}mN_m\cdot k^2(\log n)^{k-1}/m\le k^2(\log n)^{k-1}$ because at
most one cycle is longer than $n/2$.

(iii) Markov for $(\kappa)_k$ with $k=\lceil\log n\rceil\le\log n+1$:
on $\{\kappa\ge K\}$, $(\kappa)_k\ge(K-k+1)^k\ge((A-1)\log n)^k$ for
$K=A\log n$, and $k^2/\log n\le\log n+3$, so
$\Pr[\kappa\ge K]\le2^{k-1}(4+k^2/\log n)(\log n)^k/((A-1)\log n)^k
\le(7+\log n)(2/(A-1))^{k}\le(7+\log n)(2/(A-1))^{\log n}$ for $A\ge3$,
and $(A-1)/2\ge e^2$ for $A\ge16$.  For the equal-length types,
$\ell\mid n$ and $\ell<n$ force $\ell\le n/2$, so
$\Pr[N_\ell=n/\ell]\le\E[N_\ell]\ell/n\le4/n$; $\ell=n$ is the $n$-cycle.
\end{proof}

With this, the tail estimates in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction}
no longer need \cite[Cor.~4.5]{CGW08}: $\PP_n(\kappa>K)=O(n^{-2}\log n)$
for $K=\lceil16\log n\rceil$ and $\PP_n(\text{non-plain})=O(n^{-1+o(1)})$,
which is all the proof uses (the exponent of $n$ in the tail only has
to be negative).


\begin{proposition}[short cycles in two rows; lower bounds under CGW($3/2$), Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}]\label{prop:shortcycles}
Let $L$ be a uniform Latin square of order $n$, $\sigma$ the column
permutation of two fixed rows, $N_\ell$ the number of $\ell$-cycles of
$\sigma$, and $Q_q$ the $\sigma$-cycle through a fixed column $q$.
For all $\ell\ne\beta$ with $\ell,\beta\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgwprod}
\tfrac23\,\E\bigl[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}\bigr]\;\le\;\E[\ell N_\ell\cdot\beta N_\beta]\;\le\;2\,\E\bigl[(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}\bigr].
\end{equation}
Consequently, with $\tau_n=\Pr[\sigma\text{ has at least }10\sqrt n\text{ cycles}]$,
which is $e^{-\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ for $n\ge n_0$ (\cite{CGW08}, Theorem~4.9; the
theorem is asymptotic, so no explicit $n_0$), for every $2\le\ell\le\sqrt n/40$,
\[
\frac{2}{3\ell}\Bigl(1-\frac{(\ell+1)(4+n\tau_n)}{n}\Bigr)\;\le\;\E[N_\ell]\;\le\;\frac{4+n\tau_n}{\ell},
\qquad
\Pr\bigl[|Q_q|=\ell\bigr]=\frac{\ell\,\E N_\ell}{n}\in\Bigl[\frac{2/3-o(1)}{n},\ \frac{4+o(1)}{n}\Bigr].
\]
In particular $\Pr[|Q_q|\le\ell',\ q'\notin Q_q]\ge(\tfrac23-o(1))(\ell'-1)/n$
for any second column $q'$ and $\ell'\le\sqrt n/40$: a fixed pair of
rows carries a witness of length $\le\ell'$ with probability at least
$(2/3-o(1))(\ell'-1)/n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $\ell\ne\beta$ let $X(\ell,\beta)$ be the set of $(L,\{q,q'\})$
with $q,q'$ in a common $\sigma$-cycle of length $\ell+\beta$ and
$q'=\sigma^\ell(q)$, and $J(\ell,\beta)$ the set of $(L,\{q,q'\})$ with
$q$ in an $\ell$-cycle and $q'$ in a $\beta$-cycle.  Each $(L,\{q,q'\})$
in $X(\ell,\beta)$ has a type $\lambda$ with an $(\ell+\beta)$-part and
lies in $X(n,\lambda;\ell,\beta)$; splitting that part gives the type
$\mu$ of $J(n,\mu;\ell,\beta)\subseteq J(\ell,\beta)$, and this is a
bijection between the types occurring on the two sides.  Summing the
CGW inequality over types,
$\tfrac23|X(\ell,\beta)|\le|J(\ell,\beta)|\le2|X(\ell,\beta)|$.  Now
$|X(\ell,\beta)|=\sum_L(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}(L)$: every $q$ in an
$(\ell+\beta)$-cycle gives the pair $\{q,\sigma^\ell(q)\}$, and
distinct $q$ give distinct pairs because $\{q,\sigma^\ell q\}=\{q_2,\sigma^\ell q_2\}$
with $q_2\ne q$ forces $\sigma^{2\ell}q=q$, i.e.\ $\ell=\beta$.  And
$|J(\ell,\beta)|=\sum_L\ell N_\ell(L)\,\beta N_\beta(L)$ ($q$ in an
$\ell$-cycle, $q'$ in a $\beta$-cycle; the unordered pair determines
which is which since $\ell\ne\beta$).  Dividing by the number of
squares gives \eqref{eq:cgwprod}.

Sum \eqref{eq:cgwprod} over $\beta\ge2$, $\beta\ne\ell$.  Since
$\sigma$ is a derangement, $\sum_{\beta\ge2,\beta\ne\ell}\beta N_\beta=n-\ell N_\ell$,
and $\sum_{\beta}(\ell+\beta)N_{\ell+\beta}=n-W_\ell$ with
$W_\ell=\sum_{m\le\ell+1}mN_m+2\ell N_{2\ell}$ the number of columns in
cycles of length $\le\ell+1$ or exactly $2\ell$.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cgwsum}
\tfrac23\,\E[n-W_\ell]\;\le\;\E\bigl[\ell N_\ell\,(n-\ell N_\ell)\bigr]\;\le\;2\,\E[n-W_\ell]\;\le\;2n .
\end{equation}
Upper bound: on $\{\ell N_\ell\le n/2\}$ the middle term is at least
$\ell N_\ell\cdot n/2$, so $\E[\ell N_\ell;\ \ell N_\ell\le n/2]\le4$ and
$\E[mN_m]\le4+n\Pr[N_m>n/(2m)]\le4+n\tau_n$ for every $m\le\sqrt n/20$,
because $N_m>n/(2m)\ge10\sqrt n$ means at least $10\sqrt n$ cycles, and
CGW's Theorem~4.9, $\Pr[\ge k\text{ cycles}]=o\bigl((3/4)^ke^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}\bigr)$,
is $e^{-\Omega(\sqrt n)}$ for $k=10\sqrt n$ ($10\log\tfrac43=2.88>\pi\sqrt{2/3}=2.57$).
By column symmetry
$\Pr[|Q_q|=m]=\E[mN_m]/n\le(4+n\tau_n)/n$ for each $m$.  Lower bound:
$\E[\ell N_\ell]\cdot n\ge\E[\ell N_\ell(n-\ell N_\ell)]\ge\tfrac23(n-\E W_\ell)$
and $\E W_\ell/n=\sum_{m\le\ell+1}\Pr[|Q_q|=m]+\Pr[|Q_q|=2\ell]\le(\ell+1)(4+n\tau_n)/n$
($\ell+1$ terms, all with $m\le2\ell\le\sqrt n/20$, where the upper bound applies).
Finally $\Pr[q'\in Q_q\mid|Q_q|=m]=(m-1)/(n-1)$ by column symmetry,
which gives the last claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
(i) For a uniform derangement the two sides of \eqref{eq:cgwprod} are
\emph{exactly} equal (both count $n!\,D_{n-\ell-\beta}/((n-\ell-\beta)!\,D_n)$:
ordered pairs of an $\ell$- and a $\beta$-cycle against marked
$(\ell+\beta)$-cycles), so \eqref{eq:cgwprod} is a constant-factor
form of the cycle-count part of the CGW conjecture; type by type the
exact ratio is $2\Pr_X[A]$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:marked}.  At $n=5$ the
ratio $|J(2,3)|/|X(2,3)|$ is $2/3$ exactly.
(ii) Until now the literature had $\E N_\ell\ge c_\ell/\ell$ only with
$c_\ell$ decaying exponentially in $\ell$ (from the universal bounds of
Allsop--Morris \cite{AM26}), and $\E N_\ell\to1/\ell$ only for $\ell=2$
(Kwan--Sudakov, Kwan--Sah--Sawhney).  We have not found the constant-factor bound for all
$\ell\le\sqrt n/40$ in the literature, although it is an
immediate corollary of \cite{CGW08}; the point is that the switching
theorem compares a cycle of length $\ell+\beta$ with the \emph{pair}
of cycles $(\ell,\beta)$ in one step, so no loss accumulates along
$\ell$.
(iii) The data (Remark~\ref{rem:ladderdata}(iv)): $\Pr[|Q_q|=\ell]\approx1/n$
for all $\ell$ at $n=50,100$, inside $[0.66,4]/n$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{What is still missing for the witness lemma.}  The
proposition is unconditional.  The witness lemma needs the same lower
bound (a) for pairs of rows selected by the frame, i.e.\ conditionally
on columns $j,j'$, and (b) inside $X$, i.e.\ conditionally on the type
of $\sigma$ and the mark, together with (c) the lower-tail
concentration.  For (a), CGW's framework already allows an arbitrary
prescription of full columns, so conditioning on columns $j,j'$ is of
the kind their argument tolerates; the one obstacle is their standing
assumption that the two distinguished rows carry the same symbols on
the prescribed columns (so that their row-pair permutation restricted
to the free columns is a permutation).  For a ladder pair $(x,y)$ this
fails --- the four symbols $L(x,j),L(x,j'),L(y,j),L(y,j')$ are distinct,
since an equality would force $\pi(x)=y$ or $\pi(y)=x$ --- and the free
part of rows $x,y$ consists of cycles together with two paths; the
witness is precisely ``the path starting at the column where row $y$
holds $L(x,j)$ is short and ends at the column where row $x$ holds
$L(y,j)$''.  Whether CGW's three switchings and their counting extend
to this path structure is examined in \S\ref{sec:crossspace}: they do
not, and the reason is instructive.  (c) is the second-moment version
of the same question.

\paragraph{A duality (heuristic).}  Transposing the square exchanges
the roles.  With $T(c,r)=L(r,c)$ and $\rho$ in the convention
$L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$, the frame $\pi$ of $(j,j')$ in $L$ is the
row-pair permutation $\sigma_T:=\rho^T_{j,j'}$ of rows $j,j'$ of $T$, and
$\rho_{x,y}$ is the frame of the column pair $(x,y)$ of $T$.  So the
witness event for the ladder pair $(x,y)$ --- a short
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle through column $j$ avoiding $j'$ --- becomes the
event that the column pair $(x,y)$ of $T$ has rows $j,j'$ in different
frame cycles with the cycle of row $j$ short; the ladder pairs become
the column pairs $(\sigma_T^{-k}(c_1),\sigma_T^{-k}(c_2))$ of $T$, where
$c_1,c_2$ are the columns of $T$ that were rows $1,2$ of $L$; the bit
of $(L,P)$ becomes ``$c_1,c_2$ in a common $\sigma_T$-cycle''; and the
conditioning becomes: on $\sigma_T$ (over which the hypothesis is
annealed) and on the frame type of the column pair $(c_1,c_2)$ of $T$
(the type $\lambda$ of $L$).  So the conditional witness lemma is
equivalent to a \emph{lower} bound on short frame cycles of the
$\sigma_T$-shifts of a column pair, given $\sigma_T$ --- the opposite
direction to Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped}, which bounds them from
above by the splice-in switching.  The splice-in move
lengthens $C_1$ using a free cycle and is always available; the cut
that shortens it needs a flippable pair inside $C_1$.  This is the
same flippability obstruction in yet another guise; heuristically it
suggests what a proof of the witness lemma must contain --- a lower
bound on flippable pairs inside a frame cycle --- which is what CGW's
cross-switch supplies for the pair $(1,2)$ at the level of constants
(this is a reading of the structure, not a demonstrated reduction).

\subsection{The path version of CGW's lemma: where it breaks, and what survives}
\label{sec:crossspace}

Work in $\Omega(F)$, $F=$ the two full columns $p=j$, $p'=j'$, for a
pair of rows $x,y$ carrying four distinct symbols on $\{p,p'\}$ (every
ladder pair); this extends CGW's notation, whose $F$ is required to be
suitable for the distinguished rows.  Write $Q_p,Q_{p'}$ for the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycles through $p,p'$ (here $\rho_{x,y}$ is the column
permutation with $L(y,\rho_{x,y}(q))=L(x,q)$, so that
$\sigma=\rho_{1,2}$ up to inversion).  \emph{Crossed}: $p'\in Q_p$,
with arcs $P_1$ (from $p$ to $p'$, $d_1-1$ interior columns) and $P_2$
(from $p'$ to $p$, $d_2-1$); \emph{parallel}: $Q_p\ne Q_{p'}$, interior
sizes $\ell_p-1$, $\ell_{p'}-1$ (these two sizes are unrelated to the
split $(\alpha,\beta)$ of the mark).  Parallel is flippability.  All
other $\rho_{x,y}$-cycles are \emph{free}, and a \emph{free column} is
a column other than $p,p'$.  ``Flip'' below means the turn of a column
cycle of a free column pair $(q,c)$ through $x$ or through $y$ (CGW's
flip and backflip alike); ``flippable'' remains the property of the
row pair.

\paragraph{Which of CGW's moves are legal.}  A flip at a free column
pair $(q,c)$ (turn of the $(q,c)$-column cycle through $y$) never
touches columns $p,p'$ and is legal in $\Omega(F)$; it replaces
$\rho_{x,y}$ by $(q\,c)\circ\rho_{x,y}$ when $(q,c)$ is an $A$-pair
(rows $x,y$ in different $(q,c)$-column cycles).  A switch (trade of
rows $x,y$ along a $\rho$-cycle containing exactly one of $q,c$) is
legal iff that cycle is free.  A cross-switch at a $B$-pair $(q,c)$ in
one cycle is legal iff the $\rho$-arc from $q$ to $c$ avoids $p,p'$.
Hence: pairs $(q,c)$ inside one free cycle, or $q$ in a free cycle and
$c$ anywhere, or both on the same path with the arc inside the path,
are fully repairable as in CGW (cases 1--3 of their splitting
procedure), and the within-path/free-cycle statistics of
Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} go through in $\Omega(F)$.  But a
\emph{cross-path} pair --- $q\in P_1$, $c\in P_2$ in the crossed state,
or $q\in Q_p\setminus p$, $c\in Q_{p'}\setminus p'$ in the parallel
state --- cannot be repaired when it is a $B$-pair: in the parallel state the
switch would trade along $Q_p$ or $Q_{p'}$ (through $p$ or $p'$), and
in the crossed state both arcs between $q$ and $c$ contain $p$ or
$p'$, so the cross-switch is illegal too.  And the cross-path pairs
are exactly the ones whose flip (at an $A$-pair) exchanges crossed and
parallel.  CGW's condition that the distinguished rows carry the same
symbols on $F$ is therefore not cosmetic: it is what makes every pair
repairable, by excluding paths.  (This paragraph settles legality with
respect to the prescribed columns only.  Preservation of rows $1,2$,
needed in $X$, is automatic for the switch, which trades rows
$x,y\notin\{1,2\}$, and requires for a flip or a cross-switch that the
rows it changes --- those of the traded column cycle or column arc ---
avoid $\{1,2\}$ or contain both of them; the latter conjugates
$\sigma$ by $(q\,c)$ and keeps type and mark.  Only the
within-free-cycle statistics of Proposition~\ref{prop:shortcycles} go
through in $\Omega(F)$ without further work; the within-path
statistics meet boundary cases, when a neighbour pair
$\{\omega(j),\omega(j')\}$ hits $p$ or $p'$, which we have not
checked.)

\paragraph{What survives: two exact identities in the cross space.}
Identity (I) holds conditionally on the frame of $(p,p')$ and on rows
$1,2$ (it preserves both); identity (F) holds in $X$ conditionally on
the frame, and conditionally also on rows $1,2$ if legality is
restricted to column cycles avoiding rows $1,2$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[(F)] The flip at a cross-path $A$-pair is an involution between
crossed and parallel squares preserving the pair and its $A$-status
(Lemma~\ref{lem:cycletrade}), legal when the $(q,c)$-column cycle
through $y$ avoids rows $1,2$ (or contains both --- then $\sigma$ is
conjugated and the mark is kept, but rows $1,2$ change).  Summing,
$\E[\#\text{legal $A$ cross-path pairs};\ \mathrm{crossed}]
=\E[\#\text{legal $A$ cross-path pairs};\ \mathrm{parallel}]$.
\item[(I)] An intercalate on rows $x,z$ ($z\notin\{1,2,x\}$;
$z\ne y$ is automatic, since $\{q,c\}$ is never a $2$-cycle of
$\rho_{x,y}$ for the pairs counted) and free columns $q,c$ is a Latin
trade whose switch replaces $\rho_{x,y}$ by $\rho_{x,y}\circ(q\,c)$
and touches nothing else that matters; it is always legal.  It
separates $p,p'$ iff crossed with $q\in P_1$, $c\in P_2$, and merges
iff parallel with $q\in Q_p\setminus p$, $c\in Q_{p'}\setminus p'$.
With $I_{\rm sep}$, $I_{\rm merge}$ the numbers of such intercalates,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:icid}
\E[I_{\rm sep};\ \mathrm{crossed}]=\E[I_{\rm merge};\ \mathrm{parallel}]
\qquad\text{exactly, in every fibre (rows $1,2$ and columns $p,p'$ fixed).}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Identity (I) has the merit that its moves are local and automatically
legal, and its weights are intercalate counts of a single row with
columns constrained to two sets.  On real squares (\texttt{s13\_icsw.py},
\texttt{runs/s13/f1/icsw50.log}: $9000$ ladder pairs at $n=50$)
$\E[I_{\rm sep}\mid\mathrm{crossed}]=0.98\cdot\E[(d_1-1)(d_2-1)/n]$ and
$\E[I_{\rm merge}\mid\mathrm{parallel}]=0.98\cdot\E[(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)/n]$,
with ratio $0.97$--$0.99$ in every bin of the product.  But (I) is a
\emph{fair} identity and yields no inequality by itself: the only
deterministic bound is $I_{\rm merge}\le(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)$ (at
most one row $z$ per column pair), and this is attained up to
constants in structured squares --- in the Cayley table of
$\mathbb Z_8\times\mathbb Z_2$ a parallel pair with
$\ell_p=\ell_{p'}=8$ has $I_{\rm merge}=12>\min(\ell_p,\ell_{p'})-1$.
A constant lower bound on flippability would follow from (i)
$\E[I_{\rm sep}\mid\mathrm{crossed},d_1,d_2]\ge\kappa(d_1-1)(d_2-1)/n$,
(ii) an arc-trapping bound (crossed squares have macroscopic arcs
except with probability $\le\Pr[\mathrm{crossed}]-c$), and (iii) the
matching upper bound
$\E[I_{\rm merge}\mid\mathrm{parallel},\ell_p,\ell_{p'}]\le
C(\ell_p-1)(\ell_{p'}-1)/n$: two-sided control of the two-cell pattern
``$L(z,q_0)=L(x,c_0)$ in the row $z$ holding $L(x,q_0)$ at $c_0$''
conditionally on rows $1,2$, columns $p,p'$ and the structure of rows
$x,y$.  (An earlier version of this paragraph claimed
$I_{\rm merge}\le\min(\ell_p,\ell_{p'})-1$ and deduced
$\Pr[\mathrm{parallel}]\ge\frac2n\E[I_{\rm sep};\mathrm{crossed}]$;
both are wrong, as the example shows.)

\paragraph{Why neither input is available.}  The universal bounds of
Allsop--Morris require the conditioning to be a partial square on
$\le\alpha n$ rows and $\le\beta n$ columns; two full rows or two full
columns violate this.  CGW's framework tolerates full columns but not
full rows, and its repair moves fail across the paths.  For (ii), the
splice-in switching that proves Proposition~\ref{prop:trapped} needs
turns of column cycles through $y$ avoiding rows $1,2$ --- legality is
again a cycle-avoidance event --- while the intercalate version of the
splice has availability $1/n$ per position and yields only
$\Pr[\mathrm{crossed},d_1=d]\lesssim1/d$.  In every formulation the
obstruction is one and the same: in the space with two rows and two
columns prescribed there is no exact move that is both automatically
legal and able to change a cross-path status, and the probability
that such moves are available is a cycle-avoidance event of the kind
we are trying to bound.

\paragraph{The core problem, stated once.}  Let $(L,P)$ be uniform on
$X$, condition on rows $1,2$ and columns $p,p'$, and let $(x,y)$ be a
pair of rows carrying four distinct symbols on $\{p,p'\}$.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CP}
\textup{(CP)}\qquad
\Pr\bigl[p,p'\text{ lie in different cycles of }\rho_{x,y}\bigm|\text{rows }1,2,\ \text{columns }p,p'\bigr]\;\ge\;c>0 ,
\end{equation}
uniformly over the conditioning (or: for the ladder, not all pairs
crossed).  Which half of the conditioning is the obstacle is settled
by transposition (\S\ref{sec:orbit} below): with only the two
\emph{columns} prescribed, (CP) is CGW's Theorem~3.13 for the
transposed square and holds with $c=\frac13$ under CGW($3/2$)
(Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}) ($c=\frac12$ exactly, unconditionally, when
rows $1,2$ are apart in the frame); with only rows $1,2$ prescribed
(their type and the mark --- the conditioning that defines $X$) it is
the path version, which breaks for the reason above; with both it is
what (L) needs, and the frame conditioning is then free.  Everything
in \S\ref{sec:ladder}--\S\ref{sec:crossspace} is exact except this one
constant.

\paragraph{The unconditional core problem is settled with an explicit constant (under CGW($3/2$), Remark~\ref{rem:cgwgap}).}
For a uniform Latin square and fixed rows $1,2$ and columns $p,p'$ let
$W=\{p,p'\text{ in a common }\sigma\text{-cycle}\}$ ($\sigma$ the column
permutation of rows $1,2$) and $B=\{1,2\text{ in a common frame cycle
of }(p,p')\}$.  Three exact facts determine the joint law of $(W,B)$
up to one number.  (i) $\Pr[B]=\Pr[W]$: transposition preserves the
uniform measure and carries $W$ to the event $B$ for rows $p,p'$ and
columns $1,2$, which has the same probability as $B$ by row and
column relabelling.  (ii) $\Pr[B,\neg W]=\Pr[A,\neg W]$: on $\neg W$
the trade of rows $1,2$ along the $\sigma$-cycle through $p$ inverts
that cycle in place, so it is an involution preserving $\neg W$, and
it changes column $p$ at rows $1,2$ only, so it toggles $B$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle}, whose proof uses only
$p\in Q\not\ni p'$).  (iii) $\Pr[A\mid W,\lambda,\alpha]\ge\frac13$
for every type and every split with $\alpha,\beta\ge2$: conditionally
on the type of $\sigma$ and on the mark data, $L$ is uniform on a
fibre of $X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$ over the ordered mark, all such
fibres have the same $A$-probability by column relabelling, so this
is \eqref{eq:cgwA}; the pairs at $\sigma$-distance $1$ have $B$ surely
and total probability $\delta\le2/(n-1)$ by column symmetry.
Writing $w=\Pr[W]$: $\Pr[A,\neg W]=\Pr[B,\neg W]=\frac{1-w}2$,
$\Pr[B,W]=w-\frac{1-w}2=\frac{3w-1}2$, $\Pr[A,W]=\frac{1-w}2$, hence
$\frac{1-w}2\ge\frac13(w-\delta)$ and $\Pr[B,W]\ge0$ give
\[
\frac13\;\le\;\Pr[W]\;\le\;\frac35+\frac25\delta\;\le\;\frac35+\frac{4}{5(n-1)},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\Pr\bigl[p,p'\text{ in different cycles of }\rho_{1,2}\bigr]\;\ge\;\frac25-\frac{4}{5(n-1)},
\]
and $\E|Q_p|=1+(n-1)\Pr[W]\le(\frac35+o(1))n$ by column symmetry: the
cycle of two given rows through a given column covers at most three
fifths of the columns on average.  (The fairness conjecture is
$w=\frac12$; the data give $0.50$.  Exhaustively at $n=5$:
$w=\Pr[B]=11/14$, $\Pr[B,\neg W]=\Pr[A,\neg W]=3/28$,
$\Pr[B,W]=19/28$, and $w=\frac35+\frac25\delta$ exactly with
$\delta=13/28$, matching $\Pr_X[A]=\frac13$ at $n=5$.)  So (CP) holds
without any conditioning, with $c=2/5$, by combining the two exact
involutions with CGW's constant.  What is missing is the same
statement after conditioning on two rows and two columns.  For a
ladder pair $(x,y)$ in place of $(1,2)$: (i) fails (transposition does
not preserve the conditioning); (ii) survives the conditioning on rows
$1,2$ but not on columns $p,p'$, which the trade of rows $x,y$ alters
--- and with both prescribed the apart/together status of $(x,y)$ is
fixed by the frame, so the $2\times2$ table degenerates; (iii) is
CGW's lemma in the path version, which breaks as described.

\subsection{The orbit method: commuting column turns in $X$}
\label{sec:orbit}

This subsection records what the search for a switching proof of (CP)
produced: (a) a transposition remark that locates the obstacle
precisely; (b) an exact fairness statement for generic row pairs in
$X$; (c) a product bound for the ladder bits, which replaces the
witness lemma by a \emph{genericity} hypothesis; (d) data.  Throughout
$X=X(n,\lambda;\alpha,\beta)$, and ``legal'' means: preserves the
type of rows $1,2$ and the mark, i.e.\ maps $X$ to itself.

\paragraph{(a) Transposition: which half of the conditioning is the
obstacle.}  Let $L$ be uniform on \emph{all} Latin squares of order
$n$ (no condition on rows $1,2$ in this paragraph), let $x,y$ carry
four distinct symbols on $\{p,p'\}$, and condition on the two full
columns $p,p'$ (the frame $\pi$ of $(p,p')$).  In the transposed square $L^{\mathsf T}$ the
columns $p,p'$ are the distinguished rows, the frame $\pi$ is their
column permutation, and the event ``$p,p'$ lie in different cycles of
$\rho_{x,y}$'' is the event ``$\{x,y\}$ is an $A$-pair for the
distinguished rows''.  Conditioning on the two full rows $p,p'$ of
$L^{\mathsf T}$ is conditioning on their type and on the position of
the pair $\{x,y\}$ in their cycle structure, which is free by symbol
and column relabelling.  Hence, by Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle}
($|J_A|=|J_B|$, which needs no condition on the cycle lengths) and
\eqref{eq:cgwA}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CPcols}
\Pr\bigl[p,p'\text{ in different cycles of }\rho_{x,y}\bigm|\text{columns }p,p'\bigr]
\;=\;\tfrac12\ \text{ if $x,y$ are apart in the frame},\qquad
\ge\tfrac13\ \text{ if together at distances $\ge2$ (the latter under CGW($3/2$))}.
\end{equation}
So (CP) with the \emph{columns} prescribed is CGW's theorem; it is the
conditioning on rows $1,2$ --- on their type and the mark, which is
what defines $X$ and cannot be removed --- that is the path version.
In the same transposed picture the ladder trades $T_k$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} at flippable pairs are CGW's flips and
backflips at the column pairs $\{x_k,y_k\}$ of $L^{\mathsf T}$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:ladder} is exactly the legal half of CGW's
Lemma~3.12 for the distinguished rows $p,p'$: the other half --- the
switch and the cross-switch at $\{x_k,y_k\}$ --- trades the columns
$p,p'$ of $L$ along a frame cycle or frame arc through exactly one of
rows $1,2$ (apart: $C_1\ni1$, $C_1\not\ni2$; together: the arc from
$x_k$ to $y_k$ contains $1$ but not $2$, the other arc $2$ but not
$1$), so it changes the type of rows $1,2$ and leaves $X$.  The switch
is, in the original orientation, the unflip of rows $1,2$ at $(p,p')$
(which maps $X_A\to J_A$); it toggles the crossed/parallel status of
\emph{every} pair $(x,y)$ with $x$ on the side of row $1$ and $y$ on
the side of row $2$, and reverses the orientation of one side, so it
carries the ladder (a diagonal of the side-by-side array of pairs) to
a different family (an anti-diagonal).  The cross-switch likewise maps
$X_B$ to $J_B$ (it splits the $\sigma$-cycle through $p,p'$ while
keeping rows $1,2$ in one frame cycle, CGW Lemma~3.10) and toggles
the interior pairs.  Both are exact but not usable for the ladder bits
in $X$.

\paragraph{(b) Row trades are free in $X$; column turns are
conditionally free.}  For rows $z,w\notin\{1,2\}$ every trade of rows
$z,w$ along a $\rho_{z,w}$-cycle or $\rho_{z,w}$-arc changes rows
$z,w$ only, hence is legal in $X$.  In particular:

\begin{lemma}[exact fairness of column-pair status for generic rows]
\label{lem:rowfair}
Let $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ and let $(q,c)$ be a column pair lying in two
different cycles of $\rho_{x,y}$.  The CGW switch at $(q,c)$ for the
rows $x,y$ (trade of rows $x,y$ along the one of the two cycles that
contains the smaller minimum symbol) is a legal involution of $X$
that toggles whether $x,y$ lie in one column cycle of $(q,c)$, and
preserves the cycle partition of $\rho_{x,y}$ and the separation
status of every column pair with respect to $\{p,p'\}$.  Hence,
conditionally on everything determined by the other rows and by the
cycle partition of $\rho_{x,y}$,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[x,y\text{ in different column cycles of }(q,c)\bigr]=\tfrac12 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:switchtoggle} with $(1,2)$ replaced by $(x,y)$:
the trade swaps the entries of rows $x,y$ on the columns of the chosen
cycle $Q\ni q$, $Q\not\ni c$, so $\delta_{q,c}\mapsto\delta_{q,c}\circ(x\,y)$,
which splits or joins the column cycles through $x,y$.  It inverts $Q$
in place, so the symbol sets of the two cycles are unchanged and the
min-symbol rule re-selects $Q$: an involution.  Rows $1,2$ are
untouched, so type and mark are preserved.  Inverting $Q$ exchanges the
two arcs between $p$ and $p'$ when $Q\ni p,p'$, so separation of any
pair by $\{p,p'\}$ is preserved.  (If $Q$ contains $p$ or $p'$ the
frame changes at rows $x,y$; so frame-determined data are part of the
conditioning only when $Q$ avoids $p,p'$.)
\end{proof}

A column turn, by contrast, changes the rows of a column cycle.  Let
$(q,c)$ be a free column pair and $Z$ one of its column cycles.

\begin{lemma}[legal column turns]\label{lem:legalturn}
The turn of $Z$ (swapping the entries of columns $q,c$ in the rows of
$Z$) is a measure-preserving involution of $X$ if and only if
$|Z\cap\{1,2\}|\in\{0,2\}$; when $|Z\cap\{1,2\}|=2$ it conjugates
$\sigma$ by $(q\,c)$ and keeps the type and the mark.  It preserves the
columns $p,p'$ (hence the frame and every frame-determined family of
row pairs) and the column-cycle partition of $(q,c)$ and of every
column pair disjoint from $\{q,c\}$ (in particular of every pair of
a matching containing $(q,c)$).
For a row pair $(x,y)$ with $|Z\cap\{x,y\}|=1$ it replaces
$\rho_{x,y}$ by $\rho_{x,y}\circ(q\,c)$ (if $x\in Z$) or
$(q\,c)\circ\rho_{x,y}$ (if $y\in Z$), which toggles the
crossed/parallel status of $(x,y)$ exactly when $(q,c)$ is separated
by $\{p,p'\}$ in $\rho_{x,y}$ (different arcs when crossed, different
cycles $Q_p,Q_{p'}$ when parallel), and leaves $(q,c)$ separated
afterwards; with $|Z\cap\{x,y\}|\in\{0,2\}$ the status is unchanged.
Turns of cycles of column pairs from a fixed matching $\mathcal M$ of
the free columns pairwise commute.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A column cycle is a Latin trade (Lemma~\ref{lem:cycletrade}), so the
turn is a bijection of Latin squares and an involution, and it
preserves the cycle partition of $(q,c)$; column pairs disjoint from
$\{q,c\}$ are untouched, and so are columns $p,p'$.  Rows $1,2$ change
iff $Z$ meets them; if $Z\ni1,2$ both rows swap their entries at
$q,c$, so $\sigma'=(q\,c)\sigma(q\,c)$, whose type is that of $\sigma$
and in which $p,p'$ (fixed by $(q\,c)$) keep their distance.  If $Z$
meets exactly one of rows $1,2$, $\sigma'=\sigma\circ(q\,c)$ or
$(q\,c)\circ\sigma$, which changes the type.  The effect on
$\rho_{x,y}$ is the computation of \S\ref{sec:crossspace}; separation
is preserved by the arc computation there.  Turns of cycles of
disjoint column pairs act on disjoint sets of cells; two cycles of the
same pair have disjoint row sets.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{(c) The product bound.}  Fix a matching $\mathcal M$ of the
free columns, independent of the square, and a frame-determined family
$\mathcal G$ of row pairs $(x,y)$, $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$, whose
$x$-rows are pairwise distinct and disjoint from its $y$-rows (a block
of the ladder, where the pairs are even disjoint; or a union of
diagonals $D_c$ with distinct $x$-rows, which may share $y$-rows).
For $(x,y)\in\mathcal G$ call a column pair $(q,c)\in\mathcal M$ a
\emph{clean coordinate} of $(x,y)$ if the column cycle $Z$ of $(q,c)$
through $x$ has length at most $\ell_1$ (a fixed constant), satisfies
$|Z\cap\{1,2\}|\in\{0,2\}$, and contains no row of $\mathcal G$
other than $x$ (no other $x$-row and no $y$-row, in particular not
$y$).  Let $\mathcal T_{x,y}$ be the set of clean coordinates of
$(x,y)$ and $\eta_{x,y}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal T_{x,y}}$.  By
Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn} the turns of all clean coordinates of all
pairs of $\mathcal G$ commute, generate a group acting freely on $X$,
preserve $\mathcal G$ and the sets $\mathcal T_{x,y}$, and
\[
\rho_{x,y}(\eta)\;=\;\rho_{x,y}(0)\circ\!\!\prod_{(q,c)\in\mathcal T_{x,y}:\ \eta_{x,y}(q,c)=1}\!\!(q\,c),
\]
the bit $b_{x,y}(\eta)=[\,(x,y)\text{ parallel}\,]$ depending on
$\eta_{x,y}$ only.  Let the \emph{orbit weight} of $(x,y)$ be
\[
\bar y_{x,y}\;=\;\E_{\eta_{x,y}}\Bigl[\frac{\#\{(q,c)\in\mathcal T_{x,y}:\ (q,c)\text{ separated by }\{p,p'\}\text{ in }\rho_{x,y}(\eta)\}}{|\mathcal T_{x,y}|}\Bigr]
\]
($0$ if $\mathcal T_{x,y}=\emptyset$), a function of the orbit, and
$Y_{\mathcal G}=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal G}\bar y_{x,y}$.

\begin{proposition}[product bound]\label{prop:orbit}
Conditionally on the orbit, the bits $b_{x,y}$, $(x,y)\in\mathcal G$,
are independent, and
\[
\Pr\bigl[(x,y)\text{ crossed}\bigm|\text{orbit}\bigr]\le1-\tfrac12\bar y_{x,y},
\qquad
\Pr\bigl[\text{no pair of }\mathcal G\text{ is flippable}\bigm|\text{orbit}\bigr]
\le\prod_{(x,y)\in\mathcal G}\bigl(1-\tfrac12\bar y_{x,y}\bigr)\le e^{-Y_{\mathcal G}/2}.
\]
Consequently $\Pr_X[\mathrm{Flip}_{\mathcal G}=\emptyset]\le\E_X[e^{-Y_{\mathcal G}/2}]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The action is free: distinct column pairs act on disjoint cells, and
two clean coordinates of different pairs at the same $(q,c)$ are the
cycles through two distinct $x$-rows, each avoiding the other, hence
different cycles; the sets $\mathcal T_{x,y}$ are orbit-invariant by
Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn} (row sets of the cycles, their meeting
$\{1,2\}$, and the frame-determined family are preserved).  The
uniform measure on $X$ restricted to an orbit is therefore uniform on
the orbit, which is the product of the cubes
$\{0,1\}^{\mathcal T_{x,y}}$.  A clean coordinate of $(x,y)$ contains
$x$ and not $y$, and a clean coordinate of another pair contains
neither $x$ nor $y$ (it avoids all $x$-rows but its own and all
$y$-rows), so $b_{x,y}$ depends on $\eta_{x,y}$ alone, whence the
independence.  Fix $(x,y)$ and
$Z=(q,c)\in\mathcal T_{x,y}$.  Whether $Z$ is separated in
$\rho_{x,y}(\eta)$ does not depend on $\eta_Z$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}: the turn of $Z$ leaves $Z$ separated), and
when it is, the two values of $\eta_Z$ give opposite bits.  Hence
$\Pr[b_{x,y}=0]\le1-\tfrac12\Pr_\eta[Z\text{ separated}]$ for every
$Z$, and averaging the right side over $Z\in\mathcal T_{x,y}$ gives
the first bound; the second is the product of the first over the
independent bits.
\end{proof}

\begin{hypothesis}[orbit genericity]\label{hyp:orbit}
With $\ell_0=n/\log^2n$: (i) for the sub-ladder $I$ of the first
$\min(K_0-1,\lceil n/8\rceil)$ ladder pairs,
$\Pr[K_0\ge\ell_0,\ Y_I<\kappa|I|]=o(1/\log n)$; (ii) for a
$B$-instance with $d_{12}=\ell\le n/4$ and $|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell$, with
$S=\{2\ell t:\ 0\le t\le(n/2-\ell)/(2\ell)\}$ (offsets at gaps
$2\ell$, so that the $x$-rows $\pi^{-(c+i)}(1)$, $c\in S$,
$1\le i<\ell$, are pairwise distinct, $|S|\ge n/(4\ell)$, and the
diagonals $D_c$, $c\in S$, involve at most $n/4+\ell$ rows), and
cleanliness taken with respect to all rows of
$\bigcup_{c\in S}D_c$: the event that fewer than half of the $D_c$,
$c\in S$, have $Y_{D_c}\ge\kappa(\ell-1)$ has probability
$o(1/\log n)$ (as a single event over all $\ell$).
\end{hypothesis}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:Lorbit}
Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit} implies \textup{(L)}, hence the weak CGW
conjecture.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Follow the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:Lwitness}.  Long ladders:
by Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit}, on $\{K_0\ge\ell_0\}$ and off the
exceptional event of (i),
$\Pr[\mathrm{Flip}=\emptyset\mid\text{orbit}]\le e^{-\kappa|I|/2}\le
e^{-\kappa\ell_0/4}=o(1/\log n)$.  Short arcs: fix $\ell$ and apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit} to the family
$\bigcup_{c\in S}D_c$: its $x$-rows $\pi^{-(c+i)}(1)$ lie in the
disjoint intervals of depths $[c+1,c+\ell-1]$, $c\in S$, so they are
pairwise distinct, and they lie on the frame arc from $2$ to $1$ while
the $y$-rows $\pi^{-i}(2)$ lie on the arc from $1$ to $2$, so the two
sets are disjoint.  Given the orbit the bits of all pairs are
independent, hence so are the diagonals' flippabilities, and off the
exceptional event at least $|S|/2$ of the diagonals are flippable with
conditional probability $\ge1-e^{-\kappa(\ell-1)/2}\ge4\theta_0$,
$\theta_0:=\tfrac14(1-e^{-\kappa/2})$.  The number of flippable
offsets in $S$ then has conditional mean $\ge2\theta_0|S|$, and by
Chernoff the fraction $\theta_S$ is $\ge\theta_0$ except with
conditional probability $\le e^{-\theta_0|S|/4}\le
e^{-\theta_0\log^2n/16}$ (as $|S|\ge n/(4\ell_0)=\log^2n/4$),
summable over $\ell<\ell_0$ to $o(1/n)$.  The identity behind \eqref{eq:offsetavg}
holds for the average over $c\in S$ as well as over all $c$, so
$\sum_{\ell<\ell_0}\Pr[B,d_{12}=\ell,|C_1|\ge n/2+\ell,\theta_S\ge\theta_0]
\le36\ell_0/((n-2)\theta_0)=O(1/\log^2n)$; the parts $|C_1|<n/2+\ell$
and $K_0<\ell_0$ on $A$ are as before.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What the hypothesis says.}  It replaces the witness
lemma (a lower-tail statement about rare short cycles, probability
$\ell'/n$ per pair) by a statement about a quantity that is
typically of constant size: $\bar y_{x,y}$ is the average, over the
cube of a few column turns that fix rows $y$ and $1,2$ and the whole
family, of the fraction of those turns' column pairs that are
separated by $\{p,p'\}$ in the row pair $(x,y)$.  It is a
\emph{genericity} statement --- the short column cycles of the
matching pairs through $x$ are not aligned with the arcs of
$\rho_{x,y}$ at $p,p'$ --- together with concentration of the sum over
the family; concentration of the \emph{flippabilities} themselves is
no longer required, since Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit} makes them
conditionally independent.  It still contains (CP)-type inputs: a
positive orbit weight needs the cycles of $\rho_{x,y}$ through $p,p'$
to be macroscopic (the arc-trapping input (ii) of
\S\ref{sec:crossspace}), and the genericity itself.  The pure
permutation core is: for a permutation $\rho_0$ and a matching
$\mathcal T$ of transpositions on points other than $p,p'$, with
$\tau(\eta)$ the product of a uniformly random subset of
$\mathcal T$, bound $\Pr_\eta[p,p'\text{ separated in }\rho_0\tau(\eta)]$
from below.  For an adversarial $\mathcal T$ (pairs at
$\rho_0$-distance $2$) the probability is $0$, so the content is that
the clean coordinates of a random square are not adversarial; and the
moves that re-randomise the clean coordinates without touching
$\rho_{x,y}$ --- the free row trades of Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair} --- have
availabilities that are again same-cycle events of generic row pairs.
This is the recursion noted in \S\ref{sec:crossspace}: every
availability is a cycle-avoidance event, and the exact identities in
$X$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair}, (F), (I), Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit})
are all fair.  What Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit} adds is that the
fairness can be \emph{compounded} over a family: a product of
conditionally independent coins, so that a constant per-pair
genericity gives exponential decay in the family size.

\paragraph{(d) Data.}  \texttt{s13\_orbit.py}
(\texttt{runs/s13/orbit/orbit\{30,50,100\}.log}; $600$, $600$ and
$360$ ladder pairs at $n=30,50,100$, true marks, $\ell_1\le4,5,8$;

\paragraph{(e) The arc-trapping input $(\alpha)$: CGW's lemma for a
generic row pair in $X$, and the blocked fraction.}  The orbit weight
$\bar y_{x,y}$ vanishes when the $\rho_{x,y}$-cycles through $p,p'$
are short, so Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:orbit} needs
$\Pr_X[|Q_p(\rho_{x,y})|\le\varepsilon n]\le C\varepsilon$ for the
ladder pairs --- CGW's short-cycle bound for the row pair $(x,y)$
inside $X$.  Run CGW's splitting/joining multigraph for the
distinguished rows $x,y\notin\{1,2\}$ inside $X$.  Switches and
cross-switches of rows $x,y$ are free (they change rows $x,y$ only),
so the repairs of their splitting and joining procedures are always
available; the flip and backflip at a column pair $(q,c)$ turn the
$(q,c)$-column cycle through $x$ or through $y$ and are legal iff
that cycle meets $\{1,2\}$ in $0$ or $2$ rows
(Lemma~\ref{lem:legalturn}).  Since either cycle may be used, the
flip at $(q,c)$ is \emph{blocked} exactly when rows $1,2$ and rows
$x,y$ are \emph{interleaved} in the column-cycle structure of $(q,c)$:
in the $A$-state ($x,y$ in different cycles $Z_x,Z_y$) one of $1,2$
lies in $Z_x$ and the other in $Z_y$; in the $B$-state the four rows
alternate $x,1,y,2$ on the common cycle.  Interleaving is invariant
under the switch of rows $x,y$ (which maps $A$-crosswise to $B$-cross
and back, Lemma~\ref{lem:rowfair}) and under the switch of rows $1,2$
at a column pair in different $\sigma$-cycles (same computation with
the roles exchanged; this move preserves $X$ up to $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$).
Legality is a property of the bitrade, so CGW's edge correspondence
survives the removal of the blocked edges, and the $\mu$-side flip
edges alone give, for the cycle through the mark column
($N_\lambda(p)=\ell$ splitting pairs leave $p$ in the $\ell$-piece):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Jp}
\E_X\bigl[\#\{(q,c):\ q\in Q_p,\ c\notin Q_p,\ \text{not interleaved}\};\ |Q_p|=\ell\bigr]
\;\le\;2\ell\,\Pr_X\bigl[|Q_p|\ge\ell+2\bigr]\;\le\;2\ell .
\end{equation}
Hence, with $b_\ell$ the conditional expectation given $|Q_p|=\ell$ of
the fraction of interleaved pairs among the $\ell(n-\ell)$ joining
pairs,
\[
\Pr_X\bigl[|Q_p(\rho_{x,y})|=\ell\bigr]\;\le\;\frac{2}{(1-b_\ell)(n-\ell)} ,
\]
i.e.\ CGW's bound $\le 4/n$ holds in $X$ up to the factor
$1/(1-b_\ell)$: $(\alpha)$ \emph{follows from} (BL): $b_\ell\le1-c$.
Data (\texttt{s13\_interleave.py}, \texttt{runs/s13/orbit/interleave50.log},
$300$ ladder pairs at $n=50$, $1.8\cdot10^5$ column pairs):
$\Pr[\text{interleaved}]=0.186$ over all pairs in different
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycles, $0.1867$ in the $A$-state and $0.1861$ in the
$B$-state (the switch invariance), $0.188$ on joining pairs of $Q_p$
and $0.183$ off them, $0.193$/$0.181$ according as $q,c$ lie in
different/the same $\sigma$-cycles; and the lengths $|Q_p|$ are
spread uniformly ($3,7,5,7,5,9,5,5,3$ instances at
$\ell=2,\dots,10$ against $6$ expected per length).  So the blocked
fraction is $\approx0.19$ and the loss is a factor $1.23$.

What (BL) needs is again a same-cycle statement for generic row pairs:
the interleaving at $(q,c)$ is changed only by trades of third rows
$z,w\notin\{1,2,x,y\}$ along $\rho_{z,w}$-cycles through exactly one
of $q,c$ ($\delta_{q,c}\mapsto\delta_{q,c}\circ(z\,w)$), which
de-interleave when $z,w$ lie on adjacent arcs between the special
rows (in the $B$-cross state) or separate $x$ from the row of
$\{1,2\}$ in its cycle (in the $A$-crosswise state); the
cross-switch of $z,w$ reverses a $\delta_{q,c}$-arc and is either
illegal (the arc contains exactly one of $1,2$), harmful (it contains
exactly one of $x,y$), or useless (it contains no special row).  A
double count of these involutions gives
$\Pr[\text{interleaved}]\le1/(1+\kappa)$ as soon as, for an
interleaved square, a $\kappa$-fraction of the row pairs $(z,w)$ in
the de-interleaving positions have $q,c$ in different
$\rho_{z,w}$-cycles --- the generic same-cycle event of
\S\ref{sec:crossspace} once more, now for the third rows at the
prescribed column pair.  This is the explicit form of the recursion:
each level's availability is the next level's genericity, and no
level is settled by an exact identity.  For the ladder pairs there is
the further point that $(x,y)$ is selected by the frame, so
$(\alpha)$ is needed conditionally on the frame, while \eqref{eq:Jp}
is stated for fixed rows $x,y$ with the frame free.  The same
blocked-edge multigraph can be run with columns $p,p'$ fixed if every
move avoids them: the flips at free column pairs do; the switch of
rows $x,y$ used to repair a $B$-pair $(q,c)$ trades along the
$\rho_{x,y}$-cycle selected by the min-symbol rule, which is
unavailable when that cycle is $Q_p$ or $Q_{p'}$ (one may then trade
along the other cycle, if it avoids $p,p'$, at the cost of the
involution property); and CGW's cross-switch repairs trade along
$\rho_{x,y}$-arcs that may pass through $p$ or $p'$.  This enlarges
the blocked set by a second cycle-avoidance event; we have not carried
the bookkeeping through.
